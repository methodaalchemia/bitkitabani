Sunflower

Helianthus annuus L.
Evidence: LimitedGenerally safe

When pre-Columbian sunflower remains were found at excavation sites in Mexico, a second centre of domestication was proposed. A genetic study published in PNAS in 2011 supports a single domestication of today’s cultivated sunflower, in eastern North America.

In short

What is Sunflower?

Sunflower (Helianthus annuus L.) is a plant of the Asteraceae (daisy family) family. Traditions that use it: North America, Anatolia and the Mediterranean. Part used: seed and seed oil; the press cake left after the oil is taken is animal feed.

What is Sunflower used for?

Traditional use: A synthesis published in PNAS in 2006 counts sunflower, together with marshelder (Iva annua), chenopod and squash, among the four plants domesticated independently in the eastern United States. Scientific evidence: limited. According to the 2017 review in Chemistry Central Journal, the seed contains 35–42% oil; 55–70% of the oil is linoleic and 20–25% oleic acid.

Is Sunflower safe?

Safety label: generally safe. It is widely consumed as food. The 2016 review in the journal Allergy writes that hypersensitivity reactions to edible seeds, sunflower seed among them, are often severe, while the publications are mostly case reports; for people with the allergy the only management is to avoid the seed. ⚠️ There is a separate risk for children: in the 2007 study in the Journal of Pediatric Surgery, 3 of the 53 seed bezoars found in children with faecal impaction were of sunflower seeds, and most of the children were treated under general anaesthesia. Small children should not be given seeds in the shell. The sources relied on here contain no warning about consumption in food amounts in pregnancy and breastfeeding. No drug interaction study could be found.

Traditional use

North America — a plant domesticated by indigenous farming

A synthesis published in PNAS in 2006 counts sunflower, together with marshelder (Iva annua), chenopod and squash, among the four plants domesticated independently in the eastern United States. The same paper confirms this region as one of the world’s independent centres of domestication. Today sunflower has spread across the whole world as an oil crop.

Folk medicine — heart and respiratory complaints

A review published in Chemistry Central Journal in 2017 reports that sunflower seed is used in folk medicine for conditions such as heart disease and bronchial, laryngeal and pulmonary complaints. The same review writes that the nutritional and medical value of the seed is recognised but under-researched. These uses are traditional; they have not been confirmed clinically.

Thrace and Marmara — for oil and as a snack

According to the Thrace Agricultural Research Institute of the Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry, 73% of the sunflower area of Türkiye is in the Thrace-Marmara region; the plant is grown both for oil and for snack seed. A paper published in the journal OCL in 2019 reports that in 2017 oilseed and confectionery sunflower together were sown on 779,622 hectares and yielded 1,964,385 tonnes of seed. According to the same paper, 932 sunflower accessions are conserved in the National Seed Gene Bank.

Farming — press cake and feed

According to the institute’s page, the cake left after the oil is extracted contains 44% protein. The same source writes that sunflower oil is rich in oleic and linoleic acid.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew POWO it is an annual; its native range runs from the south-western USA to Mexico and it grows mainly in the temperate zone. In Türkiye it is a field crop; 73% of the sown area is in the Thrace-Marmara region. For sowing, a soil temperature of at least 8–10 °C is wanted.
Harvest season
According to the Thrace Agricultural Research Institute it is sown in March in the Aegean and south-eastern Anatolia, in April in Marmara, central Anatolia and the Black Sea region, and in May in eastern Anatolia. It reaches harvest maturity 45–60 days after flowering.
Local names
Günebakan, gündöndü (Thrace and the Marmara Region; Thrace Agricultural Research Institute).

Preparation

Sunflower is a food; no official monograph sets an accepted dose. The seed is eaten roasted or raw as a snack, the kernel is added to bread and salads, and the oil pressed from the seed is used as cooking oil. According to the Thrace Agricultural Research Institute, sowing takes place between March and May depending on the region, and the plant reaches harvest maturity 45–60 days after flowering. Because seed swallowed with its shell can cause bowel blockage in children, the shell is not eaten.

What science says

According to the 2017 review in Chemistry Central Journal, the seed contains 35–42% oil; 55–70% of the oil is linoleic and 20–25% oleic acid. The same review writes that the seed has 37.8 mg of vitamin E per 100 g, higher than the 10.1 mg in peanut, and that it carries phenolic acids such as chlorogenic acid as well as flavonoids. The antioxidant, antimicrobial, antidiabetic and anti-inflammatory effects cited in the review rest mostly on laboratory and animal studies; the authors note that the subject is under-researched. The human data concern the oil: a meta-analysis published in 2026, covering 13 randomised controlled trials (771 participants), found no significant difference between sunflower oil and canola oil in total cholesterol, LDL, HDL or triglycerides. None of these sources contains a clinical trial showing that sunflower seed treats a disease.

Safety

Generally safe

It is widely consumed as food. The 2016 review in the journal Allergy writes that hypersensitivity reactions to edible seeds, sunflower seed among them, are often severe, while the publications are mostly case reports; for people with the allergy the only management is to avoid the seed. ⚠️ There is a separate risk for children: in the 2007 study in the Journal of Pediatric Surgery, 3 of the 53 seed bezoars found in children with faecal impaction were of sunflower seeds, and most of the children were treated under general anaesthesia. Small children should not be given seeds in the shell. The sources relied on here contain no warning about consumption in food amounts in pregnancy and breastfeeding. No drug interaction study could be found.
