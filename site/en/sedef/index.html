Rue

Ruta graveolens
Evidence: TraditionalHigh risk

The Spanish brought rue to the Americas, and Indigenous healers folded it into their own cleansing rites. Today the same plant does the same job on two continents: protecting against the evil eye.

Other names
Ruda (Spanish), sedef otu (Turkish)
Part used
Leaves, flowering stems
Region
Anatolia and the Mediterranean
Andes
Use
Incense and protection, Healing, Poisonous
In short

What is Rue?

Rue (Ruta graveolens) is a plant of the Rutaceae family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Andes. Part used: leaves, flowering stems.

What is Rue used for?

Traditional use: Grown at home or carried as a sprig against the evil eye. Scientific evidence: traditional. It contains furanocoumarins and rutin.

Is Rue safe?

Safety label: high risk. Touching the fresh plant and then going into the sun can cause severe skin burns (phytophotodermatitis). It can cause miscarriage and is toxic to the liver and kidneys in high doses.

Traditional use

Anatolia

Grown at home or carried as a sprig against the evil eye. In folk medicine it is known for bringing on menstruation and expelling worms.

Andes

In Peruvian and Bolivian markets, ruda is the core plant of limpia (cleansing) rites; it is rubbed on the body against the evil eye (mal de ojo) and “bad air” (mal aire). In northern Argentina and Bolivia, “caña con ruda” is drunk on 1 August, at the start of Pachamama’s month, to ward off winter illness.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to the Mediterranean. In Anatolia it is mostly grown in gardens and pots; its close relative Ruta chalepensis grows wild on Mediterranean coasts. In the Andes it is grown in market gardens.
Harvest season
Leaves are picked before flowering, in late spring and early summer.
Local names
sedef otu, sedefotu, ruta; Spanish ruda

Mix-up risk

Recognised by its blue-grey leaves and sharp smell. Picking it bare-handed and then going into the sun risks severe skin burns.

Preparation

A fresh sprig is rubbed on the body or carried in a pocket. Drinking it as tea is not recommended.

What science says

It contains furanocoumarins and rutin. Furanocoumarins make the skin highly sensitive to sunlight. Rutin is a flavonoid studied for blood vessel health; there is no clinical evidence for the plant itself.

Safety

High risk

Touching the fresh plant and then going into the sun can cause severe skin burns (phytophotodermatitis). It can cause miscarriage and is toxic to the liver and kidneys in high doses.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Blood thinners (warfarin)Contains coumarin derivatives; may increase bleeding risk.
Medicines that cause sun sensitivity (some antibiotics, retinoids)Increases the risk of phytophotodermatitis.
