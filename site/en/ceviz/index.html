Walnut

Juglans regia
Evidence: ClinicalGenerally safe

Juglone released into the soil from walnut roots and leaves stops other plants growing; this is why tomatoes struggle under a walnut tree.

In short

What is Walnut?

Walnut (Juglans regia) is a plant of the Juglandaceae family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Asia and Siberia. Part used: kernel, leaf, green husk.

What is Walnut used for?

Traditional use: Green husks and leaves give a brown dye for wool and carpets and are rubbed into hair with henna to darken it. Scientific evidence: clinical. The nut itself is well studied: a meta-analysis of controlled trials (2018) showed that eating walnuts regularly lowers total and LDL cholesterol.

Is Walnut safe?

Safety label: generally safe. A serious allergy risk for people allergic to walnuts. Juglone in the green husk stains skin brown and can irritate it. There are no safety data for drinking leaf tea in large amounts over long periods; not recommended in pregnancy.

Traditional use

Anatolia

Green husks and leaves give a brown dye for wool and carpets and are rubbed into hair with henna to darken it. Green walnuts are made into jam. Leaf tea is drunk in folk medicine for blood sugar and eczema.

Iran and Central Asia

Walnut leaf is one of the leading anti-diabetic herbs of Iranian folk medicine. In the Caucasus, walnut sauces and green walnut jam are kitchen staples.

In nature

Where it grows
A garden and village tree all over Anatolia; wild forms grow in mountain forests from the Caucasus to Central Asia.
Harvest season
Green walnuts for jam in June, before the shell hardens; leaves June–July; nuts September–October.
Local names
ceviz, koz, goz

Mix-up risk

Black walnut (Juglans nigra) is a different species with a thicker shell and a stronger-tasting kernel.

Preparation

A dessertspoon of dried leaf is steeped in a cup of water for 10 minutes, or boiled for an external compress. The kernel is eaten raw or roasted.

What science says

The nut itself is well studied: a meta-analysis of controlled trials (2018) showed that eating walnuts regularly lowers total and LDL cholesterol. Evidence for the leaf is weaker; the EMA recognises walnut leaf only for external traditional use in minor skin inflammation and excessive sweating. A few small studies have looked at leaf extract and blood sugar in type 2 diabetes.

Safety

Generally safe

A serious allergy risk for people allergic to walnuts. Juglone in the green husk stains skin brown and can irritate it. There are no safety data for drinking leaf tea in large amounts over long periods; not recommended in pregnancy.
