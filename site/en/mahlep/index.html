Mahaleb cherry

Prunus mahaleb
Evidence: TraditionalGenerally safe

Mahaleb is used in Europe as rootstock for cherry trees; because it tolerates dry, chalky soil, many cherry orchards actually stand on mahaleb roots.

Other names
Mahlep, mahleb, St Lucie cherry
Part used
Kernel (seed)
Region
Anatolia and the Mediterranean
Use
Food, Healing
In short

What is Mahaleb cherry?

Mahaleb cherry (Prunus mahaleb) is a plant of the Rosaceae family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean. Part used: kernel (seed).

What is Mahaleb cherry used for?

Traditional use: The seed inside the stone of its small fruit is crushed into Easter bread, simit, poğaça and kandil simidi; it is the signature scent of Greek and Armenian festive breads. Scientific evidence: traditional. The seed is rich in coumarin, which gives its scent.

Is Mahaleb cherry safe?

Safety label: generally safe. Safe as a spice. Because of its coumarin, it should not be eaten in large amounts for long periods; people on blood thinners should be careful. Cross-allergy has been reported in people allergic to almonds and other nuts.

Traditional use

Anatolia

The seed inside the stone of its small fruit is crushed into Easter bread, simit, poğaça and kandil simidi; it is the signature scent of Greek and Armenian festive breads.

Middle East and Mediterranean

Arab countries add it to festival biscuits (maamoul) and Greece to the Easter bread tsoureki. Iranian folk medicine uses the seed against kidney stones.

In nature

Where it grows
Rocky, limestone slopes of Anatolia, as a shrub or small tree among oak and juniper.
Harvest season
Flowers April–May; fruit is picked June–July when black and the stones dried.
Local names
mahlep, idris

Mix-up risk

Its fruit looks like small cherries but is bitter. Stones of other wild Prunus species can release cyanide; do not collect kernels in the wild.

Preparation

The seed is pounded or ground just before use; used sparingly so it does not turn bitter.

What science says

The seed is rich in coumarin, which gives its scent. Animal studies show effects against kidney stone formation and gut inflammation. There are no clinical studies in people.

Safety

Generally safe

Safe as a spice. Because of its coumarin, it should not be eaten in large amounts for long periods; people on blood thinners should be careful. Cross-allergy has been reported in people allergic to almonds and other nuts.
