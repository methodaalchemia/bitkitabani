Soya bean

Glycine max (L.) Merr.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

Soya stands on the official list not as soya but as two of its fractions: lecithin and refined oil. Both are industrial products — lecithin is used as an emulsifier in thousands of foods, and refined soya oil is one of the most-produced vegetable oils in the world. The uses the monographs approve are correspondingly modest: a sensation of fatigue, and a bath additive for dry skin. Everything contested about soya — isoflavones, menopause, hormones, cholesterol — falls outside the monographs. It shows that a plant’s most-discussed property may never have entered official assessment at all.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the name "soya fasulyesi"; Soja hispida and Glycine soja Sieb. & Zucc. are synonyms. In English: soybean, soy, soja. The parts on the official list are the seed, the fixed oil obtained from it and its lecithin — that is, not soya itself but its fractions.

In short

What is Soya bean?

Soya bean (Glycine max (L.) Merr.) is a plant of the Fabaceae (legume family) family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia. Part used: the seeds, the fixed oil obtained from them, and their lecithin.

What is Soya bean used for?

Traditional use: The European Medicines Agency has published two monographs for soya, both under "traditional use". Scientific evidence: limited. The claim soya is famous for is menopausal symptoms, and here there is evidence — but not quite where expected.

Is Soya bean safe?

Safety label: caution. Allergic reactions including severe anaphylaxis and angioedema have been reported — the EU monograph is that plain, and soya is one of the world’s commonest food allergens. Not for use by anyone sensitive to soya, peanut, other legumes or birch pollen; itching, dermatitis, urticaria and gastrointestinal complaints have also been reported. Not for men with prostate cancer or women with oestrogen-receptor-positive tumours unless a doctor recommends it (Turkish monograph). Soya consumption may cause hypothyroidism, and the monograph separately states it may reduce the absorption of levothyroxine — for anyone on thyroid medication those two lines matter together. The oil and lecithin may cause stomach discomfort and diarrhoea; in case of vomiting the patient must not be made to vomit further, since oil can be aspirated into the lungs. Eye contact with the oil may cause keratitis; on contact the eye should be rinsed immediately with cold water. Full baths are contraindicated in open wounds, large skin injuries, acute skin disease, high fever, severe infections, severe circulatory disturbances and cardiac insufficiency. Not to be used under 12; not recommended in pregnancy or lactation.

Traditional use

Europe — two drugs, two indications

The European Medicines Agency has published two monographs for soya, both under "traditional use". Soya lecithin: relief of temporary fatigue and sensation of weakness — orally, a single dose of 750–2700 mg, 2–3 times daily. Refined soya oil: symptomatic relief of dry skin conditions associated with mild recurrent eczema — as a bath additive. Menopause and cholesterol appear in neither.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph lists the same two drugs and adds two serious limits absent from the EU monographs: it must not be used by men with prostate cancer or by women with oestrogen-receptor-positive tumours unless a doctor recommends it, and soya consumption may cause hypothyroidism. The monograph also notes that in pre-menopausal women a soya-rich diet is associated with low blood oestrogen levels and longer menstrual cycles. Use under 12 is prohibited (the EU monograph says "not established"; the Turkish one is firmer).

East Asia

Soya originates in East Asia and has been eaten for thousands of years; its fermented forms (miso, tempeh, soy sauce) are foundations of whole cuisines. Today it is the world’s most widely planted legume and is everywhere in the industrial food chain — and lecithin is precisely a product of that industry, present as an emulsifier in thousands of products.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to East Asia; today the world’s most widely planted legume, above all in the Americas, Brazil and Argentina. In Turkey it is grown as a second crop in the Black Sea and Mediterranean regions.
Harvest season
A summer crop; the seeds are harvested in autumn.
Local names
Soya fasulyesi — the Turkish monograph gives one Turkish name.

Mix-up risk

The confusion here is not at species level but at the level of part and fraction: the seed itself (food), an isoflavone extract (supplement), lecithin (a monograph drug) and refined oil (a monograph drug) all come from one plant but differ in composition and in evidence. ⚠️ Refined oil contains practically no isoflavones; isoflavone studies are not evidence for the oil or the lecithin.

Preparation

Lecithin: for adolescents a single dose of 750 mg twice daily; for adults a single dose of 750–2700 mg, 2–3 times daily, orally. If symptoms do not resolve within two weeks, consult a doctor. Refined soya oil as a bath additive (liquid preparations containing 70–90% oil, 2–3 times a week): full bath (100–150 l) 15–45 ml; partial bath (25–50 l) 5–15 ml; children’s bath (50 l) 20 ml, (25 l) 2.5–10 ml; applied directly to the skin in the shower, 20 ml. Bath temperature must not exceed 36 °C; maximum 20 minutes for adults, a few minutes for infants and children.

What science says

The claim soya is famous for is menopausal symptoms, and here there is evidence — but not quite where expected. A systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2025 in PeerJ screened 2,099 articles and pooled 12: soy isoflavones showed a significant but small effect on menopausal symptoms overall (seven studies, 533 people, Hedges’ g = −0.25; 95% CI −0.42 to −0.08). Broken down, significant effects were found for headache, psychosocial symptoms, palpitation and depression; but no significant effect was found for hot flushes, excessive sweating, insomnia, vasomotor symptoms, fatigue or physical symptoms. So soya’s best-known claim — hot flushes — did not come out in this analysis, while less-known ones did. The authors ask that the results be read with caution given the small sample sizes. One more distinction is needed on top of that: isoflavones are a constituent of the seed, while the monographs’ drugs are lecithin and refined oil — and refined oil retains practically no protein or isoflavones. The menopause evidence therefore does not belong to the drugs the monographs approve. The evidence level is Limited.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
TamoxifenThe Turkish monograph states soya may reduce the effect of tamoxifen. That line should be read together with the monograph’s provision that women with oestrogen-receptor-positive tumours must not use it unless a doctor recommends it: the two are sides of the same concern. For anyone in breast-cancer treatment this is a matter to raise with the doctor, not a personal preference.
Levothyroxine and iron absorptionThe monograph states soya may reduce the absorption of both. For someone on levothyroxine the practical consequence is clear: do not take the medicine and a soya product at the same hour, and tell the doctor about the change. That the monograph also states soya consumption may cause hypothyroidism strengthens this warning.
Warfarin and clozapineThe Turkish monograph reports that soya may reduce the effect of both. Each is a narrow-margin medicine whose blood level or effect is monitored.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographsNeither European monograph lists anything in its interaction section. For the same plant the Turkish monograph reports five items; taking the more protective of the two as the guide is the right approach.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Soya bean.
