In short

What is Grapefruit?

Grapefruit (Citrus × paradisi Macfad.) is a plant of the Rutaceae family. Traditions that use it: North America, Anatolia and the Mediterranean. Part used: fruit and fruit juice.

What is Grapefruit used for?

Traditional use: According to a review published in HortTechnology in 2021, grapefruit arose in Barbados in the 17th century and is taken to be a chance hybrid of pummelo and sweet orange; chemical and molecular data support this. Scientific evidence: limited. The most solid scientific finding is not a therapeutic effect but a drug interaction.

Is Grapefruit safe?

Safety label: caution. The sources relied on here contain no warning about its consumption as food by healthy people who take no medicines. ⚠️ The real risk is for people on medication. According to the FDA, grapefruit juice raises the amount of many drugs in the blood; a higher statin level, for example, increases the risk of liver and muscle damage and, as a result, kidney failure. With some drugs (fexofenadine) the effect is the opposite: less of the drug reaches the blood and it works less well. The FDA says that people whose medicine interacts with grapefruit should not eat Seville oranges, pomelos or tangelos either. For older people, people taking many medicines, and pregnant or breastfeeding women who take medicines, the same rule applies: read the patient information leaflet and ask a doctor or pharmacist. There are no separate data for children.

Traditional use

Barbados — the "forbidden fruit"

According to a review published in HortTechnology in 2021, grapefruit arose in Barbados in the 17th century and is taken to be a chance hybrid of pummelo and sweet orange; chemical and molecular data support this. In 1750 Hughes mentioned a tree in Barbados bearing the "forbidden fruit", and in 1756 Browne described it as a "smaller shaddock".

Florida and Texas — becoming a commercial fruit

According to the same review, Count Odette Philippe brought seeds to Florida in 1823, raised trees and distributed seed. By 1909 the fruit was known in commerce as "grapefruit". The red-fleshed varieties arose in Texas: Ruby Red was found in 1929 and patented in 1934, and Star Ruby was released in 1970.

The world and Türkiye — production

The review gives world production in the 2019–20 season as 6.7 million tonnes. The largest producer is China, with 4.9 million tonnes including pummelo; it is followed by South Africa (387,000 tonnes), Mexico (350,000 tonnes) and Türkiye with 270,000 tonnes. Türkiye is also named among the countries where the Star Ruby and Rio Red varieties are grown.

Pharmacy — the textbook example of a drug interaction

According to a 1998 paper in the British Journal of Clinical Pharmacology, the effect was found by chance: in a study of the interaction between felodipine and alcohol, grapefruit juice was used to mask the taste of the alcohol, and the level of the drug in the blood was seen to rise unexpectedly. The HortTechnology review writes that the first report was made in 1989.

In nature

Where it grows
According to the review it is a hybrid of Barbadian origin and is grown today in China, the USA, South Africa, Mexico, Türkiye and Israel. It is sensitive to frost: the review reports that in February 2021 the temperature in some Texas groves fell to −7.2 °C for hours and that freezes have reduced the bearing area.
Harvest season
The sources relied on here give no calendar of harvest months. The HortTechnology review writes that cold weather enhances the red colour of the peel.
Local names
The sources relied on here give no Turkish local name.

Mix-up risk

Pummelo (pomelo, Citrus maxima) is one of the ancestors of grapefruit and is a different species; the name "pomelo" was for a time also used for grapefruit. Bitter orange (Citrus × aurantium) is a separate record on this site. According to the FDA, Seville oranges, pomelos and tangelos may interact with medicines in a similar way to grapefruit.

Preparation

Grapefruit is a food; there is no accepted medicinal dose. The fruit is eaten fresh or its juice is pressed and drunk. For people taking medicines no general "safe amount" can be given: the FDA advises asking a doctor or pharmacist whether grapefruit juice interacts with the medicine and, if so, how much is safe. According to the FDA, the labels of fruit juice blends and fruit-flavoured drinks should also be read for grapefruit juice.

What science says

The most solid scientific finding is not a therapeutic effect but a drug interaction. Grapefruit juice suppresses the enzyme CYP3A4 in the wall of the small intestine; the amount of drugs broken down by this enzyme that reaches the blood increases. According to the 1998 reviews the effect can last 24 hours, varies greatly between individuals, and for felodipine, nitrendipine, nisoldipine and saquinavir the median increase in measures of blood level exceeded 70%. The HortTechnology review names the furanocoumarins (bergamottin and dihydroxybergamottin) as responsible for the effect. On the side of health claims the evidence is weak: a 2017 systematic review and meta-analysis examined 3 randomised trials with 250 overweight and obese participants, found no significant difference in body weight (mean difference −0.45 kg) and detected a small fall of 2.43 mmHg in systolic blood pressure. The authors note that the small number of trials and their short duration make it hard to draw conclusions.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Statins (simvastatin, atorvastatin)On the FDA list. The level of the drug in the blood rises; the risk of liver and muscle damage, and of kidney failure as a result, increases.
Calcium channel blockers (nifedipine, felodipine, nisoldipine)The drug group in which the interaction was first shown. According to the 1998 review, the median increase in measures of blood level exceeded 70% for felodipine and nisoldipine; the effect can last 24 hours.
CiclosporinOn the FDA list; the blood level of this drug, used after organ transplantation, rises. Transplant patients should not consume grapefruit without asking their doctor.
Buspirone, budesonide, amiodaroneOther examples listed by the FDA: the anti-anxiety drug buspirone, budesonide used in Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, and the heart rhythm drug amiodarone.
FexofenadineAccording to the FDA the effect here is the reverse: grapefruit juice affects transporter proteins, less of the drug reaches the blood and it works less well. Orange and apple juice may have the same effect.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Grapefruit.
