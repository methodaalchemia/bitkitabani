BT-183 · Pedaliaceae (sesame family)

Devil’s claw

Harpagophytum procumbens DC.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

The name comes from the fruit: when ripe it is covered in stiff arms ending in hooks, and it spreads by catching in the feet or mouths of grazing animals. The part used, however, is underground — secondary tubers growing off the main root. The part that gives the plant its name and the part that is made into medicine are entirely separate.

Other names
The Turkish monograph lists the English names devil’s claw, grapple plant, wood spider and harpago. The name comes from the hooked arms of the fruit, which spreads by catching in the coats of animals.

In short

What is Devil’s claw?

Devil’s claw (Harpagophytum procumbens DC.) is a plant of the Pedaliaceae (sesame family) family. Traditions that use it: Africa. Part used: the root.

What is Devil’s claw used for?

Traditional use: The plant is native to the Kalahari and its surroundings; the part used is the secondary tubers below ground. Scientific evidence: limited. What is interesting here is the direction in which the regulator and the literature diverge.

Is Devil’s claw safe?

Safety label: caution. Not for anyone hypersensitive to the active substance, or with an active gastric or duodenal ulcer. Safety under 18 has not been established. Joint pain accompanied by swelling, redness or fever should be examined by a doctor before reaching for a herbal product — those can be signs of inflammatory joint disease. People with gallstones should consult a doctor first. Reported undesirable effects: gastrointestinal symptoms (diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain), central nervous system effects (headache, vertigo) and hypersensitivity reactions (rash, hives, facial oedema). Frequencies are unknown. Not used in pregnancy or lactation for lack of data. The Turkish monograph requires supervision by a doctor or pharmacist trained in herbal treatment.

Traditional use

South Africa — where it comes from

The plant is native to the Kalahari and its surroundings; the part used is the secondary tubers below ground. It reached Europe in the early twentieth century and settled among the herbal products used for joint pain. Note that most commercial harvesting is still from the wild.

Europe — two separate indications

The EMA monograph gives both indications under "traditional use": relief of minor articular pain, and relief of mild digestive complaints such as bloating and flatulence with temporary loss of appetite. In the monograph’s own words these rest "exclusively upon long-standing use" — the agency did not look for clinical evidence.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the roots". What stands out is its interaction section: where the EMA says "none reported", the Turkish monograph names six classes of medicine (below).

In nature

Where it grows
The Kalahari desert and its surroundings — Namibia, Botswana, South Africa. It grows on sandy, arid soils. It has no natural distribution in Türkiye; it is imported.
Harvest season
It sprouts in the rainy season; the tubers are lifted in the dry season, when nothing of the plant shows above ground.
Local names
Şeytanpençesi in Turkish, the name the regulatory monograph also uses.

Mix-up risk

It can be confused with Harpagophytum zeyheri of the same genus, whose harpagoside content differs, so the species matters in commercial products. In some places the Turkish name is applied to other plants as well; the Latin name on the label is what settles it.

Preparation

The monograph describes both a tea and extract forms. Tea: 4.5 g of the herbal substance in 500 ml of boiling water, infused for 8 hours and divided into three doses — that unusually long infusion time is what the monograph specifies. For extracts the daily dose ranges from 240 mg to 2.4 g depending on the preparation; which extract is used is stated on the package, and one is not interchangeable with another. Not used under 18.

What science says

What is interesting here is the direction in which the regulator and the literature diverge. In the earlier records the EMA was the more generous of the two; here it is the opposite. The EMA rests the indication on long use alone, while a 2004 systematic review (12 trials, BMC Complementary and Alternative Medicine) reports "strong evidence" for an aqueous extract at a daily dose equivalent to 50 mg harpagoside in acute exacerbations of chronic non-specific low back pain; moderate evidence for preparations at 60 and 100 mg harpagoside in osteoarthritis of the knee, hip and spine and in low back pain; and in one trial an aqueous extract was non-inferior to 12.5 mg of rofecoxib daily in the short term. Against that, the 2014 Cochrane review of low back pain rates the same plant "low quality evidence": two trials, 315 participants, short-term improvement in pain and reduced use of rescue medication. The gap comes from both method and from which preparation was studied — the harpagoside content and whether the extract is aqueous or ethanolic change the result. The evidence level here is Limited: the positive findings belong to a particular preparation at a particular dose, and the most rigorous review rates the quality low.

Safety

Caution

Not for anyone hypersensitive to the active substance, or with an active gastric or duodenal ulcer. Safety under 18 has not been established. Joint pain accompanied by swelling, redness or fever should be examined by a doctor before reaching for a herbal product — those can be signs of inflammatory joint disease. People with gallstones should consult a doctor first. Reported undesirable effects: gastrointestinal symptoms (diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain), central nervous system effects (headache, vertigo) and hypersensitivity reactions (rash, hives, facial oedema). Frequencies are unknown. Not used in pregnancy or lactation for lack of data. The Turkish monograph requires supervision by a doctor or pharmacist trained in herbal treatment.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
The Turkish monograph names six classes; the EMA names noneThis is the most practical information in the record. The interaction section of the European monograph says "none reported". The Turkish monograph states that devil’s claw may interact with warfarin, antihypertensives, statins, antiepileptic and antidiabetic medicines, and proton pump inhibitors. The two do not contradict each other: "none reported" is a statement about what has been studied, not evidence that no interaction exists. Anyone taking these medicines should tell their doctor or pharmacist.
AnticoagulantsWarfarin on the Turkish list matters especially: its effect depends on a narrow range of blood levels, and a small shift changes the risk of bleeding or clotting.
Acid-reducing medicinesProton pump inhibitors appearing on the list fits the plant’s effect on the stomach: the monograph already contraindicates active ulcer.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Devil’s claw.
