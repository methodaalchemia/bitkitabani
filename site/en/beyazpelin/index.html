White wormwood

Artemisia herba-alba Asso
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

Much of the silver-grey cover that runs for kilometres across the semi-desert steppes of North Africa is made up of this plant; those steppes are named directly after its Arabic name, "chih".

Other names
Arabic "chih"; English white wormwood, desert wormwood; French armoise blanche. It has no established Turkish name — Turkey does not appear in Kew’s distribution records. Neither the European Medicines Agency nor the Turkish agency has a monograph for this species. Both agencies’ wormwood monographs are for *Artemisia absinthium*, a different species, and do not apply to this record.

In short

What is White wormwood?

White wormwood (Artemisia herba-alba Asso) is a plant of the Asteraceae (daisy family) family. Traditions that use it: Africa. Part used: aerial parts (leaves and flowering shoots).

What is White wormwood used for?

Traditional use: A review published in the Journal of Ethnopharmacology in 2019 surveyed the plants used for diabetes in Algeria and reported Artemisia herba-alba among the most frequently named. Scientific evidence: traditional. In the sources relied on here there is no controlled trial in humans.

Is White wormwood safe?

Safety label: caution. Because there is no monograph for this species there is no official list of contraindications either; what follows comes from the sources. Its composition varies markedly with where it is collected and which part is used, so there is no measured answer to the question of how much is safe. People sensitive to the Asteraceae family should be careful. There are no data for pregnancy, lactation, children or drug interactions. This matters most for the traditional use in diabetes: taking a plant that affects blood sugar together with diabetes medicine is an unmeasured risk, and such use should be mentioned to a doctor. The thujone restrictions written for wormwood belong to *Artemisia absinthium* and cannot simply be carried over to this species — though for both, the essential oil should not be consumed as a food.

Traditional use

Morocco and Algeria — diabetes

A review published in the Journal of Ethnopharmacology in 2019 surveyed the plants used for diabetes in Algeria and reported Artemisia herba-alba among the most frequently named. A Moroccan review in Diseases in 2024 records the same use. Both reviews document the use; neither demonstrates efficacy.

North-eastern Morocco — frequency in folk medicine

A field study published in Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine in 2021 reports that this species appears often in the traditional knowledge recorded in north-eastern Morocco. Boiling the aerial parts, or preparing them as a tea, are the main forms described.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew’s records its natural range is Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Spain and the Canary Islands. It is a low, silver-haired shrub of semi-desert steppe, growing on dry and saline ground.
Harvest season
The sources relied on here give no collection calendar for Turkey; the plant does not grow there.
Local names
It has no established Turkish local name. In North Africa it is known as "chih".

Mix-up risk

It is the same genus as wormwood (*Artemisia absinthium*) but a different species. The EMA and Turkish monographs are for *A. absinthium* and do not apply here, and neither do the thujone restrictions. A species’ monograph does not belong to its relative.

Preparation

No official monograph sets an accepted dose. The reviews relied on here describe the aerial parts being boiled or infused and drunk, without giving a measure. The essential oil is a separate product and is not a food.

What science says

In the sources relied on here there is no controlled trial in humans. The blood-sugar effects the reviews report come from cell and animal studies. A study published in PLoS One in 2015 showed that the essential oils from different parts of the plant differ markedly in composition: oxygenated sesquiterpenes dominate in the leaves and aerial parts, monoterpenes in the flower heads, esters in the stem. This means that what is called "Artemisia herba-alba oil" is not one substance — it varies with the part used, the place and the season. The same study reports that the oils reduced division in tumour cell lines; that is an in vitro measurement and does not mean treatment.
