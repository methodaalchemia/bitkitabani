BT-326 · Myrtaceae (myrtle family)

Anise myrtle

Syzygium anisatum (Vickery) Craven & Biffin
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

The species has travelled through three genera: first *Backhousia anisata*, then *Anetholea anisata* in a genus erected for it alone, and today *Syzygium anisatum*. The 2022 *Backhousia* review still includes it and says plainly that it does so "for historical reasons" — a good example of how a plant looked up under an old name can sit in two places in the literature at once.

Other names
English anise myrtle, aniseed myrtle, ringwood. Its former names are *Backhousia anisata* and *Anetholea anisata*; the species was moved out of *Backhousia* and placed in *Syzygium*. It has no established Turkish name; "anason mersini" is a descriptive rendering of the English one. Neither the European Medicines Agency nor the Turkish agency has a monograph for this species.

In short

What is Anise myrtle?

Anise myrtle (Syzygium anisatum (Vickery) Craven & Biffin) is a plant of the Myrtaceae (myrtle family) family. Traditions that use it: Oceania. Part used: leaf (fresh or dried); the essential oil distilled from the leaf.

What is Anise myrtle used for?

Traditional use: In Australia the leaf is dried and ground as a spice and infused as a tea, and is sold alongside lemon myrtle. Scientific evidence: traditional. In the sources available to us there is no study in humans at all.

Is Anise myrtle safe?

Safety label: caution. Using the leaf as a spice and a tea is ordinary, and in the zebrafish screen it came out at a level similar to lemon myrtle (96-hour LC50 284 mg/L). Even so, there is no safety assessment in humans for this species, and the dominant component of the oil is anethole. Anethole itself has been assessed toxicologically: at its 51st meeting the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives (JECFA) re-evaluated trans-anethole (WHO Food Additives Series 42, 1999) and, taking hepatotoxicity in rats as the end-point, allocated an acceptable daily intake (ADI) of 0–2 mg/kg body weight, derived by applying a 200-fold safety factor to the no-observed-effect level of 300 mg/kg bw per day in a 90-day rat study. The Committee writes that trans-anethole is unlikely to be genotoxic in vivo and that a cytotoxic metabolite, anethole epoxide, is the likely cause of the hepatic effects. That assessment is of the compound, not of this species or its oil; there is no assessment in humans for this species, so taking the oil internally is not appropriate and the spice measure should not be exceeded. The monograph and restrictions written for clove (*Syzygium aromaticum*) do not belong to this species: the European Medicines Agency’s *Caryophylli floris aetheroleum* monograph covers clove only. The sources relied on here contain no separate assessment for pregnancy, breastfeeding, children or drug interactions; as there are no data, it is appropriate not to use concentrated extracts in these groups.

Traditional use

Spice and tea — in the Australian spice trade

In Australia the leaf is dried and ground as a spice and infused as a tea, and is sold alongside lemon myrtle. A study published in Foods in 2018 identifies the species’ major active compound as anethole; its anise-like scent comes from that.

Against food spoilage — in vitro

The same 2018 study tested the species’ solvent extracts against two bacteria and six weak-acid-resistant yeasts, reporting broad-spectrum antifungal activity compared with standard antifungal agents, with *Dekkera anomala* the most sensitive organism and *Escherichia coli* the least. Hexane extracts carry the most active compound, methanol and ethanol extracts the highest phenolic content. These are in vitro measurements.

Antioxidant measurements and a toxicology screen

A study published in Antioxidants in 2024 compared the antioxidant capacity and toxic effects of anise myrtle and lemon myrtle leaves in zebrafish larvae. The two species were found very similar in both; the 96-hour LC50 was 284 mg/L for anise myrtle and 270 mg/L for lemon myrtle. The authors present this as establishing safe exposure levels for products to be developed later; it is an animal model. The Ali study published in Molecules in 2024 screened the phenolic profile of anise myrtle, lemon myrtle and cinnamon myrtle (*Backhousia myrtifolia*) leaves by LC-ESI-QTOF-MS/MS, identifying 145 phenolic compounds and quantifying 27; of the three species anise myrtle had the highest total phenolic content (52.49 ± 3.55 mg GAE/g) and the highest antioxidant potential. The most abundant compounds were catechin, epicatechin, isovitexin, cinnamic acid and quercetin. This too is an in vitro measurement.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew’s records its natural range is a single region: New South Wales. It is a tree of the temperate rainforest gullies of north-eastern Australia, found wild over a narrow area, and is cultivated for its leaf.
Harvest season
The sources relied on here give no collection calendar for Turkey; the plant does not grow there naturally.
Local names
It has no established Turkish local name.

Mix-up risk

It is in the same genus as clove (*Syzygium aromaticum*) but a separate species; one species’ monograph and restrictions do not belong to its relative. Its name also invites confusion with anise (*Pimpinella anisum*) and star anise (*Illicium verum*): the three are in separate families, and their scents resemble one another because they carry the same compound (anethole), not because they are related. A dose or restriction written for one cannot be carried over to the others.

Preparation

No dose is accepted in an official monograph. The leaf is a food, used as a spice or a tea; the sources relied on here give no measure or upper limit for that use. The essential oil and the solvent extracts are not food; the sources relied on here contain no dose for their use in humans.

What science says

In the sources available to us there is no study in humans at all. All the efficacy data are in vitro (inhibition of yeasts and bacteria, antioxidant measurements, the 2024 phenolic profile and antioxidant measurement in Molecules); the only animal data are a toxicity screen in zebrafish larvae. A review of the leaf oils of the genus *Backhousia* published in Plants in 2022 includes this species "for historical reasons" — confirming that it now sits in a different genus. The oil has been measured to be anethole-dominant; that explains its scent but shows no health effect.
