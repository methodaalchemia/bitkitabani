Cardamom

Elettaria cardamomum
Evidence: LimitedGenerally safe

Cardamom ranks with saffron and vanilla among the world’s most expensive spices; the pods are picked one by one, by hand, before they ripen.

Other names
Green cardamom, elaichi, hel, kakule
Part used
Pods and seeds
Region
Asia and Siberia
Use
Food, Healing
In short

What is Cardamom?

Cardamom (Elettaria cardamomum) is a plant of the Zingiberaceae family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia. Part used: pods and seeds.

What is Cardamom used for?

Traditional use: A “warming” spice of Ayurveda that eases digestion; it goes into masala chai and sweets, and the seeds are chewed against bad breath. Scientific evidence: limited. Meta-analyses of controlled trials found that cardamom powder supplements may slightly lower some inflammation markers and blood pressure, with inconsistent effects on cholesterol and blood sugar.

Is Cardamom safe?

Safety label: generally safe. Safe as a spice. The long-term safety of high-dose supplements is not well known; in pregnancy, stay with food amounts. It may cause allergy in people allergic to the ginger family.

Traditional use

India and Sri Lanka

A “warming” spice of Ayurveda that eases digestion; it goes into masala chai and sweets, and the seeds are chewed against bad breath.

Arab world and Anatolia

Arabic coffee is brewed with cardamom, and in south-eastern Anatolia and Hatay coffee is also spiced with it. Ottoman cooks used it in sherbets and electuaries.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to the moist forests of the Western Ghats in southern India; today Guatemala is the largest grower.
Harvest season
Pods are picked green, by hand, after the rainy season.
Local names
kakule, hel

Mix-up risk

Black cardamom (Amomum subulatum) is a different plant, larger and smoky, used in savoury dishes.

Preparation

Pods are lightly crushed and added to tea, coffee or food; the seeds can be chewed on their own.

What science says

Meta-analyses of controlled trials found that cardamom powder supplements may slightly lower some inflammation markers and blood pressure, with inconsistent effects on cholesterol and blood sugar. Most studies are small and come from a single country. The belief that it aids digestion rests on traditional use.

Safety

Generally safe

Safe as a spice. The long-term safety of high-dose supplements is not well known; in pregnancy, stay with food amounts. It may cause allergy in people allergic to the ginger family.
