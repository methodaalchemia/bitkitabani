Witch hazel

Hamamelis virginiana
Evidence: TraditionalGenerally safe

It blooms as most trees drop their leaves: its yellow, ribbon-like flowers appear October–December. The “witch” in its name comes not from witches but from an Old English word for “pliant, bending”.

Other names
American witch hazel, Hamamelis
Part used
Leaf, bark, twigs (distilled water)
Region
North America
Use
Healing
In short

What is Witch hazel?

Witch hazel (Hamamelis virginiana) is a plant of the Hamamelidaceae family. Traditions that use it: North America. Part used: leaf, bark, twigs (distilled water).

What is Witch hazel used for?

Traditional use: Indigenous peoples boiled the bark and leaves for compresses on swellings, bruises and wounds. Scientific evidence: traditional. The EMA recognises witch hazel leaf, bark and distilled water as traditional remedies for minor skin inflammation, small wounds, dry skin, and the itching and burning of haemorrhoids.

Is Witch hazel safe?

Safety label: generally safe. External use is generally safe and rarely causes contact allergy. Most distilled water contains alcohol and can dry the skin. Oral use is not recommended; rich in tannins, it can cause nausea and constipation.

Traditional use

North America

Indigenous peoples boiled the bark and leaves for compresses on swellings, bruises and wounds. European settlers learned this and in the 19th century turned distilled witch hazel water into a commercial product.

Today

The distilled water is used in toners and acne products, and bark and leaf extracts in haemorrhoid creams.

In nature

Where it grows
Deciduous forests, woodland edges and stream banks of eastern North America.
Harvest season
Flowers October–December; leaves in summer, bark in spring.
Local names
cadı fındığı, hamamelis

Mix-up risk

Winter-flowering Asian Hamamelis species and hybrids are garden plants; the medicinal species is H. virginiana.

Preparation

At home, leaves or bark are boiled and cooled for a compress. Ready-made distilled water is dabbed on with cotton.

What science says

The EMA recognises witch hazel leaf, bark and distilled water as traditional remedies for minor skin inflammation, small wounds, dry skin, and the itching and burning of haemorrhoids. It works through the astringent action of its tannins. Clinical studies are few and small.

Safety

Generally safe

External use is generally safe and rarely causes contact allergy. Most distilled water contains alcohol and can dry the skin. Oral use is not recommended; rich in tannins, it can cause nausea and constipation.
