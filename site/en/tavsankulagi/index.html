BT-260 · Apiaceae (carrot family)

Bupleurum root

Bupleurum falcatum L.; B. chinense DC.
Evidence: TraditionalGenerally safe

Bupleurum is almost never used alone in Chinese medicine; its name travels with a formula’s: sho-saiko-to, a prescription Kampo drug for hepatitis in Japan. That formula’s fame and its stain both stuck to this plant: the interstitial pneumonia cases that appeared in Japan with concurrent interferon became one of the textbook examples of herbal-medicine safety. A plant’s safety record can be written, without its ever being tested alone, through the mixture it sits in.

Other names
Turkish tavşankulağı (hare’s ear) — the Turkish name of the genus Bupleurum; Chinese cháihú, Japanese saiko, English Chinese thorowax root. The WHO monograph defines the drug as "Radix Bupleuri" — the dried root. No Turkish or EU monograph. Turkey’s native Bupleurum species are outside the monograph.

In short

What is Bupleurum root?

Bupleurum root (Bupleurum falcatum L.; B. chinense DC.) is a plant of the Apiaceae (carrot family) family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia. Part used: the dried root.

What is Bupleurum root used for?

Traditional use: "Uses supported by clinical data": none. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no clinical trial of bupleurum on its own; human data belong almost entirely to multi-herb Kampo and Chinese formulas, above all sho-saiko-to.

Is Bupleurum root safe?

Safety label: generally safe. Interstitial pneumonia with sho-saiko-to. Cases of interstitial pneumonia and acute respiratory failure linked to this bupleurum-containing Kampo formula have been reported in Japan; according to the 2011 review the risk rises with co-administration of interferon, with long duration of use and in the elderly. That is a finding about the formula, not the root alone — but bupleurum is the herb that gives the formula its name, and this catalog does not skip it. WHO monograph: sedation at large doses, caution with driving and machinery; sedation may increase with alcohol and other CNS depressants; lassitude and drowsiness are frequent; not in pregnancy or lactation. Safety level Low, on account of the pneumonia cases.

Traditional use

WHO monograph (1999)

"Uses supported by clinical data": none. In pharmacopoeias and traditional systems: fever, pain and inflammation of influenza and the common cold; analgesic for chest and hypochondriac pain; amenorrhoea; extracts in chronic hepatitis, nephrotic syndrome and autoimmune diseases. 3–9 g daily. Causes sedation at large doses; caution driving and with machinery. Frequent adverse effects: lassitude, drowsiness; at large doses loss of appetite, flatulence and abdominal distension.

Turkey

Turkey has many native Bupleurum species and the name "tavşankulağı" is used for the genus; but the cháihú root of Chinese medicine belongs to two East Asian species. Not on the Turkish list; not found at herbalists.

China and Japan — a formula herb

Cháihú is almost never used alone but nearly always in a formula; the most famous is Japanese Kampo’s sho-saiko-to (xiao chai hu tang) — a prescription drug for chronic hepatitis in Japan. The history of that formula is also the source of the most important warning in the safety section.

In nature

Where it grows
Meadows and slopes of China, Korea, Japan and Siberia; cultivated in China. These two species do not grow in Turkey.
Harvest season
Small yellow umbels in late summer; the root is lifted in spring or autumn.
Local names
Tavşankulağı; Chinese cháihú, Japanese saiko.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ Turkey’s native Bupleurum species are outside the monograph. Some Bupleurum species sold as "cháihú" on the Chinese market (B. longiradiatum) are poisonous — species verification is expert work.

Preparation

The WHO monograph gives 3–9 g of dried root daily as a decoction. Not to be used in pregnancy or lactation for lack of data. Use on its own is rare even in Chinese medicine; it is used as a formula ingredient under expert supervision.

What science says

There is no clinical trial of bupleurum on its own; human data belong almost entirely to multi-herb Kampo and Chinese formulas, above all sho-saiko-to. A review published in 2011 in Hepatology Research gathers the clinical use of sho-saiko-to for chronic liver disease in Japan and at the same time marks the limit: the formula consists of seven herbs, so which effect belongs to bupleurum cannot be separated out. At laboratory level the anti-inflammatory and hepatoprotective effects of the saikosaponins are much studied, but a laboratory finding is not clinical evidence. Evidence level Traditional.

Safety

Generally safe

Interstitial pneumonia with sho-saiko-to. Cases of interstitial pneumonia and acute respiratory failure linked to this bupleurum-containing Kampo formula have been reported in Japan; according to the 2011 review the risk rises with co-administration of interferon, with long duration of use and in the elderly. That is a finding about the formula, not the root alone — but bupleurum is the herb that gives the formula its name, and this catalog does not skip it. WHO monograph: sedation at large doses, caution with driving and machinery; sedation may increase with alcohol and other CNS depressants; lassitude and drowsiness are frequent; not in pregnancy or lactation. Safety level Low, on account of the pneumonia cases.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Interferon (within the sho-saiko-to formula)In Japan concurrent use of sho-saiko-to and interferon is held contraindicated because it raises the risk of interstitial pneumonia. Anyone in hepatitis treatment must tell their doctor about any formula containing bupleurum.
Alcohol, sedatives and CNS depressantsThe WHO monograph states sedation may increase synergistically; not evaluated in a clinical study but patients should be cautioned.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Bupleurum root.
