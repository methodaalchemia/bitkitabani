In short

What is Yew?

Yew (Taxus baccata) is a plant of the Taxaceae (porsukgiller) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: no part should be used at home; the needles are a raw material for the drug industry.

What is Yew used for?

Traditional use: Ancient yews are often found in early Christian churchyards and near prehistoric burials and ritual sites. Scientific evidence: clinical. Every part except the red fleshy aril contains taxine alkaloids; the hard seed inside the aril is also toxic.

Is Yew safe?

Safety label: high risk. Highly poisonous. Lethal doses of 0.6–1.3 g of leaves per kilogram of body weight have been reported; a tea of 50–100 g of needles is considered fatal. There is no antidote. The fleshy part of the red berry is not toxic, but the seed inside is, so it is dangerous for children and animals. In Turkey call 114 or 112 if poisoning is suspected.

Traditional use

Britain and Ireland

Ancient yews are often found in early Christian churchyards and near prehistoric burials and ritual sites. The Fortingall Yew in Scotland is estimated at 2,000–5,000 years old and the Defynnog Yew in Wales at over 3,000; the churches are thought to stand on pre-Christian sanctuaries. In the Celtic, Greek and Roman worlds the yew was linked with death, rebirth and immortality.

Rome

Pliny calls the yew “sombre and ominous” and writes that in Gaul wine flasks made of its wood killed those who drank from them.

Bursa, Turkey

In Alpağut village, Mustafakemalpaşa, green yew shoots were fed to incurably sick animals to spare them suffering, a practice passed from father to son. Elders in Dallıca say the minaret of a wooden mosque was built of “gürdüç” wood.

Modern medicine

The cancer drug paclitaxel (Taxol) was found in the bark of the Pacific yew in the 1960s. When trees had to be felled to treat patients, production moved to semi-synthesis from a precursor in the needles of European yew, a renewable part of the tree.

In nature

Where it grows
Turkey’s only native yew: Thrace (Demirköy), the Black Sea region (Düzce, Bolu, Karabük, Zonguldak, Bartın, Rize), Kazdağı, the Sultan Mountains and the Amanos. It usually grows at 1,000–1,900 m; monumental trees around Zonguldak and Bartın are estimated at 1,900–2,000 years old (Yaman and Zaman 2022). The Bursa population is declining (Dikeş et al. 2021).
Harvest season
Evergreen; the red-arilled seeds of female trees ripen in autumn.
Local names
Gürdüç (Mustafakemalpaşa, Bursa)

Mix-up risk

The needles can be confused with fir or juniper branches; yew has no cones, and the seed sits in a red fleshy cup.

Preparation

Never used at home in any form. Paclitaxel and docetaxel are purified drugs given only in hospital under medical supervision.

What science says

Every part except the red fleshy aril contains taxine alkaloids; the hard seed inside the aril is also toxic. Taxines A and B block sodium and calcium channels in the heart and stop its conduction. Paclitaxel, by contrast, stabilises the microtubules used in cell division and so stops cancer cells from dividing.

Safety

High risk

Highly poisonous. Lethal doses of 0.6–1.3 g of leaves per kilogram of body weight have been reported; a tea of 50–100 g of needles is considered fatal. There is no antidote. The fleshy part of the red berry is not toxic, but the seed inside is, so it is dangerous for children and animals. In Turkey call 114 or 112 if poisoning is suspected.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Heart medicines (antiarrhythmics, beta blockers, calcium channel blockers)Taxines block the heart’s sodium and calcium channels; poisoning may be more severe in people on heart medicines.
