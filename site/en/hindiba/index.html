Chicory

Cichorium intybus L.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

In Turkish, "hindiba" and "karahindiba" differ by one syllable and name two different plants, both in this catalog: chicory is Cichorium intybus, dandelion Taraxacum officinale. The same family, but different genera and different monographs. Both have a root that is used, both are bitter, both carry a digestive indication — but their warnings are not the same. The similarity is reassuring, which is exactly why the Latin name on the label is the thing to check.

Other names
The TİTCK monograph gives the Turkish name as "beyaz hindiba" and shows no synonyms. In English chicory. ⚠️ Karahindiba (Taraxacum officinale) in our catalog is a different plant; the same family but a separate genus and a separate monograph.

In short

What is Chicory?

Chicory (Cichorium intybus L.) is a plant of the Asteraceae (daisy family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the root.

What is Chicory used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": relief of symptoms related to mild digestive disorders (a feeling of abdominal fullness, flatulence, slow digestion) and temporary loss of appetite. Scientific evidence: limited. Chicory presents an interesting case: there is pooled clinical evidence, but not for the monograph’s indication.

Is Chicory safe?

Safety label: caution. People with gallstones should not use it — the Turkish monograph states that it may stimulate the formation of gallstones. It may lower blood sugar, so it should be used with care and stopped two weeks before surgery — a pre-surgical warning is uncommon in this catalog, and the reason is direct: an unexpected fall in blood sugar during an operation is not wanted. Sensitivity can occur to the active constituents and to other species of the Asteraceae family — a large family, so people allergic to chamomile, chrysanthemum or sunflower should take care. Not recommended under 12. Not to be used in pregnancy or lactation for lack of data. If symptoms last more than two weeks, consult a doctor or pharmacist.

Traditional use

Europe — digestion and appetite

The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": relief of symptoms related to mild digestive disorders (a feeling of abdominal fullness, flatulence, slow digestion) and temporary loss of appetite. The classic use of the bitter-drug class.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the roots" and adds two warnings. First: it may lower blood sugar, so it should be used with care and stopped two weeks before surgery. Second: it may stimulate the formation of gallstones, so people with gallstones should not use it. Not recommended under 12.

Anatolia — in the kitchen

Chicory is gathered and eaten as a wild green in Turkey, common with its blue flowers along fields and roadsides. Its roasted root is also used as a coffee substitute — a use found worldwide and independent of the monograph’s indication.

In nature

Where it grows
Field margins, roadsides and meadows of Europe, North Africa and western Asia. Very common in Turkey; leafy cultivars (radicchio, Belgian endive) are grown as vegetables.
Harvest season
Sky-blue flowers from June to September, opening in the morning and closing in the afternoon. The root is lifted in autumn.
Local names
Hindiba, beyaz hindiba; the wild plant is also gathered under names such as "güneyik" and "çıtlık" in Turkey.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ It is confused with karahindiba (dandelion, Taraxacum officinale) in our catalog: the same family but a separate genus. Dandelion’s flower is yellow and single; chicory’s is blue and many on a branched stem. In addition, the leafy chicory varieties (radicchio, Belgian endive) are the same species but have been bred as vegetables; the subject of the monograph is the root.

Preparation

The monograph describes preparations of the root: herbal tea, infusion, decoction or macerate. As with bitter drugs, they are taken shortly before meals. If symptoms last more than two weeks, consult a doctor or pharmacist. Not recommended under 12.

What science says

Chicory presents an interesting case: there is pooled clinical evidence, but not for the monograph’s indication. A systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2023 in Clinical Nutrition ESPEN examined chicory supplementation in patients with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease: five randomised trials, 197 patients. The result: chicory significantly lowered two liver enzymes — AST by 7.07 U/L (95% CI −13.82 to −0.32) and ALT by 17.53 U/L (95% CI −32.64 to −2.42). By contrast, no significant effect was seen on ALP or GGT, or on any component of the lipid profile. The review’s own conclusion is measured: chicory may exert potential hepatoprotective effects in these patients, but studies with more patients and longer intervention are mandatory before widespread recommendation. Two distinctions need keeping. First: a fall in enzymes is a laboratory measurement and does not by itself show that the course of the disease improved. Second: the EU monograph’s indication is digestion and appetite, not the liver — so what was measured and what is approved are not the same thing. The monograph’s own indication rests on long use alone. The evidence level is Limited.

Safety

Caution

People with gallstones should not use it — the Turkish monograph states that it may stimulate the formation of gallstones. It may lower blood sugar, so it should be used with care and stopped two weeks before surgery — a pre-surgical warning is uncommon in this catalog, and the reason is direct: an unexpected fall in blood sugar during an operation is not wanted. Sensitivity can occur to the active constituents and to other species of the Asteraceae family — a large family, so people allergic to chamomile, chrysanthemum or sunflower should take care. Not recommended under 12. Not to be used in pregnancy or lactation for lack of data. If symptoms last more than two weeks, consult a doctor or pharmacist.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Diabetes medicinesThe Turkish monograph states it may interact with antidiabetic drugs and gives the reason: the plant may lower blood sugar. When two things work in the same direction, blood sugar can fall further than expected. Anyone on diabetes medication should tell their doctor.
Surgery (not an interaction but a matter of timing)Not a drug interaction, but it needs the same care: because of the effect on blood sugar, the Turkish monograph recommends stopping use two weeks before surgery. Tell your doctor if an operation is planned.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing; the Turkish one reports one drug group.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Chicory.
