BT-212 · Aquifoliaceae (holly family)

Yerba mate

Ilex paraguariensis A.St.-Hil.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

Mate’s link with cancer is often heard and often misstated, and IARC’s 2016 evaluation draws the distinction clearly: drinking very hot beverages was classified as "probably carcinogenic to humans" (Group 2A) and linked with cancer of the oesophagus, while drinking maté at temperatures that are not very hot was not classifiable (Group 3). IARC defines "very hot" as above 65 °C. What is held responsible is not the plant but the temperature — and the finding is not limited to mate: how hot tea and coffee are drunk in Turkey raises the same question.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the names "Paraguay çayı, mate"; Ilex paraguayensis is a synonym. Guayusa (Ilex guayusa), also in this catalog, is a relative in the same genus.

In short

What is Yerba mate?

Yerba mate (Ilex paraguariensis A.St.-Hil.) is a plant of the Aquifoliaceae (holly family) family. Traditions that use it: Andes. Part used: the leaf.

What is Yerba mate used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places both indications under "traditional use": (1) symptoms of fatigue and a sensation of weakness; (2) to increase the amount of urine and flush the urinary tract as an adjuvant in minor urinary complaints. Scientific evidence: limited. Of the three organisms in this batch, mate is the only one with pooled clinical evidence.

Is Yerba mate safe?

Safety label: caution. Not to be used in gastric or intestinal ulcer, in cardiac disorders such as hypertension and arrhythmia, or in an overactive thyroid — the classic contraindications of a caffeinated drug. The EU monograph adds one more: conditions where a reduced fluid intake is recommended, such as obstruction of the urinary tract. Taken in excess it causes insomnia, unease, tension and palpitations; the meta-analysis also reports mucosal irritation, tachycardia, angina and headache. Not recommended before bedtime. The temperature of the drink is a separate matter, and especially important here: in 2016 the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classified drinking very hot beverages as "probably carcinogenic to humans" (Group 2A), defining "very hot" as above 65 °C; in the same evaluation drinking maté at temperatures that are not very hot was not classifiable (Group 3). So the problem is not mate itself but the temperature — and the same applies to tea and coffee in Turkey. Not recommended under 18, nor in pregnancy or lactation. If fever, burning on urination, spasms or blood in the urine occur, see a doctor.

Traditional use

Europe — two indications

The EMA monograph places both indications under "traditional use": (1) symptoms of fatigue and a sensation of weakness; (2) to increase the amount of urine and flush the urinary tract as an adjuvant in minor urinary complaints. The first comes from the caffeine.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the leaves", and the contraindications fall where you would expect for a caffeinated drug: not to be used in gastric or intestinal ulcer, in cardiac disorders such as hypertension and arrhythmia, or in an overactive thyroid. Taken in excess it causes insomnia, unease, tension and palpitations.

South America

Mate is the everyday drink of Argentina, Paraguay, Uruguay and southern Brazil; the dried, ground leaf is brewed in a gourd and drunk through a metal straw (bombilla), the vessel passing from hand to hand. The drink is traditionally taken very hot — a detail that returns in the safety section.

In nature

Where it grows
The humid subtropical forests of Paraguay, northern Argentina, Uruguay and southern Brazil; now widely cultivated. It does not grow in Turkey and is imported as a product.
Harvest season
The leaves are harvested once a year, usually in late winter, then dried and ground.
Local names
Mate, Paraguay çayı — both appear in the Turkish monograph.

Mix-up risk

Guayusa (Ilex guayusa, in this catalog) and Ilex vomitoria of the same genus are used in similar ways and the products can be confused; the EU monograph covers I. paraguariensis only. Holly (Ilex aquifolium), also of the same genus, is an ornamental whose berries are poisonous and must not be confused with the species made into drinks.

Preparation

The monograph describes preparations of the leaf. Two practical notes: the EU monograph does not recommend taking it before bedtime, as it may disturb sleep; and for the second indication the increase in urine depends on adequate fluid intake. Not recommended under 18. For the temperature of the drink, see the safety section.

What science says

Of the three organisms in this batch, mate is the only one with pooled clinical evidence. A systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2025 in Frontiers in Endocrinology gathered 13 randomised controlled trials (dyslipidaemic, overweight and normal-weight volunteers). The findings split in two. On the positive side: in pre-diabetic patients, significant falls in postprandial glucose (mean difference −12.76), HbA1c (−0.37) and the HOMA index (−0.24) — though each comes from only two studies, and the review says they need confirming. On the negative side: no significant effect on triglycerides, total cholesterol, HDL, LDL, fasting glucose, fasting insulin, waist circumference or BMI. The review’s own conclusion is measured: mate consumption may show favourable effects on glycaemic control, while its impact on lipid profiles and weight management appears limited. The review also lists adverse events: mucosal irritation, insomnia, tachycardia, angina, headache and gastrointestinal discomfort. One note is needed: what was measured is blood sugar, whereas the EU monograph’s indications are fatigue and the urinary tract — so the evidence is not in the field of the approved use. The evidence level is Limited.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
MAO inhibitorsThe EU monograph states that people taking MAO-inhibitor drugs should use mate with caution. With stimulants such as caffeine, these medicines can produce unexpected effects on blood pressure and heart rate.
Sedative medicinesThe EU monograph states that caffeine-containing preparations reduce sedative action. Anyone taking a sleeping tablet or a sedative should take mate’s effect into account.
Sympathomimetic medicinesThe EU monograph states that caffeine-containing preparations increase the side effects of these drugs. The group runs from decongestant sprays to asthma medicines, and the effect shows in heart rate and blood pressure.
Other sources of caffeineNot a reported interaction but the commonest situation in practice: mate, tea, coffee and energy drinks carry the same substance and their effects add up. Anyone with palpitations or insomnia should review the daily total.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Yerba mate.
