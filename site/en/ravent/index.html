BT-182 · Polygonaceae (knotweed family)

Chinese rhubarb

Rheum palmatum L., Rheum officinale Baillon
Evidence: LimitedCaution

The rhubarb of pies and jam and the rhubarb here are the same genus but not the same species: what is eaten is the stalk of Rheum rhabarbarum, while the medicinal part is the root of R. palmatum and R. officinale. And even in the edible species the leaf blade is not eaten, because of its oxalic acid content — only the stalk is. Rhubarb is a good example of how much depends on which part of a plant is used, and for what.

Other names
The Turkish agency keeps separate monographs for the two species but gives both the Turkish name "ravent"; the EU monograph covers both and their hybrids, so this is one record. R. potaninii Losinsk. is a synonym of R. palmatum. Garden rhubarb, whose stalks are eaten, is a different species (Rheum rhabarbarum).

In short

What is Chinese rhubarb?

Chinese rhubarb (Rheum palmatum L.) is a plant of the Polygonaceae (knotweed family) family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia. Part used: the root and rhizome.

What is Chinese rhubarb used for?

Traditional use: Rhubarb root is among the oldest and most used drugs of Chinese medicine; it reached Europe along the Silk Road and long remained an expensive import. Scientific evidence: limited. The situation is the same as for alder buckthorn, and for the same reason: the EMA treats rhubarb root as well-established use, but the 2021 systematic review of over-the-counter therapies for chronic constipation (41 randomised trials) gives grade A evidence among anthranoid laxatives to senna alone, and does not assess rhubarb separately.

Is Chinese rhubarb safe?

Safety label: caution. Not to be used in intestinal obstruction and stenosis, atony, appendicitis, inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn’s, ulcerative colitis), irritable bowel syndrome, or abdominal pain of unknown origin. Contraindicated under 12 and in pregnancy, and also in lactation — in pregnancy because of experimental data on the genotoxic risk of several anthranoids such as emodin and aloe-emodin, and in lactation because active metabolites such as rhein have been shown to be excreted in breast milk in small amounts. Use beyond one week is not recommended. Prolonged use can disturb water and electrolyte balance, cause albuminuria and haematuria, lead to muscle weakness through potassium loss, and cause pigmentation of the colonic mucosa. The Turkish monograph additionally reports liver toxicity with prolonged high-dose oral use. Nausea, vomiting and cramping abdominal pain may occur; the urine may turn yellow or red-brown and distort biochemical results. Supervision by a doctor or pharmacist trained in herbal treatment is required.

Traditional use

China — a staple of the classical materia medica

Rhubarb root is among the oldest and most used drugs of Chinese medicine; it reached Europe along the Silk Road and long remained an expensive import.

Europe — short-term laxative

The EMA monograph places the indication under "well-established use" and limits it to one sentence: short-term use in occasional constipation. The active compounds are hydroxyanthracene derivatives, calculated as rhein.

European Union — medicine and food supplement treated differently

The European Food Safety Authority stated in 2018 that it could not establish a safe daily intake for hydroxyanthracene derivatives: there is in vitro evidence of genotoxicity for emodin, aloe-emodin and danthron, aloe-emodin was also found genotoxic in vivo, and epidemiological data suggest a link between laxative use and colorectal cancer. The panel noted data to the contrary for rhein. Regulation (EU) 2021/468 then prohibited aloe-emodin, emodin and danthron in food, and placed preparations from the root or rhizome of Rheum palmatum and Rheum officinale containing hydroxyanthracene derivatives under Union scrutiny. This is not a ban on the medicine: the monograph remains in force and approved for short-term use; what is restricted is taking it as a food supplement, without a time limit.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monographs give the part used as "roots and rhizomes" for both species. Their warning list carries one item the EMA monograph does not: liver toxicity with prolonged high-dose oral use.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to western China and the Tibetan plateau; grows in cool, moist, deep soils at altitude. It has no wild distribution in Turkey and is imported as a medicine or supplement.
Harvest season
The root is lifted in autumn as the plant goes dormant, then sliced and dried.
Local names
Ravent, ışgın. Note that "ışgın" is used in Anatolia for Rheum ribes, a different species whose stalk is eaten in eastern Turkey. That is not the plant in this record.

Mix-up risk

Most often confused with garden rhubarb (Rheum rhabarbarum) and with the Anatolian ışgın (Rheum ribes). All three are rhubarbs, but the parts used and the purposes differ; the laxative effect belongs to the root of R. palmatum and R. officinale alone. The Latin name on the label is what settles it.

Preparation

The monograph describes a preparation equivalent to 20–30 mg of hydroxyanthracene derivatives calculated as rhein, taken once daily at night by adolescents, adults and the elderly; the correct dose is the smallest that produces a comfortable soft-formed motion. A herbal tea form is included: comminuted herbal substance equivalent to no more than 30 mg of hydroxyanthracene derivatives in 150 ml of boiling water. Not to be used for more than one week — a shorter limit than for alder buckthorn — and two or three doses during that week are usually enough. Contraindicated under 12.

What science says

The situation is the same as for alder buckthorn, and for the same reason: the EMA treats rhubarb root as well-established use, but the 2021 systematic review of over-the-counter therapies for chronic constipation (41 randomised trials) gives grade A evidence among anthranoid laxatives to senna alone, and does not assess rhubarb separately. That anthranoids increase bowel motility is not in dispute, but this plant has no randomised evidence of its own. The evidence level is Limited. In this class the safety side has in fact been studied more than the efficacy side; the legal section below explains why.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Cardiac glycosidesThe potassium loss caused by prolonged laxative use potentiates the action of cardiac glycosides.
AntiarrhythmicsThe same potassium loss interacts with antiarrhythmic medicines; the Turkish monograph also lists QT-prolonging drugs.
Diuretics, corticosteroids, liquorice rootAll three cause potassium loss, which deepens when taken together with rhubarb.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Chinese rhubarb.
