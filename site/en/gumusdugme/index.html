Feverfew

Tanacetum parthenium (L.) Schultz Bip.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

Neither of the plant’s names describes its current use. The English feverfew comes from an old use as a febrifuge; the Turkish "gümüş düğme", silver button, comes from the look of the flower; the indication in the monograph is migraine prophylaxis. More interesting is where its known side effect comes from: the mouth-ulcer warning appears both in the Turkish monograph and in the Cochrane review — one in an agency text, the other in the adverse-event records of clinical trials. Each confirms the other.

Other names
The Turkish monograph lists four synonyms: Chrysanthemum parthenium, Leucanthemum parthenium, Matricaria parthenium and Pyrethrum parthenium. The English name means "fever dispeller", though its approved use today has nothing to do with fever.

In short

What is Feverfew?

Feverfew (Tanacetum parthenium (L.) Schultz Bip.) is a plant of the Asteraceae (daisy family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the aerial parts.

What is Feverfew used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph gives a single indication under "traditional use": prophylaxis of migraine headaches after serious conditions have been excluded by a medical doctor. Scientific evidence: limited. Feverfew is one of the most heavily marketed herbs for migraine, so it is worth stating exactly where the evidence stands.

Is Feverfew safe?

Safety label: caution. Not for anyone hypersensitive to the active substance or to other plants of the Asteraceae family — a large family, so people allergic to chamomile, chrysanthemum or sunflower should take care. The Turkish monograph notes that mouth ulcers and gastrointestinal complaints may occur during use; mouth ulcers are a known, characteristic effect of feverfew. Not used under 18 for lack of data. Animal studies have shown signs of reproductive toxicity, so it is not used in pregnancy or lactation. If symptoms do not resolve over two months, or if adverse effects appear, consult a doctor. The Turkish monograph requires supervision by a doctor or pharmacist trained in herbal treatment.

Traditional use

Europe — migraine prophylaxis

The EMA monograph gives a single indication under "traditional use": prophylaxis of migraine headaches after serious conditions have been excluded by a medical doctor. That condition is in the monograph’s own sentence and it matters: the plant is not for stopping a headache but for thinning out attacks in diagnosed migraine.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the aerial parts". Two points stand out: mouth ulcers may occur during use, and if symptoms do not resolve over two months a doctor should be consulted.

What the name gets wrong

Feverfew comes from the Latin febrifugia, "fever-dispelling", reflecting an older use. That is not the indication in the monograph. The name is an example of how far a plant’s historical use can drift from its approved one.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to the Balkans and Anatolia, now naturalised across much of Europe. Also grown as a garden ornamental in Turkey.
Harvest season
Daisy-like white and yellow flowers through the summer; the aerial parts are gathered in flower.
Local names
The TİTCK monograph gives a single name: gümüş düğme.

Mix-up risk

Confusion with chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla) is the commonest: the flowers look alike and both are in the same family, but the uses are entirely different. One of its old names was Matricaria parthenium, which muddles older sources too. The Latin name on the label settles it.

Preparation

The monograph describes standardised preparations of the aerial parts. The use is long-term by nature: prophylaxis requires regular dosing over weeks, though the Turkish monograph asks for medical advice if symptoms have not resolved after two months. Not used under 18.

What science says

Feverfew is one of the most heavily marketed herbs for migraine, so it is worth stating exactly where the evidence stands. The 2015 update of the Cochrane review covers six double-blind randomised trials (561 patients). The trials had no common outcome measure and differed too much to pool; but the most recent one added to the review is larger and more rigorous (218 patients) and gives numbers: feverfew reduced migraine frequency from 4.8 to 2.9 attacks a month and placebo from 4.8 to 3.5 — a difference of 0.6 attacks per month. No statistically significant differences were reported for the secondary outcomes: intensity and duration of attacks, nausea and vomiting, global assessment. The review’s own verdict is measured: this adds "some positive evidence" to the mixed and inconclusive findings of the earlier review, but it is low quality evidence that needs confirming in larger rigorous trials with stable extracts and clearly defined migraine populations before firm conclusions can be drawn. So there is a measured effect, but a small one on a weak footing. On safety the review is positive: only mild and transient adverse events were reported, most commonly gastrointestinal complaints and mouth ulcers — which confirms the Turkish monograph’s mouth-ulcer warning from trial data. Feverfew appears not to be associated with any major safety concerns.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Medicines metabolised by liver enzymesThe Turkish monograph names four: CYP1A2, CYP2C19, CYP2C9 and CYP3A4. These enzymes break down a large share of all medicines; if one is affected, the blood level of a drug processed by it can change. Anyone on regular medication should mention feverfew to their doctor or pharmacist.
Aspirin, warfarin and antiplatelet medicinesThe Turkish monograph reports interaction with anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs. This group matters especially, because their effect is balanced within a narrow range and a small shift changes bleeding risk.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph says "none reported". The Turkish list is more detailed; the two do not conflict, since "none reported" means not studied.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Feverfew.
