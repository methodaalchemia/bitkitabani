BT-221 · Menyanthaceae (bogbean family)

Bogbean

Menyanthes trifoliata L.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

The "clover" in its Turkish name sends you to the wrong place to identify it: bogbean is not a legume but a member of its own family, Menyanthaceae, and a plant of wet ground. The comparison comes from the leaf being divided into three — but the flower resembles no clover at all: white-pink petals covered in long curling hairs, a very distinctive flower. One of the cases in this catalog where a name looks at the leaf and gets the family wrong.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the name "acı yonca" (bitter clover) and lists no synonyms. In English: bogbean, buckbean. ⚠️ Despite the "clover" in its Turkish name it is not a legume; it belongs to its own family, Menyanthaceae, and is a plant of wet ground.

In short

What is Bogbean?

Bogbean (Menyanthes trifoliata L.) is a plant of the Menyanthaceae (bogbean family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the leaf.

What is Bogbean used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places all three indications under "traditional use": (1) temporary loss of appetite; (2) relief of mild digestive disorders such as bloating and flatulence; (3) relief of minor articular and muscular pain. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no clinical trial of bogbean in humans.

Is Bogbean safe?

Safety label: caution. People with an active gastric or duodenal ulcer must not use it — both the EU and the Turkish monograph say so, and the reason is direct: the plant works by stimulating gastric secretion through its bitterness. Not for anyone hypersensitive to the active substance. For the joint-pain indication the EU monograph sets a limit: joint pain accompanied by swelling of the joints, redness or fever should be examined by a doctor — these can signal inflammatory joint disease and should not be managed with a herbal product. Use under 18 has not been established; not recommended in pregnancy or lactation. Tinctures may contain ethanol. If symptoms worsen, consult a doctor or pharmacist.

Traditional use

Europe — three indications

The EMA monograph places all three indications under "traditional use": (1) temporary loss of appetite; (2) relief of mild digestive disorders such as bloating and flatulence; (3) relief of minor articular and muscular pain. The first two are the classic use of the bitter-drug class.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the leaves" and states the key limit plainly: people with an active gastric or duodenal ulcer must not use it. The reason is always the same for bitter drugs: the bitter taste stimulates gastric secretion, which is no good for an open ulcer.

Anatolia

Bogbean grows in peat bogs, marshes and shallow water margins in northern Turkey. Its leaves are divided into three, which is what suggested clover; its white-pink flowers, with their fringed petals, are unmistakable.

In nature

Where it grows
Peat bogs, marshes and shallow water margins of the northern hemisphere. Found in northern Turkey; because its habitat is shrinking, gathering it from the wild is not appropriate.
Harvest season
White-pink flowers with fringed petals from April to June; the leaves are gathered in flower.
Local names
Acı yonca — the Turkish monograph gives one Turkish name.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ Its name causes confusion with the clovers (Trifolium, Medicago) and with sweet clover (Melilotus), also in this catalog; all three are legumes and bogbean is not. The habitat is what distinguishes it: bogbean grows only in wet and peaty ground. Gathering it from the wild is not advised; a pharmacy product is the better choice.

Preparation

The monograph describes preparations of the leaf; as with bitter drugs, for the appetite and digestive indications they are taken shortly before meals. Tinctures containing ethanol must carry the appropriate labelling. If symptoms worsen, consult a doctor or pharmacist. Use under 18 has not been established.

What science says

There is no clinical trial of bogbean in humans. Recent publications are at laboratory level, or concern the habitat rather than the plant: measurements of phenolic compounds and metals, a sedative-effect study in mice, research on peatland ecology. These do not show whether it relieves loss of appetite or bloating in a patient. The EU monograph rests all three indications on long use. The known mechanism comes from the bitterness: the secoiridoid bitter compounds in the leaf stimulate digestive secretion through taste. That mechanism explains both the first two indications and the ulcer contraindication; for the third indication (joint and muscle pain) the monograph gives no mechanism. The evidence level is Traditional.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No drug interactions reported in either monographBoth the EU and the Turkish monograph list nothing in their interaction sections. "None reported" is not evidence of no interaction; it means not studied.
Medicines that act on stomach acidThere is no interaction study; the issue comes from the mechanism. Bogbean is used to stimulate gastric secretion while acid-suppressing medicines do the opposite; the monograph already contraindicates the plant in active ulcer.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Bogbean.
