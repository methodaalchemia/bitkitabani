In short

What is Foxglove?

Foxglove (Digitalis spp.) is a plant of the Plantaginaceae (sinirotugiller) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: leaf (only as a raw material for medicines).

What is Foxglove used for?

Traditional use: The physician William Withering showed that the active plant in a family folk recipe kept in Shropshire was foxglove, and in 1785 published its careful, dosed use for dropsy (oedema) of cardiac origin. Scientific evidence: clinical. Cardiac glycosides inhibit the sodium-potassium pump in heart muscle, strengthening contraction and slowing the heart rate.

Is Foxglove safe?

Safety label: high risk. Every part of the plant is toxic. It causes nausea, vomiting, yellow-green vision and potentially fatal heart rhythm disorders. Before flowering, its leaves resemble comfrey and mullein; making tea from them by mistake is very dangerous. In Turkey call 114 (poison centre) or 112 if poisoning is suspected. The evidence label refers to the purified drug (digoxin), not the plant itself.

Traditional use

England

The physician William Withering showed that the active plant in a family folk recipe kept in Shropshire was foxglove, and in 1785 published its careful, dosed use for dropsy (oedema) of cardiac origin.

Modern medicine

Digoxin and digitoxin, obtained from the leaves, are still used for heart failure and some rhythm disorders, but only under medical supervision and with adjusted doses.

In nature

Where it grows
In Turkey species such as D. ferruginea and D. lanata grow wild; red-flowered D. purpurea is not wild in Turkey and is grown in gardens (Baytop).
Harvest season
Flowers in early summer.
Local names
Yüksük otu

Mix-up risk

The leaf rosette before flowering can be confused with comfrey (Symphytum) and mullein (Verbascum). In flower it is recognised by drooping, thimble-shaped flowers arranged on one side of the stem.

Preparation

Never used at home; only purified, dose-adjusted digoxin is used as a medicine.

What science says

Cardiac glycosides inhibit the sodium-potassium pump in heart muscle, strengthening contraction and slowing the heart rate. The therapeutic and toxic doses are very close, which is why even blood levels of prescribed digoxin are monitored.

Safety

High risk

Every part of the plant is toxic. It causes nausea, vomiting, yellow-green vision and potentially fatal heart rhythm disorders. Before flowering, its leaves resemble comfrey and mullein; making tea from them by mistake is very dangerous. In Turkey call 114 (poison centre) or 112 if poisoning is suspected. The evidence label refers to the purified drug (digoxin), not the plant itself.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Heart medicines (digoxin, beta blockers)The effect and toxicity of cardiac glycosides increase.
Diuretics and laxativesPotassium loss increases the toxicity of cardiac glycosides.
