In short

What is Mountain tea?

Mountain tea (Sideritis türleri) is a plant of the Lamiaceae family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean. Part used: flowering aerial parts.

What is Mountain tea used for?

Traditional use: Flowering stems gathered in late summer on the Taurus and western Anatolian highlands are brewed as a winter tea for colds, coughs and upset stomachs; in many places it is simply called “sage”. Scientific evidence: traditional. The EMA recognises mountain tea as a traditional remedy for cough with colds and for mild digestive complaints; this rests on long use, not on clinical trials.

Is Mountain tea safe?

Safety label: generally safe. Generally considered safe as a tea. For lack of data it is not recommended in pregnancy, while breastfeeding or for young children. Over-harvesting threatens some endemic species, so tea from cultivated plants is the better choice.

Traditional use

Anatolia

Flowering stems gathered in late summer on the Taurus and western Anatolian highlands are brewed as a winter tea for colds, coughs and upset stomachs; in many places it is simply called “sage”.

Balkans and Greece

Known as mountain tea in Greece and as the tea of the Pirin mountains in Bulgaria; village households keep it for winter.

In nature

Where it grows
Rocky, dry slopes of the Taurus, Aegean and Central Anatolian mountains, mostly above 1000 m.
Harvest season
Flowering stems are cut June–August, when the flowers are fully open.
Local names
dağ çayı, yayla çayı, adaçayı (in the Taurus)

Mix-up risk

Most tea sold as “sage” in the Taurus is actually mountain tea. True sage (Salvia) has broader leaves and a sharper smell; mountain tea has silvery, hairy leaves and yellow or white flowers.

Preparation

A handful of dried flowering stems is steeped in boiled water for 5–10 minutes, with lemon and honey if liked.

What science says

The EMA recognises mountain tea as a traditional remedy for cough with colds and for mild digestive complaints; this rests on long use, not on clinical trials. Antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects have been shown in the lab. A handful of small human studies, mostly on attention and cognition, give limited results.

Safety

Generally safe

Generally considered safe as a tea. For lack of data it is not recommended in pregnancy, while breastfeeding or for young children. Over-harvesting threatens some endemic species, so tea from cultivated plants is the better choice.
