BT-252 · Fabaceae (legume family)

Red clover

Trifolium pratense L.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

Clover’s real feat in farming is not medicine but nitrogen: bacteria living in nodules on its roots fix nitrogen from the air into the soil. Sowing clover instead of leaving land fallow let eighteenth-century European fields yield every year, and was one of the quiet parts of the agricultural revolution. The four-leaf-clover superstition belongs to this plant too — three is the ordinary number, and the name says so.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the names "kırmızı yonca, çayır tırfılı, çayır dutu, çayır üçgülü"; English red clover, cow clover, meadow clover. The part used is the flowers. No EU or WHO monograph, but there is a Turkish monograph. In the Bingöl honey registration, clover is one of the five taxa that distinguish the honey.

In short

What is Red clover?

Red clover (Trifolium pratense L.) is a plant of the Fabaceae (legume family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe, Asia and Siberia. Part used: the flowers.

What is Red clover used for?

Traditional use: Red clover is a staple of European and Anatolian meadows, and because it fixes nitrogen it has been sown for centuries as green manure and fodder in place of fallow. Scientific evidence: limited. Red clover’s claim is menopausal hot flushes, and the claim has been tested repeatedly; the overall result is not convincing.

Is Red clover safe?

Safety label: caution. A plant that grazing animals eat in the meadow — but a supplement is another matter. The Turkish contraindications are clear: not used in hormonal disease, in oestrogen-dependent cancer, or where there is a family history of it; not in protein S deficiency; stopped two weeks before surgery; not under 12, in pregnancy or in lactation. Reported adverse effects: rash-like reactions, muscle pain, headache, nausea and, in some women, vaginal bleeding. Infertility, uterine prolapse and difficulty in labour and lactation are also in the monograph. With an anticoagulant the bleeding risk rises. The WHO monograph also corrects a myth: red clover contains no coumarin, so concerns about blood clotting are groundless (a mistake that comes from confusing it with sweet clover). In clinical studies no adverse effect was reported up to 160 mg of isoflavones a day.

Traditional use

Anatolia and Europe — fodder first, medicine after

Red clover is a staple of European and Anatolian meadows, and because it fixes nitrogen it has been sown for centuries as green manure and fodder in place of fallow. In folk medicine a tea of the flowers was drunk for coughs and skin complaints and the mash put on wounds. The nectar at the base of the floret is sweet; children sucking the flowers is a habit shared across Europe and Anatolia.

Turkey — on the official list

The Turkish monograph lists the flowers, and its warnings are heavy: not for anyone sensitive to the active compound; not in hormonal disorders, in oestrogen-dependent cancers, or where there is a family history of such cancer; it may cause rash-like reactions, muscle pain, headache, nausea and, in some women, vaginal bleeding; infertility, uterine prolapse and difficulty in labour and lactation are recorded; not in protein S deficiency; stopped two weeks before surgery; insufficient data under 12, in pregnancy and in lactation.

Bingöl — an indicator plant of the honey

The denomination-of-origin registration counts clover among the five taxa that distinguish Bingöl honey. In the pollen table *T. nigrescens* is secondary, while *T. campestre*, *T. diffusum*, *T. pratense* and *T. repens* are minor or trace. Clover is a genuine nectar source for bees.

The West — a menopause supplement

Since the 1990s red clover extracts have been sold as supplements for menopausal hot flushes, on the strength of their isoflavone content. What the clinical record says is below — and it is weak.

WHO monograph — the flower

The World Health Organization monograph (Vol. 4, 2009) does something unusual: instead of giving an indication under "uses described in pharmacopoeias" it writes that although numerous clinical trials have assessed red clover extracts for menopausal symptoms, hyperlipidaemia, osteoporosis and prostate cancer, the data are as yet insufficient to support any of these indications. In traditional medicine: topically for psoriasis and eczema, orally for asthma and cough. Uses supported by clinical data: none.

Australia — "clover disease"

The plant’s hormonal effect was first seen in sheep: in Australian flocks grazing heavily on clover, infertility, abnormal lactation and prolapsed uterus — "clover disease", attributed to the oestrogen-like effect of isoflavones. The WHO monograph records this and adds: in human trials no adverse effects were reported at up to 160 mg of isoflavones a day.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to Europe, western Asia and north Africa, now across the temperate world. Common in Turkish meadows and pastures and sown as fodder. In Bingöl it grows where steppe gives way to meadow.
Harvest season
In flower from May to September; the flowers are gathered fully open and dried in the shade.
Local names
Üçgül, kırmızı yonca, çayır tırfılı, çayır dutu, çayır üçgülü (TİTCK); tırfıl, yonca.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ The Turkish word "yonca" covers three separate genera. Sweet clover is *Melilotus officinalis*, bogbean is *Menyanthes trifoliata*, and clover is *Trifolium* — three different plants with different uses and different safety profiles. *Menyanthes trifoliata* is confused especially often because "trifoliata" sits in its name, but it is not a *Trifolium* and not even in the same family. ⚠️ White clover (*T. repens*) is a different species; the medical literature is on red clover. White clover (Trifolium repens) is a separate species and is not within the scope of the monographs.

Preparation

Prepared as an infusion of the dried flowers. Supplements are sold as standardised isoflavone extracts and are not the same thing as flower tea. The Turkish monograph gives no dose; it says the plant should be used under the supervision of a doctor or pharmacist trained in herbal treatment. As fodder it is used fresh or as hay. The WHO monograph gives an oral dose: 240–480 mg of extract of the herbal substance, corresponding to 40–80 mg of isoflavones a day.

What science says

Red clover’s claim is menopausal hot flushes, and the claim has been tested repeatedly; the overall result is not convincing. A Cochrane review examined phytoestrogens for vasomotor menopausal symptoms and found no evidence of effectiveness overall (*Cochrane Database Syst Rev*, 2007). A 2015 meta-analysis and systematic review describes a small effect for phytoestrogens while stressing the inconsistency between trials (*Climacteric*, 2015); an earlier review in *JAMA* did not find red clover convincing among non-hormonal options (2006). A 2021 assessment asks separately whether the extract’s effect on hot flushes is clinically meaningful at all (*Nutrients*, 2021). ⚠️ Isoflavones bind to the oestrogen receptor, so the question is not only whether it works but who must not take it. That is where the Turkish contraindication for oestrogen-dependent cancer comes from. Evidence rating "limited". Red clover is the plant the WHO said "insufficient data" about in 2009 — and in the fifteen years since, data accumulated. A meta-analysis published in 2021 in Nutrients questioned the clinical meaningfulness of the extract’s effect on hot flushes. A GRADE-assessed meta-analysis published in 2026 in the European Journal of Obstetrics & Gynecology and Reproductive Biology pooled 9 randomised trials (women aged 40–65, 37–160 mg isoflavones a day, 12 weeks to 12 months): a small-to-moderate reduction in hot-flush frequency against placebo (SMD −0.45; 95% CI −0.81 to −0.08), most trials at low risk of bias, no publication bias detected. The authors’ conclusion: "modest but statistically significant". For comparison: in this same catalog, soy isoflavones showed no significant effect on hot flushes. Evidence level Limited: an effect exists, it is small, and the WHO’s hesitation of that day has been partly overtaken.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Anticoagulants, thrombolytics, low-molecular-weight heparins, warfarinOn the Turkish list; the risk of bleeding rises. Not used together, and stopped two weeks before surgery.
Contraceptives, oestrogen, progesterone and antioestrogens such as tamoxifenOn the Turkish list; because isoflavones bind the oestrogen receptor they may disturb hormonal treatment. This matters particularly for anyone taking tamoxifen.
Drugs metabolised by cytochrome P450 3A4, 1A2, 2C19 and 2C9On the Turkish list; blood levels of drugs broken down by these enzymes may change. Anyone on regular medication should tell their doctor.
Tamoxifen and other anti-oestrogenic drugsThe WHO monograph summarises conflicting data: some studies suggest isoflavones enhance tamoxifen’s effect, while in rodent models genistein reduced it. The monograph’s conclusion is firm: not recommended in those being treated with tamoxifen or other anti-oestrogenic drugs until further research has been done.
Hormone therapies (theoretical)A plant with oestrogen-like effects works along the same pathway as hormone replacement therapy or the contraceptive pill; the monograph does not list this separately, but it is an extension of its "contraindicated in hormone-associated diseases".

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Red clover.
