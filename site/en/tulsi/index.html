In short

What is Holy basil?

Holy basil (Ocimum tenuiflorum) is a plant of the Lamiaceae family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia. Part used: leaf.

What is Holy basil used for?

Traditional use: Regarded as a form of the goddess Lakshmi and consort of Vishnu; many Hindu homes keep a small shrine for tulsi in the courtyard, where it is watered and prayed to daily. Scientific evidence: limited. A systematic review (2017) looked at 24 human studies reporting favourable effects on stress, blood sugar and cholesterol, and found no serious side effects; but most studies were small and short.

Is Holy basil safe?

Safety label: generally safe. Generally safe as a tea. Animal studies suggest it may reduce fertility, so people trying to conceive and pregnant women should avoid it. It may lower blood sugar; take care with diabetes medicines. People on blood thinners should stop it before surgery.

Traditional use

Hinduism

Regarded as a form of the goddess Lakshmi and consort of Vishnu; many Hindu homes keep a small shrine for tulsi in the courtyard, where it is watered and prayed to daily. Prayer beads are made from its stems.

Ayurveda

Drunk as a tea for cough, fever, stress and digestion; its name is said to mean “the incomparable one”.

In nature

Where it grows
Homes, temples and fields across the Indian subcontinent and south-east Asia; grown in Türkiye as a summer pot plant.
Harvest season
Leaves are picked as flowering begins; year-round in warm climates.
Local names
tulsi, kutsal fesleğen

Mix-up risk

It differs from kitchen basil (O. basilicum): smaller, hairy, clove-scented leaves. Thai “kaphrao” is the same species.

Preparation

A dessertspoon of fresh or dried leaves is steeped in a cup of water for 5–10 minutes.

What science says

A systematic review (2017) looked at 24 human studies reporting favourable effects on stress, blood sugar and cholesterol, and found no serious side effects; but most studies were small and short. It is called an “adaptogen”, a concept that is not well defined scientifically.

Safety

Generally safe

Generally safe as a tea. Animal studies suggest it may reduce fertility, so people trying to conceive and pregnant women should avoid it. It may lower blood sugar; take care with diabetes medicines. People on blood thinners should stop it before surgery.
