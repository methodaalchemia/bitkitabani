Dill

Anethum graveolens L.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

In Turkey "dereotu" means the leaf; the WHO monograph never mentions the leaf and writes only of the seed. And for that seed the monograph’s firmest sentence is this: not in pregnancy, because in traditional medicine it was used for the opposite purpose — to prevent pregnancy and to start labour. The green on top of the cacık and the pharmacist’s seed extract come from one plant, but the monograph’s warning separates them.

In short

What is Dill?

Dill (Anethum graveolens L.) is a plant of the Apiaceae (carrot family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe, Asia and Siberia. Part used: the dried ripe fruit; the essential oil.

What is Dill used for?

Traditional use: The World Health Organization monograph (Vol. Scientific evidence: limited. There is no clinical trial of dill in the WHO indication (digestion); but there is a meta-analysis in the metabolic field.

Is Dill safe?

Safety label: caution. Not to be used in pregnancy or lactation — the WHO monograph writes this as a firm contraindication: fruit extracts have traditionally been used to prevent pregnancy and induce labour, and there are data suggesting teratogenic effects. Adding the leaf to food in the kitchen is not within this warning; the warning concerns seed extract and essential-oil doses. In a patient with a history of allergic rhinitis, oral itching, swelling of tongue and throat, urticaria, vomiting and diarrhoea were reported — anyone allergic to the carrot family should take care. The essential oil was cytotoxic to human lymphocytes in the laboratory; the oil is taken orally only in measured doses. No data for children.

Traditional use

WHO monograph — the fruit

The World Health Organization monograph (Vol. 3, 2007): no use supported by clinical data. Uses described in pharmacopoeias: dyspepsia, gastritis, flatulence and stomach ache. In traditional medicine: appetite stimulant, diuretic, galactagogue, analgesic; diarrhoea, asthma, neuralgia, insomnia, kidney stones — and, note, as a contraceptive and to induce labour. That last traditional use is the reason for the monograph’s pregnancy contraindication.

Turkey

There is no monograph for dill on the Turkish list. One of the staple greens of Anatolian cooking; its fresh leaf goes into cacık, olive-oil dishes and pastries. In folk medicine its seed is widely boiled against colic in infants; the WHO monograph writes that there are no data for use in children.

Europe — "gripe water"

Dill oil has been a classic ingredient of the "gripe water" sold in Britain for infant colic since the 19th century; the name "dill" is said to come from Old English dylle, "to soothe". The monograph says there are no data for infants; tradition and data part ways here too.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to southern Europe and western Asia; grown everywhere. In every garden in Turkey.
Harvest season
Yellow umbels in summer; the fruit ripens in late summer and is dried. The leaf is picked fresh from spring on.
Local names
Dereotu — the common Turkish name; no unsourced regional names given.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ It is confused with fennel in look and scent; separate genus, separate monograph. Umbellifer seeds should not be gathered from the wild; the family contains lethal species such as hemlock.

Preparation

The WHO monograph gives an average daily dose: fruit 3 g; essential oil 0.1–0.3 g; or equivalent preparations. Tea, essential oil and galenical preparations, orally. Not recommended in pregnancy or lactation. No data for children.

What science says

There is no clinical trial of dill in the WHO indication (digestion); but there is a meta-analysis in the metabolic field. A systematic review published in 2022 in Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition pooled 7 randomised trials (330 people): dill supplementation lowered LDL cholesterol by 15.6 mg/dL, serum insulin and HOMA-IR; no significant effect on total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL or fasting glucose. The authors write that the effect grew with higher doses and longer use and that the findings need confirming. A 2016 Cochrane review (dietary supplements for dysmenorrhoea) and a 2026 Apiaceae meta-analysis examine dill in period pain — low-quality evidence. All with seed or extract, not the fresh leaf of the kitchen. Evidence level Limited.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
WHO: no interaction dataThe monograph states plainly that no information is available on drug interactions, teratogenic effects or paediatric use. As a supplement affecting blood glucose and LDL, the possibility of stacking on antidiabetics is a theoretical note.
