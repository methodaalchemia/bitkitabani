Buckwheat

Evidence: LimitedGenerally safe

A pollen record of about 12,800 years from south-western China shows that buckwheat pollen increased markedly in the region about 4,000 years ago and reached its highest level about 1,270 years ago (*New Phytologist*, 2023).

Other names
Common buckwheat; Turkish karabuğday. Despite its name it is not wheat: it is not a grass but a pseudocereal in the same family as sorrel and rhubarb. Its close relative Tartary buckwheat (Fagopyrum tataricum) is a separate species.

In short

What is Buckwheat?

Buckwheat (Fagopyrum esculentum Moench) is a plant of the Polygonaceae (knotweed family) family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia, Europe. Part used: the grain (as flour and groats); the flowering aerial parts (tea); the husk (pillow filling).

What is Buckwheat used for?

Traditional use: Buckwheat is a field crop that originated in China and spread from there across Eurasia. Scientific evidence: limited. There are human studies, but the results are not consistent.

Is Buckwheat safe?

Safety label: generally safe. As a food it is safe in ordinary amounts. The main risk is allergy: buckwheat can cause severe allergic reactions, including anaphylaxis. According to the 2021 review the prevalence of allergy in Japan, Korea and the buckwheat-consuming areas of China is around 0.1–0.4 percent; cross-reactivity has been reported with peanut, latex, coconut, quinoa and poppy seed. Allergy can arise not only from eating but also when handling buckwheat and when sleeping on pillows filled with buckwheat husk. The plant contains fagopyrins, which cause sensitivity to light; according to the 2015 systematic review the grain, flour and tea are safe in ordinary amounts, but a diet made up largely of sprouts, herb and especially flowers, or fagopyrin-rich extracts, may cause fagopyrism (excessive sensitivity to sunlight). The sources of this page hold no data on the use of the tea or extracts in pregnancy, breastfeeding and children; eating it as food is a separate matter. At signs of allergy (hives, shortness of breath, swelling of the lips or throat) 112 is called.

Traditional use

China and East Asia — origin and everyday food

Buckwheat is a field crop that originated in China and spread from there across Eurasia. A pollen study published in *New Phytologist* in 2023 reports that buckwheat pollen increased markedly in south-western China about 4,000 years ago and that this points to cultivation. It is an everyday food in Japan, Korea and the buckwheat-consuming areas of China; a 2021 review writes that buckwheat allergy is followed most closely in these countries.

Europe — buckwheat herb tea for oedema

In Europe, besides the grain, the flowering aerial parts of the plant are also used as a tea. A double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial carried out in Berlin (1996) tested this tea for leg oedema in patients with chronic venous insufficiency. The results are below, in the science section.

Gluten-free eating — a naturally gluten-free grain

A 2015 review (*Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry*) describes buckwheat as a gluten-free pseudocereal of the knotweed family. A Turkish review (2020) likewise writes that its grains contain no gluten and have a place in the diet of people with coeliac disease. However, a study from Türkiye (2020) showed that buckwheat can become contaminated with gluten through contact with broken wheat, rye or barley grains and with surfaces these have touched.

Farming — fodder, green manure and soil protection

According to the Turkish review (*Gıda ve Yem Bilimi Teknolojisi Dergisi*, 2020) its seeds are fed to poultry; its stalks, straw and silage are eaten readily by ruminants. The same review notes that the plant enriches the soil in organic matter, conserves soil moisture and can be used as green manure and in erosion control.

In nature

Where it grows
It originated in China and is grown widely across Eurasia. According to the Turkish review it enriches the soil in organic matter, improves the physical structure of the soil and conserves soil moisture; it can be used as green manure and in erosion control.
Harvest season
It is sown as a field crop. The sources this page relies on give no sowing or harvest months.
Local names
It has no established local name in Turkish.

Mix-up risk

It shares only its name with wheat (*Triticum*); it belongs to a different family and contains no gluten. Tartary buckwheat (*Fagopyrum tataricum*) is the second cultivated species; its allergens are named separately and its composition differs.

Preparation

As food the grain is eaten cooked, as flour or groats. There is no accepted dose for medicinal use; no EU, WHO or Turkish (TİTCK) monograph was found among the sources this page relies on. In the 1996 clinical trial the buckwheat herb tea was given for 3 months. For people with coeliac disease it is important to choose a product documented as gluten-free; the grain is gluten-free by nature but contamination can occur during handling.

What science says

There are human studies, but the results are not consistent. A 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis (*Nutrients*) examined 13 randomised controlled human studies, 2 cross-sectional studies and 21 animal studies; with buckwheat intake blood glucose was 0.85 mmol/L lower, total cholesterol 0.50 mmol/L lower and triglycerides 0.25 mmol/L lower. The authors stress that all results showed high unexplained heterogeneity and that the effect on weight, blood pressure, insulin and LDL cholesterol remains unclear. In the trial that tested buckwheat herb tea (1996), 67 patients with chronic venous insufficiency were followed for 3 months: leg volume did not change in the tea group and rose by 110 ml in the placebo group; subjective symptoms, however, decreased in both groups. The authors note that this condition is very sensitive to placebo. According to the 2015 review, flavonoids such as rutin and quercetin, D-chiro-inositol and buckwheat proteins may be responsible for the effects; these findings rest predominantly on cell and animal studies.

Safety

Generally safe

As a food it is safe in ordinary amounts. The main risk is allergy: buckwheat can cause severe allergic reactions, including anaphylaxis. According to the 2021 review the prevalence of allergy in Japan, Korea and the buckwheat-consuming areas of China is around 0.1–0.4 percent; cross-reactivity has been reported with peanut, latex, coconut, quinoa and poppy seed.
