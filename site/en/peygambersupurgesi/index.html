In short

What is Sweet wormwood?

Sweet wormwood (Artemisia annua L.) is a plant of the Asteraceae (daisy family) family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia, Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Africa. Part used: flowering aerial parts (leaves and flowering tops).

What is Sweet wormwood used for?

Traditional use: The plant has been used in Chinese medicine since ancient times. Scientific evidence: limited. There are two separate things here: pure artemisinin derivatives and the plant itself.

Is Sweet wormwood safe?

Safety label: caution. ⚠️ It is not used in place of medicine to treat malaria. Malaria can be fatal; in an illness with fever, especially after returning from an area where malaria occurs, a doctor is consulted. In New Zealand, 29 reports of liver injury had been received by 31 January 2019 concerning an *A. annua* extract sold for joint health: the patients were aged 47–93, 16 had jaundice, 9 were admitted to hospital, and 27 recovered or improved when the product was stopped. If jaundice, itching or dark urine appears, the product is stopped and a doctor is seen. According to a 2026 systematic review, the adverse effects reported with the tea are mostly mild (bitter taste, transient gastrointestinal discomfort), but safety data are insufficient for children under five, pregnant women, breastfeeding women and people with liver or kidney disease. It is not used in these groups.

Traditional use

China — qinghao and intermittent fever

The plant has been used in Chinese medicine since ancient times. A 2020 review (*Pharmacology & Therapeutics*) lists its traditional uses as follows: intermittent fever due to malaria, fever arising from exhaustion, tuberculosis, lice, wounds, scabies and dysentery. According to the Nobel Foundation’s account the plant was used in China around AD 400 for "intermittent fevers"; Ge Hong’s handbook of prescriptions for emergencies, from the Eastern Jin period (about 317–420), also mentions it.

China — Project 523 and artemisinin

According to the Nobel Foundation’s account, Project 523 was launched on 23 May 1967 to find a cure for chloroquine-resistant malaria; Tu Youyou was appointed head of the team in 1969. Because boiling damaged the active ingredient, Tu switched to an ether-based solvent that boils at a lower temperature. The extract obtained in 1971 showed 100 percent success in mice and monkeys; the team tried the substance first on themselves and then on 21 patients in Hainan. Artemisinin was purified the following year, and Tu Youyou received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2015.

Africa — the tea debate

Because artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) are in some places expensive or hard to obtain, the plant’s tea, tablets and capsules were promoted as a cheap alternative. The World Health Organization reviewed the evidence in 2019 and stated that it does not support the promotion or use of Artemisia plant material in any form for the prevention or treatment of malaria. The reasons are below, in the science section.

Anatolia — one species among the wormwoods

A study published in 2023 in *Türk Tarım ve Doğa Bilimleri Dergisi* writes that the genus Artemisia is represented in Türkiye by 27 taxa and that species of the genus are used in Anatolian folk medicine as a tonic, against malaria, as an anthelmintic, to lower blood sugar and to heal wounds. The same study collected its *A. annua* specimen from a roadside in the Akyazı district of Sakarya. The source gives these uses for the genus as a whole; it records no Anatolian use specific to this species.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew’s Plants of the World Online record its native range extends from North Africa to Eurasia; it is an annual and grows primarily in the temperate biome. It has been introduced in North and South America, in many European countries and in New Zealand. In Türkiye it has been recorded on roadsides (Sakarya, Akyazı).
Harvest season
It is an annual; it grows from seed every year. The Sakarya specimen in the 2023 study was collected in October. The sources of this page give no definite month for flowering.
Local names
Pelin otu, Kâbe süpürgesi, yavşan otu, Kâbe kekiği, peygamber süpürgesi. The source (2023) gives these names for the genus Artemisia as a whole.

Mix-up risk

Wormwood (*Artemisia absinthium*) is a different species and is not a source of artemisinin. Because the genus has 27 taxa in Türkiye, the same local name may be given to more than one species; artemisinin is associated only with *A. annua*.

Preparation

There is no accepted dose; no EU, WHO or Turkish (TİTCK) monograph was found among the sources this page relies on. The traditional use is a tea prepared from the dried aerial parts. According to the Nobel Foundation’s account, boiling damages the active ingredient; in addition, WHO notes that the amount of artemisinin in herbal products is highly variable. Where malaria is suspected, the tea is not a treatment option; the treatment WHO recommends is licensed combination medicines.

What science says

There are two separate things here: pure artemisinin derivatives and the plant itself. Artemisinin derivatives are the basis of today’s standard treatment of malaria; the same cannot be said of the plant tea. In an open, randomised, controlled pilot trial (2004) the cure rate with the tea at day 7 averaged 74 percent, against 91 percent with quinine, but the rate of recrudescence was high in the tea groups; the authors concluded that use on its own cannot be recommended. In a small double-blind trial from 2008, cure in the tea group was 7/10 at day 7 but fell to 1/9 at day 28. A large trial published in 2019 comparing the tea with a drug was retracted by the journal in 2020. According to WHO’s 2019 assessment, the amount of artemisinin in herbal products is often not enough to clear the parasite from the blood completely, widespread use could hasten artemisinin resistance, and artemisinin in any form does not work well for preventing malaria. A 2013 review (*Journal of Ethnopharmacology*) finds that very little research exists on the traditional preparation and that the available publications contradict one another. The effects reported on cancer, viruses and inflammation are predominantly at the laboratory level.

Safety

Caution

⚠️ It is not used in place of medicine to treat malaria. Malaria can be fatal; in an illness with fever, especially after returning from an area where malaria occurs, a doctor is consulted. In New Zealand, 29 reports of liver injury had been received by 31 January 2019 concerning an *A. annua* extract sold for joint health: the patients were aged 47–93, 16 had jaundice, 9 were admitted to hospital, and 27 recovered or improved when the product was stopped. It is not used in these groups.
