BT-324 · Myrtaceae (myrtle family)

Lemon-scented tea tree

Leptospermum petersonii F.M.Bailey
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

Although its name says "tea tree", this is not the species tea tree oil comes from: that is *Melaleuca alternifolia*, in a different genus. In Australia the name "tea tree" is given to several quite unrelated genera; when buying an oil you cannot tell which one you have without the Latin name.

Other names
English lemon-scented tea tree, lemon tea tree. It has no established Turkish name; "limon çay ağacı" is a descriptive rendering of the English one. Neither the European Medicines Agency nor the Turkish agency has a monograph for this species.
Part used
Leaf; the essential oil distilled from the leaf
Region
Oceania
Use
Healing
In short

What is Lemon-scented tea tree?

Lemon-scented tea tree (Leptospermum petersonii F.M.Bailey) is a plant of the Myrtaceae (myrtle family) family. Traditions that use it: Oceania. Part used: leaf; the essential oil distilled from the leaf.

What is Lemon-scented tea tree used for?

Traditional use: A multicentre, randomised, assessor-blind trial published in the Australasian Journal of Dermatology in 2018 compared a head lice product containing eucalyptus oil together with this species’ oil against a neurotoxic product containing pyrethrins and piperonyl butoxide. Scientific evidence: traditional. The only study in humans concerns head lice, and it should be read carefully on three counts: the product tested is a mixture of this species’ oil and eucalyptus oil, so the result cannot be attributed to this species alone; the trial was run by the maker of the product; and although the assessor was blinded, the participants were not.

Is Lemon-scented tea tree safe?

Safety label: caution. What is at issue is a concentrated essential oil. In the lice trial skin irritancy and sensitisation testing was done with the product, but not the same testing in both age groups: the 2018 citation in the Australasian Journal of Dermatology reports no irritation or sensitisation in adults, and no irritation in children — that is, sensitisation was not tested in children. The adverse events seen were transient itching, burning and stinging. That is a finding about a particular product at a particular concentration and cannot be generalised to the undiluted oil. The only measurement of the oil’s composition we have is the Caputo study published in Frontiers in Microbiology in 2020, and in the "variety B" chemotype measured there the dominant components were geranyl acetate, γ-terpinene, geraniol, terpinolene, α-pinene, p-cymene and linalool; geraniol is a fragrant compound and can sensitise the skin. Citral was not measured in this species’ oil in the sources relied on here — citral belongs to the lemon myrtle (*Backhousia citriodora*) record in the same batch, not to this one — so no warning resting on citral is written here. The same study confirms the species has several chemotypes; without being told the chemotype you do not know the composition of a batch of oil. What is written for manuka (*Leptospermum scoparium*) does not belong to this species — manuka honey is an entirely different product and provides no grounds here. The sources relied on here contain no separate assessment for pregnancy, breastfeeding or drug interactions.

Traditional use

For head lice — a randomised trial in children

A multicentre, randomised, assessor-blind trial published in the Australasian Journal of Dermatology in 2018 compared a head lice product containing eucalyptus oil together with this species’ oil against a neurotoxic product containing pyrethrins and piperonyl butoxide. Among per-protocol participants the cure rate was 83% against 36%; in a second, open trial a single application was reported to be 100% pediculicidal. Adverse events were limited to transient itching, burning and stinging. What was tested is not this species’ oil alone but a mixture of two oils, and the trial was conducted by researchers at the company that makes the product.

Against fungi — in vitro measurements and one study in mice

A study published in Medical Mycology in 2010 examined the oil’s effect on *Candida albicans* and *Aspergillus fumigatus*, reporting that its mode of action is direct disturbance of the fungal cell membrane and that the volatile fraction is strongly antifungal even without contact with the liquid oil; that is an in vitro measurement. The Hood study published the same year in the Journal of Antimicrobial Chemotherapy was, as its title says, carried out both in vitro and in vivo: the volatiles were found fungicidal in vitro after short exposure (≤1 h), and efficacy was then assessed in a murine model. No viable fungi could be recovered from animals that completed the treatment regimen, and the reduction in fungal burden in the lungs of infected animals was, the authors write, larger than that reported for the conventional antifungal drugs of choice. This is an animal study, not a treatment trial in humans, and it is not a treatment recommendation for aspergillosis.

As a food preservative

A study published in Molecules in 2020 assessed the species as a potential natural food preservative. A study published the same year in Frontiers in Microbiology measured the oil’s composition and confirmed that the species has several chemotypes: in the "variety B" examined, the dominant components were geranyl acetate, γ-terpinene, geraniol, terpinolene, α-pinene, p-cymene and linalool. So this species’ oil is not a single composition; without knowing the chemotype you do not know what is in the oil.

In nature

Where it grows
Kew records it in three regions: New South Wales, Queensland and Kenya; the first two are its natural range, Kenya a place it later spread to. It is a small tree or large shrub native to the east coast of Australia, whose crushed leaf smells of lemon.
Harvest season
The sources relied on here give no collection calendar for Turkey; the plant does not grow there naturally.
Local names
It has no established Turkish local name.

Mix-up risk

It is in the same genus as manuka (*Leptospermum scoparium*) but a separate species; one species’ monograph and restrictions do not belong to its relative. Tea tree (*Melaleuca alternifolia*) is an altogether different genus, and the similarity of the names indicates no kinship.

Preparation

No dose is accepted in an official monograph. The sources relied on here give no measure for a home-made preparation; what was used in the lice trial is a particular commercial product, and the application interval (three times, seven days apart) belongs to that product. The essential oil is not taken internally.

What science says

The only study in humans concerns head lice, and it should be read carefully on three counts: the product tested is a mixture of this species’ oil and eucalyptus oil, so the result cannot be attributed to this species alone; the trial was run by the maker of the product; and although the assessor was blinded, the participants were not. Against that, the comparison was made against an active product and the result is clear-cut. Not all the fungal measurements are in vitro: the 2010 study in Medical Mycology is, but the 2010 study in the Journal of Antimicrobial Chemotherapy also includes an animal study in a murine model alongside its in vitro work, and reports a marked reduction in lung fungal burden. That is not a study in humans and gives no treatment recommendation, but to say "all of it is in vitro" would be to misread the record. The oil’s composition has been measured to vary by chemotype, which makes it harder to carry one study’s result over to another batch of oil.
