Bird’s-foot trefoil

Lotus corniculatus L.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

The name comes from the fruit: the ripening pods open from a single point like fingers, which Turkish called a gazelle’s horn and English a bird’s foot. The same plant is where burnet moth larvae get their cyanide defence — the larva takes the plant’s cyanogenic compounds and stores them in its own body. A harmless-looking yellow flower in a meadow is one party to a chemical treaty in the insect world.

Other names
Turkish *gazalboynuzu* ("gazelle’s horn"), *boynuzlu yonca* — named for the ripe pods that open out like horns; English bird’s-foot trefoil. ⚠️ The lotus record on this site is *Nelumbo nucifera*, the sacred lotus — another family entirely; the clash is only in the genus name. The part used is the flowering aerial parts. No Turkish, EU or WHO monograph.

In short

What is Bird’s-foot trefoil?

Bird’s-foot trefoil (Lotus corniculatus L.) is a plant of the Fabaceae (baklagiller) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe, Asia and Siberia. Part used: the flowering aerial parts.

What is Bird’s-foot trefoil used for?

Traditional use: A 2020 ethnobotany-based study in *Molecules* records that in Turkish traditional medicine the aerial parts are used as an analgesic, against haemorrhoids, as a diuretic and as a sedative. Scientific evidence: traditional. On the medical side there is no human study.

Is Bird’s-foot trefoil safe?

Safety label: caution. ⚠️ It carries cyanogenic glycosides (linamarin, lotaustralin). The amount varies genetically from plant to plant, so "how much is safe" has no answer for a herbal tea. For that reason it is not recommended as a drink, and even in animal feeding the amount of fresh herb is kept measured. Because an effect on the central nervous system was shown in an animal model, care is needed for anyone on sedatives. No data for pregnancy, lactation or children.

Traditional use

Anatolia — in folk medicine

A 2020 ethnobotany-based study in *Molecules* records that in Turkish traditional medicine the aerial parts are used as an analgesic, against haemorrhoids, as a diuretic and as a sedative. The study itself starts from that record to test an antidepressant effect in an animal model.

Field and pasture — a forage plant

Bird’s-foot trefoil is a widespread pasture and forage plant in Turkey and elsewhere; being a legume it fixes nitrogen, and it stands drought better than alfalfa. Turkey has a long literature on adapting and breeding it.

Bingöl — an indicator plant of the honey

In the pollen table of the denomination-of-origin registration, *Lotus* sp. reaches dominant level. Its yellow flowers open through the summer and are a genuine nectar source for bees.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to Europe, Asia and north Africa, now widespread. Common in Turkish meadows, pastures, roadsides and fallow fields; in Bingöl where steppe gives way to meadow.
Harvest season
Yellow flowers from May to September; the pods ripen over the summer into their horn shape.
Local names
Gazalboynuzu, boynuzlu yonca, ceylanboynuzu, sarı yonca; bird’s-foot trefoil (English).

Mix-up risk

⚠️ The "lotus" record on this site is not this plant — that is *Nelumbo nucifera*, the sacred lotus; a different family and a different use. The clash comes only from the genus name *Lotus*. ⚠️ The Turkish "sarı yonca" is also used for sweet clover (*Melilotus officinalis*), another genus. ⚠️ The genus has many species in Turkey; the medical literature is on *L. corniculatus*.

Preparation

In folk use an infusion is made from the aerial parts. ⚠️ There is no reliable dose, and the plant carries cyanogenic glycosides, so no dose is offered on this page. As fodder it is used fresh or as hay.

What science says

On the medical side there is no human study. The most relevant work is the 2020 paper (*Molecules*) that starts from the sedative use in Turkish folk medicine and tests an antidepressant effect in an animal model — promising, but not carried over into people. ⚠️ The better-known side of the plant is chemical: it carries cyanogenic glycosides (linamarin and lotaustralin). So much so that it is one of the model species for cyanogenesis in plant–insect relations — the way burnet moth larvae take these compounds from the plant and use them in their own defence has been studied in detail (*Phytochemistry*, 2011). The cyanogenic content varies genetically within the species: two plants of the same species may not carry the same amount. Evidence rating "traditional".

Safety

Caution

⚠️ It carries cyanogenic glycosides (linamarin, lotaustralin). The amount varies genetically from plant to plant, so "how much is safe" has no answer for a herbal tea. For that reason it is not recommended as a drink, and even in animal feeding the amount of fresh herb is kept measured. Because an effect on the central nervous system was shown in an animal model, care is needed for anyone on sedatives. No data for pregnancy, lactation or children.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Central nervous system depressants (sedatives, sleeping pills, antidepressants) — theoreticalAn effect on behaviour was shown in an animal model, so an additive effect with drugs acting the same way is theoretically possible. No interaction has been shown in people.
General — no dataNo human drug-interaction study exists for this plant. Regular use is not recommended, because of the cyanogenic content.
