Common polypody

Polypodium vulgare L.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

The same property, in the same monograph, is both an indication and a side effect. The monograph warns those using polypody as an expectorant of a "mild laxative effect", then a paragraph later approves that same laxative effect as the second indication. That is not a contradiction but an honest classification: whether an effect is wanted is decided not by the plant but by the intention of the person using it. No other monograph in this catalog writes that duality so plainly.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the names "eğreltiotu, benlieğrelti, kaya eğreltisi"; Ctenopteris vulgaris (L.) Newman is a synonym. In English: common polypody. Since "eğreltiotu" is the general Turkish word for all ferns, this catalog uses the species-specific "kaya eğreltisi" (rock fern) as the title. ⚠️ Not to be confused with Polypodium leucotomos — the monograph’s own warning.

In short

What is Common polypody?

Common polypody (Polypodium vulgare L.) is a plant of the Polypodiaceae (polypody fern family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the rhizome.

What is Common polypody used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places both indications under "traditional use": (a) as an expectorant in cough and cold; (b) short-term use in occasional constipation. Scientific evidence: limited. There is no trial of common polypody in the monograph indication, but there is a single-ingredient randomised trial in a neighbouring field, and its results are mixed.

Is Common polypody safe?

Safety label: caution. See a doctor if dyspnoea, fever or purulent sputum occurs. When used as an expectorant a mild laxative effect may occur — the monograph counts it as an adverse reaction; at the constipation dose it is the intended effect. Not to be used longer than a week; prolonged use of a laxative plant habituates the bowel. Not under 12; not recommended in pregnancy or lactation. Hypersensitivity to the active substance can occur. ⚠️ If gathered from the wild, the species must be certain; there are poisonous ferns.

Traditional use

Europe — two indications

The EMA monograph places both indications under "traditional use": (a) as an expectorant in cough and cold; (b) short-term use in occasional constipation. The monograph does something curious: it lists "mild laxative effect" as an adverse reaction of indication (a) — the same property is an indication in one use and a side effect in the other.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph lists the rhizome and adds a sentence absent from the EU monograph: it must not be confused with Polypodium leucotomos Hieron. See a doctor if dyspnoea, fever or purulent sputum occurs; not to be used longer than a week; not under 12. The interaction section is empty.

Anatolia and Europe

Common polypody is a small fern of the northern forests of Turkey, growing on rocks, walls and the trunks of old trees. Its rhizome has a sweet taste reminiscent of liquorice; European folk medicine has known it for centuries as an expectorant and mild laxative.

In nature

Where it grows
Damp forests of the northern hemisphere; on rocks, stone walls and mossy tree trunks. Common in the Black Sea forests of Turkey.
Harvest season
Evergreen; the rhizomes are lifted in autumn and dried.
Local names
Eğreltiotu, benlieğrelti, kaya eğreltisi — all three appear in the Turkish monograph.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ The monograph’s own warning: not to be confused with Polypodium leucotomos. The Turkish word "eğreltiotu" also covers every fern, including poisonous ones such as male fern (Dryopteris filix-mas); a pharmacy product is the better choice, and gathering from the wild is not advised.

Preparation

For those over 12 the EU monograph gives separate doses for the two indications. As an expectorant: 4–5 g of the comminuted rhizome as a tea, 3–4 times daily. For constipation: 14–30 g of dried rhizome daily as a tea. Not to be used longer than a week. If symptoms persist, consult a doctor or pharmacist.

What science says

There is no trial of common polypody in the monograph indication, but there is a single-ingredient randomised trial in a neighbouring field, and its results are mixed. A multicentre trial published in 2026 in Explore divided 88 adults hospitalised with mild-to-moderate severe acute respiratory infection in two: half received a polypody root decoction in addition to standard care, half standard care alone. The findings cut both ways: heart rate fell faster and oxygen saturation improved more each day in the polypody group; but inflammatory markers (ESR and CRP) fell more slowly, and the neutrophil count rose faster. No serious adverse events occurred. The authors’ own conclusion is cautious: larger trials are needed. Three things need separating: the setting is more severe than the monograph’s (hospital, not merely a cold); what was measured were physiological indices, not whether the cough resolved; and some of the findings run the wrong way. One trial, 88 people, mixed results — evidence level Limited. A trap in reading: there are many randomised trials on Polypodium leucotomos (for oral photoprotection), but that is a different species and its data cannot be carried over; the monograph does not forbid the confusion for nothing.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No interactions reported in either monographBoth the EU and the Turkish monograph leave the interaction section empty. For a plant with a laxative effect there is a theoretical point: diarrhoea can deplete potassium, which can affect potassium-sensitive drugs such as digoxin; but the monographs do not say so, and it is not expected within a week’s use.
Not attributing Polypodium leucotomos data to this plantNot a drug interaction, but important for the record: all the photoprotection and skin studies were done with P. leucotomos. Common polypody has neither that indication nor that evidence.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Common polypody.
