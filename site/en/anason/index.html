Rakı turns white with water: anethole dissolves in alcohol but not in water, so it forms tiny droplets that scatter light. This is called the “ouzo effect”.

In short

What is Anise?

Anise (Pimpinella anisum) is a plant of the Apiaceae (carrot family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean. Part used: fruit (known as seed).

What is Anise used for?

Traditional use: It is the spice that gives rakı its scent; it is grown in fields in the Lakes Region and the Aegean. Scientific evidence: traditional. The EMA recognises anise fruit as a traditional remedy for mild indigestion, bloating and wind, and for cough with colds.

Is Anise safe?

Safety label: generally safe. Generally safe as a tea. It can cause allergy in people allergic to the carrot family (celery, fennel). The essential oil is concentrated and should not be used in pregnancy or by children. Ask a doctor before giving anise tea to babies.

Traditional use

Anatolia

It is the spice that gives rakı its scent; it is grown in fields in the Lakes Region and the Aegean. Among the people anise tea is drunk for wind, indigestion and cough; it is given to nursing mothers to increase their milk.

Mediterranean

Greek ouzo, French pastis and the arak of the Levant are also flavoured with anise. In Rome, anise cakes are said to have been served at the end of great feasts to help digestion.

In nature

Where it grows
It is an annual crop plant; in Türkiye it is grown in fields in the Lakes Region and the Aegean.
Harvest season
Seeds are harvested July–August, when the umbels turn yellow.
Local names
anason

Mix-up risk

Star anise (Illicium verum) is a different plant; its similar smell comes from the same anethole. Poison hemlock belongs to the same family as anise: wild white umbellifers should never be used as anise. ⚠️ The genus Pimpinella also has wild species that are not anise: the Pimpinella sp. that comes out dominant in the Bingöl honey registration is a wild one.

Preparation

A teaspoon of crushed seed is steeped in a cup of boiling water for 10 minutes; 2–3 cups a day.

What science says

The EMA recognises anise fruit as a traditional remedy for mild indigestion, bloating and wind, and for cough with colds. Its main compound, anethole, relaxes muscle and loosens phlegm in the lab. Human studies are few and small; there are a handful on menopausal hot flushes and period pain.

Safety

Generally safe

Generally safe as a tea. It can cause allergy in people allergic to the carrot family (celery, fennel). The essential oil is concentrated and should not be used in pregnancy or by children. Ask a doctor before giving anise tea to babies.
