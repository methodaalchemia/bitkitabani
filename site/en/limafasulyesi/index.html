BT-315 · Fabaceae (legume family)

Lima bean

Phaseolus lunatus L.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

The lima bean is one of the few crops domesticated twice in two different places: once from the Mesoamerican gene pool and once from the Andean. A 2023 study calls it "an excellent model to understand convergent evolution", because only a small share of the genes under selection is shared between the two gene pools — the same outcome was reached by two separate genetic routes.

Other names
It has no established Turkish name; "lima fasulyesi" is the descriptive name used here. Lima bean or butter bean in English, pallar or frijol lima in Spanish. Neither the European Medicines Agency nor Türkiye’s TİTCK has a monograph for this species. EMA’s `Phaseoli fructus (sine semine)` monograph belongs to *Phaseolus vulgaris* — a separate record on this site — and does not apply to *P. lunatus*.

In short

What is Lima bean?

Lima bean (Phaseolus lunatus L.) is a plant of the Fabaceae (legume family) family. Traditions that use it: Andes, North America. Part used: mature seed.

What is Lima bean used for?

Traditional use: A 2014 compilation of Phaseolus archaeobotany reports a domesticated *P. Scientific evidence: traditional. The lima bean is cyanogenic: its principal cyanogenic glycoside is linamarin, alongside lotaustralin.

Is Lima bean safe?

Safety label: caution. Raw seeds are not eaten; they are soaked, cooked and the water discarded. The seed carries the cyanogenic glycoside linamarin and releases hydrogen cyanide when raw. EFSA reports the acute lethal oral dose of cyanide in humans as 0.5–3.5 mg/kg body weight, with an acute reference dose of 20 µg/kg bw. The signs of acute cyanide poisoning are headache, vertigo, agitation, respiratory depression, metabolic acidosis, confusion, coma, convulsions and death. No human poisoning case from lima beans with published detail could be found — the summary sentence of EFSA’s 2019 assessment lists amygdalin preparations, bitter almonds and cassava as the sources of poisoning cases and does not list the lima bean; the acute toxicity summary in the same document adds apricot kernels to that list, but the lima bean is absent there too. But that does not mean no poisonings have occurred: in Table 6 of WHO Food Additives Series 65 (cyanogenic glycosides, addendum to the 74th JECFA meeting) the lima bean row has two parts, and the part carrying the high values is labelled "samples incriminated in poisonings" (2,100–3,120 mg/kg), while the values given in the same row as the normal range are 144–167 mg/kg (both from Gupta 1987). The upper figures cited above therefore belong, in that body’s own table, to samples linked with poisonings. The rule is thus not merely one of precaution. The sources set no maximum HCN level for lima beans; the limits set by the EU and by Australia-New Zealand cover apricot kernels, marzipan, canned stone fruit, alcoholic drinks and cassava products, and do not include the lima bean. The warning about cooking at low temperature in a slow cooker does not belong to this species: that warning was framed for phytohaemagglutinin (lectin) poisoning and for *Phaseolus vulgaris*, especially red kidney beans, and cannot be carried over to the lima bean’s linamarin. (There is no such warning for the pressure cooker; on the contrary, that is the method actually measured.) Children have been assessed separately, and the lima bean is named: Table 11 of the same WHO Food Additives Series 65 (p. 274) splits acute dietary exposure by age and states that "the highest acute dietary exposures for children 2–16 years of age were from apple juice, bread containing linseed, cassava and lima beans". The figures given are 1,170 µg/day, or 31 µg/kg bw for children 2–16 (90th percentile), and 4,500 µg/day, or 60 µg/kg bw, for adults 17 and over (97.5th percentile). The figure for children is above the EFSA acute reference dose of 20 µg/kg bw cited above. The fair counterpoint belongs here too: JECFA’s own benchmark in that same document was an acute reference dose of 90 µg/kg bw as cyanide equivalents, and against that there is no exceedance for lima beans — the exceedances identified in that document are for cassava, apple juice and bitter apricot kernels. Safety level "moderate".

Traditional use

The Andean coast — pre-Columbian agriculture

A 2014 compilation of Phaseolus archaeobotany reports a domesticated *P. lunatus* pod from the site of Chilca in Peru: 5,600 radiocarbon years, roughly 6,400 calendar years ago. The same source records that the species was domesticated twice, independently — once in Mesoamerica and once in the Andean region.

A staple and a protein source

EFSA’s 2019 assessment describes the lima bean as "one of the most widely cultivated pulse crops in temperate and subtropic regions" and, together with cassava, as a staple food "for large numbers of people in some regions of the world". Raw-grain protein measured across five Brazilian varieties ranges from 19.8 to 21.3 g per 100 g.

In nature

Where it grows
Kew records a range of 106 regions, of which 17 are native and 89 introduced. The native distribution Kew gives runs from Mexico to Peru (Belize, Colombia, Costa Rica, Ecuador, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, six regions of Mexico, Nicaragua, Panama, Peru); Bolivia, Brazil and Argentina are not in the native range, and neither is Türkiye.
Harvest season
The seeds are gathered when ripe and the pods have dried; the crop is not widely grown in Türkiye.
Local names
No established local name in Turkish.

Mix-up risk

It should not be confused with its sister species, the common bean (*Phaseolus vulgaris*): the common bean is not cyanogenic and the lima bean is, while the phytohaemagglutinin that causes raw bean poisoning is most concentrated in the common bean, especially red kidney beans. The two species therefore have different reasons for being cooked, but neither is eaten raw.

Preparation

Raw seeds are not eaten. Honesty is required here: no cooking instruction *specific to the lima bean* could be found from any of the bodies examined (EFSA, FAO/WHO JECFA, Codex, FSANZ); Codex’s code of practice for reducing cyanide covers cassava only. The one measured method available comes from a 2022 study of five varieties, and its method has to be given as it stands, because the detail matters: 150 g of grain is soaked in 300 ml of distilled water for 12 hours, taken into a pressure cooker together with the soaking water, cooked for an average of 20 minutes, and the broth then separated from the grains. A distinction is needed here: discarding the soaking water is not part of that study’s method — the soaking water goes into the pot with the grain, and only the cooking broth is discarded. Discarding the soaking water does improve safety, but it is this record’s own precaution, not part of the measured method. The reduction that study measured was obtained under pressure; there is no measurement showing that the same time in an open pot gives the same result. The authors’ own summary puts the reduction at "approximately 2 times", but the variation between varieties in their tables is large: in two varieties the fall was only 5 to 28 per cent, and in two varieties free cyanide rose somewhat after cooking. The authors nonetheless found free cyanide at safe levels in all varieties after cooking. A 2001 study shows that soaking alone is not enough: after 9 hours the reduction was only 30%, whereas autoclaving or toasting brought levels below detection in 20 minutes. The soaking in that study was done, as EFSA reports it, at 2 °C (for 3, 6 and 9 hours); no measurement showing the same result at room temperature could be found for this record. As a precaution, both the soaking water and the cooking water are discarded. No official monograph sets an accepted dose; this is a food record.

What science says

The lima bean is cyanogenic: its principal cyanogenic glycoside is linamarin, alongside lotaustralin. This is recorded at species level in the FAO/WHO joint expert committee’s monograph on cyanogenic glycosides, and EFSA’s 2019 assessment notes that "some cultivars have high levels of CNGs, in particular linamarin". It shares that glycoside with cassava. Its sister species *P. vulgaris* is not cyanogenic — a 2024 study compares the two on exactly this trait. The concentration range is very wide, and that is the most important number on this page: the FAO/WHO 74th report gives lima beans as "not detected to 3120 mg/kg" expressed as HCN; EFSA’s table carries values of 144–167 and 265–530 mg/kg, while an Australian-New Zealand survey found 32 mg/kg in one of three samples. A common claim needs correcting here: the idea that coloured varieties carry more cyanide than white ones comes from Table 2 of the cyanogenic glycosides monograph in WHO Food Additives Series 30; that table rests on Nartey’s 1980 compilation and holds only three samples (Java coloured 3,120; Puerto Rico black 3,000; Burma white 2,100 mg HCN/kg). A 2022 measurement of five Brazilian varieties does not support it: the lowest values were in the pink (6.0 mg/kg) and white (7.8 mg/kg) varieties, and the highest in a striped one (62.8 mg/kg). What decides is variety and genotype, not colour. The lectin side also needs a species distinction: a 1983 survey of legume seeds placed *P. vulgaris*, *P. coccineus* and *P. acutifolius* in a group "highly reactive with all cell types and also highly toxic", while putting the lima bean in a separate and milder group that agglutinated only human and pronase-treated rat erythrocytes. Evidence level "traditional".
