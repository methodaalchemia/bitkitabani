Hyssop

Dracocephalum officinale (L.) Y.P.Chen & B.T.Drew
Evidence: LimitedCaution

According to the review by Atazhanova and colleagues, hyssop was mentioned by Hippocrates (about 460–377 BC). The plant had borne the name Hyssopus officinalis since 1753; a phylogenetic study published in 2022 transferred it to the genus Dracocephalum.

Other names
Its synonym, and still the most common name in the literature, is Hyssopus officinalis L.; English hyssop. A 2022 phylogenetic study showed that the genus Hyssopus is nested within Dracocephalum, and Kew’s plant list accepts the species as Dracocephalum officinale. Almost all medical and scientific publications use the old name. The active constituents of its essential oil are pinocamphone and isopinocamphone. There is no EMA monograph.

In short

What is Hyssop?

Hyssop (Dracocephalum officinale (L.) Y.P.Chen & B.T.Drew) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: Europe, Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Asia and Siberia. Part used: flowering aerial parts (leaf and flower); the essential oil.

What is Hyssop used for?

Traditional use: A 2024 review (Atazhanova et al.) writes that in folk medicine hyssop is used as an infusion, as an expectorant in chronic bronchitis and asthma, and also in chronic gastritis, for wound healing and to reduce sweating. Scientific evidence: limited. There are no clinical studies of hyssop in humans; the 2024 review states plainly that human studies, investigations of adverse reactions and clinical trials are lacking.

Is Hyssop safe?

Safety label: caution. ⚠️ Its essential oil is a convulsant. In the rat studies by Millet and colleagues hyssop oil caused symptomless changes in the brain recording at 0.08 g/kg, seizures above 0.13 g/kg and death above 1.25 g/kg; daily repetition of symptomless doses showed a cumulative effect. The same team reported essential-oil poisonings in humans. People with epilepsy, those with a history of seizures, children, and pregnant and breastfeeding women should not use hyssop oil or its concentrated products. The 2022 review writes that there is very little research in pregnancy and mentions a possible effect on uterine contraction or menstruation. The safety of the herbal tea has not been studied in humans; use should not go beyond short periods and small amounts. According to the LiverTox record hyssop has not been implicated in liver injury. If the essential oil has been swallowed, or if convulsions, tremor or clouded consciousness occur, call 114 (the National Poison Information Centre) or 112.

Traditional use

Europe — a herb to ease the chest

A 2024 review (Atazhanova et al.) writes that in folk medicine hyssop is used as an infusion, as an expectorant in chronic bronchitis and asthma, and also in chronic gastritis, for wound healing and to reduce sweating. Its decoction appears as a wash for inflammation of the eyes and mouth, and its poultice as a compress for bruises and rheumatism. According to the same review the plant has been included in the pharmacopoeias of France, Portugal, Romania, Sweden and Germany. These are traditional uses.

Kitchen and scent — spice and additive

Atazhanova and colleagues report that the plant is also used in cooking and perfumery, and that its green parts are believed to support digestion and appetite. A 2002 toxicology study describes hyssop oil as “an important food additive and herbal medicine”. The LiverTox record of the US National Institutes of Health states that the plant is widely used to relieve the symptoms of gastrointestinal and respiratory tract infections and the common cold.

⚠️ The essential oil — records of poisoning

A 1981 clinical and experimental study (Millet et al.) reported that commercially sold oils of sage, hyssop, thuja and cedar caused poisoning in eight people, the main symptom being tonic-clonic seizures. The same team showed in rats that hyssop oil is a more powerful convulsant than sage oil. For this reason the traditional herbal tea and the distilled essential oil are not regarded as the same product.

In nature

Where it grows
Arid mountain slopes and steppes, on rocky or sandy soils. Its native range runs from southern and central Europe to Türkiye, the Caucasus and Iran, and in North Africa to Morocco and Algeria; it is grown in gardens and was later carried to northern Europe and North America.
Harvest season
Flowers in summer; for medicinal use the green shoots are cut before flowering.
Local names
No established local name in Turkish.

Mix-up risk

It has blue-, pink- and white-flowered forms, and the proportion of pinocamphone in their oils differs (in the review 44.99% in the white-flowered and 20.85% in the blue-flowered form). Hyssopus cuspidatus, used in Central Asia, is a separate species. Whether the “hyssop” of scripture is this plant is not addressed in the sources of this page.

Preparation

The traditional form is an infusion or decoction of the leaves and flowering tops; according to the review by Atazhanova and colleagues the green shoots are cut before flowering for medicinal use. Because there is no EMA, WHO or TİTCK monograph, there is no accepted dose. ⚠️ The essential oil is not taken by mouth; it is on record as causing seizures in both humans and animals, and this page deliberately gives no recipe for internal use of the essential oil. The composition of the essential oil varies greatly with the source, so the amount of pinocamphone in a product cannot be known in advance.

What science says

There are no clinical studies of hyssop in humans; the 2024 review states plainly that human studies, investigations of adverse reactions and clinical trials are lacking. A 2022 review (Sharifi-Rad et al.) reports that the essential oil is active in the test tube against bacteria and Candida yeasts, and that antioxidant and cytotoxic effects have likewise been shown only at the cell level. According to the same review the proportion of pinocamphone and isopinocamphone in the oil differs greatly with origin: isopinocamphone was 22.53–28.74% in Poland, and pinocamphone 19.6% in Türkiye. The data on the nervous system look contradictory but belong to different products: the essential oil causes seizures in rats, whereas in a 2020 rat study an aqueous extract (100 mg/kg) delayed the onset of chemically induced seizures. A 2002 study showed that the pinocamphone isomers and hyssop oil block the GABA-A receptor in the brain and that the seizures in mice were alleviated by diazepam.

Safety

Caution

⚠️ Its essential oil is a convulsant. In the rat studies by Millet and colleagues hyssop oil caused symptomless changes in the brain recording at 0.08 g/kg, seizures above 0.13 g/kg and death above 1.25 g/kg; daily repetition of symptomless doses showed a cumulative effect. The same team reported essential-oil poisonings in humans. People with epilepsy, those with a history of seizures, children, and pregnant and breastfeeding women should not use hyssop oil or its concentrated products. The 2022 review writes that there is very little research in pregnancy and mentions a possible effect on uterine contraction or menstruation. The safety of the herbal tea has not been studied in humans; use should not go beyond short periods and small amounts. According to the LiverTox record hyssop has not been implicated in liver injury. If the essential oil has been swallowed, or if convulsions, tremor or clouded consciousness occur, call 114 (the National Poison Information Centre) or 112.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Epilepsy medicinesThe pinocamphone in the essential oil blocks the GABA-A receptor and may lower the seizure threshold; it works against the protective effect of anti-seizure medicines. Anyone taking anti-seizure medication should keep away from hyssop oil and its concentrated products.
Antidepressants and medicines that depress the nervous systemThe 2022 review (Sharifi-Rad et al.) draws attention to possible herb-drug interactions with these drug groups; there is no interaction study in humans.
Other essential oils that lower the seizure threshold (sage, thuja, camphor)Millet and colleagues showed that the thujone and camphor in these oils are also convulsant; combined use may increase the risk.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Hyssop.
