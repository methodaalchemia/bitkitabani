Peach

Prunus persica (L.) Batsch
Evidence: TraditionalGenerally safe

The persica in the species name is Latin for "belonging to Persia". Yet archaeological evidence shows that the peach began to be domesticated in China at least 7,500 years ago; similar stones from 6,700–6,400 years ago have also been found in Japan (*PLoS One*, 2014).

Other names
Synonym Amygdalus persica; Turkish şeftali. The kernel is used as a crude drug in Chinese medicine under the name taoren (桃仁) and in Japanese Kampo medicine as Persicae Semen. The fruit is food; the kernel carries amygdalin, which releases cyanide.

In short

What is Peach?

Peach (Prunus persica (L.) Batsch) is a plant of the Rosaceae (rose family) family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia, Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the fruit (food); the kernel (a crude drug in chinese and japanese traditional medicine).

What is Peach used for?

Traditional use: The peach originated in China. Scientific evidence: traditional. The sources of this page contain no human study showing that peach fruit treats a disease; the fruit is a food.

Is Peach safe?

Safety label: generally safe. The fruit is safe as food in ordinary amounts. There are two warnings. ⚠️ The kernel is not eaten: it carries amygdalin, and hydrogen cyanide is released when it is taken by mouth. The inside of a cracked stone is not left where a child can reach it. If any symptom appears after kernels have been eaten, 114 (the National Poison Information Centre in Türkiye) or 112 is called. Allergy: the peach is a food allergen that can cause severe reactions, particularly in the Mediterranean region; if swelling of the lips and throat or shortness of breath occurs, 112 is called. The sources of this page contain no restriction on eating the fruit as food in pregnancy and breastfeeding; the kernel and products made from it are not used in these periods either.

Traditional use

China — domestication

The peach originated in China. An archaeological study published in *PLoS One* in 2014 showed that peach stones found in Zhejiang Province take its use back to about 8,000 years before the present. The authors propose that the lower Yangzi valley was the region of early selection and domestication and that the process began at least 7,500 years ago. The first stones most similar to today’s cultivated forms belong to the Liangzhu culture (about 5,300–4,300 years ago).

China and Japan — taoren, the kernel

The kernel is known in Chinese medicine as taoren and is used traditionally in the pattern called "blood stasis", especially in gynaecological conditions and injuries (*Journal of Traditional Chinese Medicine*, 2013). In Japan it enters many Kampo formulas; a 2022 study writes that although the applications of peach and apricot kernels are quite different, it is difficult to tell them apart by conventional methods. This use is carried out under a practitioner’s supervision with the processed drug; it is not the same thing as eating kernels at home.

The Mediterranean — food and allergy

The peach is a common summer fruit in Mediterranean countries. In the same region it is also an important source of food allergy: a 2021 review (*Current Allergy and Asthma Reports*) writes that the lipid transfer protein in peach called Pru p 3 is regarded as the prototypic marker of this type of allergy and that lipid transfer proteins are major allergens in the Mediterranean area that can frequently cause severe reactions.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew’s Plants of the World Online record its native range is north-central China and the plant is a cultigen; it grows as a shrub or tree, primarily in the temperate biome. Türkiye is listed among the areas where it has been introduced.
Harvest season
It is a fruit tree. The sources this page relies on give no flowering or harvest months.
Local names
The name şeftali is in general use; no other local name appears in the sources of this page.

Mix-up risk

Peach kernel and apricot kernel (Armeniacae Semen) look very much alike; the 2022 study reports that it is difficult to tell the two apart by conventional methods. Both carry amygdalin. It should not be confused with sweet almond (*Prunus dulcis*).

Preparation

The fruit is eaten fresh or dried; there is no accepted dose for medicinal use. No EU, WHO or Turkish (TİTCK) monograph was found among the sources this page relies on. For the kernel this page gives no recipe and no dose: according to the 2013 review the toxicity of taoren appears when it is used without proper processing or in overdose, and safe use depends on the processed drug being given at the dose laid down in regulations and on a practitioner’s decision. The kernel is not eaten at home.

What science says

The sources of this page contain no human study showing that peach fruit treats a disease; the fruit is a food. Research concentrates more on the amygdalin in the kernel. A 2020 review (*Journal of Ethnopharmacology*) examined 110 cell and animal studies on amygdalin; anti-tumour, anti-fibrotic and anti-inflammatory effects were reported, but these are not evidence of efficacy in humans. According to the same review the toxicity of amygdalin comes from its breakdown, after being taken by mouth, into benzaldehyde and hydrogen cyanide, and toxicity by the oral route is far higher than by the intravenous route. The 2013 review on taoren surveyed ancient and modern sources; it found that the toxic component is clearly known and that problems arise from use without differential diagnosis or in overdose. A 1984 study measured cyanide in apricot, peach and apple seeds.

Safety

Generally safe

The fruit is safe as food in ordinary amounts. There are two warnings. ⚠️ The kernel is not eaten: it carries amygdalin, and hydrogen cyanide is released when it is taken by mouth. The inside of a cracked stone is not left where a child can reach it. If any symptom appears after kernels have been eaten, 114 (the National Poison Information Centre in Türkiye) or 112 is called. Allergy: the peach is a food allergen that can cause severe reactions, particularly in the Mediterranean region; if swelling of the lips and throat or shortness of breath occurs, 112 is called. The sources of this page contain no restriction on eating the fruit as food in pregnancy and breastfeeding; the kernel and products made from it are not used in these periods either.
