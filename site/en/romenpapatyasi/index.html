Roman chamomile

Chamaemelum nobile (L.) All.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

This is not the plant Turkish speakers mean by "papatya". The chamomile in this catalog is Matricaria chamomilla; Roman chamomile belongs to a different genus, comes from western Europe, and has no wild distribution in Turkey. Their monographs differ too: Matricaria’s indications include skin and oral-mucosa use, while Roman chamomile’s cover digestion alone. Yet both go by "chamomile" in the literature — so one common name covering two species causes trouble not just in the market but in reading scientific sources.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the names "Romen papatyası, Roman papatyası"; Anthemis nobilis L. and Chamomilla nobilis Godr. are synonyms. In English: Roman chamomile. ⚠️ The monograph warns in one sentence: it must not be confused with Matricaria chamomilla L. or with other Anthemis species.

In short

What is Roman chamomile?

Roman chamomile (Chamaemelum nobile (L.) All.) is a plant of the Asteraceae (daisy family) family. Traditions that use it: Europe. Part used: the flower head (capitulum).

What is Roman chamomile used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": symptomatic treatment of mild spasmodic gastro-intestinal complaints including bloating and flatulence. Scientific evidence: limited. There is no clinical trial of Roman chamomile in the monograph indication (digestion).

Is Roman chamomile safe?

Safety label: caution. The interaction section of the Turkish monograph says "must not be used together with" for three groups of medicines (detailed below); that is stricter wording than many plants in this catalog carry, and it means anyone on regular medication should not use this plant without asking their doctor. Hypersensitivity can occur to the active substance and to the daisy family — sunflower, chrysanthemum, mugwort and marigold are in the same family. Allergic reactions such as itching and redness have been reported with external use. If symptoms last longer than two weeks, worsen, or adverse effects appear, consult a doctor or pharmacist. Use under 12 has not been established; not recommended in pregnancy or lactation. Extracts may contain ethanol.

Traditional use

Europe — one indication

The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": symptomatic treatment of mild spasmodic gastro-intestinal complaints including bloating and flatulence. Unlike the Matricaria chamomile monograph, there is no skin or oral-mucosa use here.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the flower head (capitulum)". Its interaction section is among the strictest of any plant in this catalog: it says the plant must not be used together with three groups of medicines. It also reports allergic reactions such as itching and redness with external use, and asks that the plant not be confused with Matricaria chamomilla.

History of use in Europe

Roman chamomile is native to western Europe and is known from the scented "chamomile lawns" planted instead of grass in England. It has no wild distribution in Turkey and arrives as a product — which is why the plant meant by "papatya" in Anatolia is almost never this one.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to western Europe, growing in sandy, tread-tolerant turf and planted in gardens as a scented ground cover. No wild distribution in Turkey.
Harvest season
It flowers from June to September; the flower heads are gathered in full bloom and dried.
Local names
Romen papatyası, Roman papatyası — both appear in the Turkish monograph.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ The monograph’s own warning: it must not be confused with Matricaria chamomilla L. or other Anthemis species. All three look like daisies and all three are in the same family, but their monographs and indications are separate. Check the Latin name when buying from a pharmacy, and the label when buying tea.

Preparation

The monograph describes oral preparations of the flower head: as a herbal tea, 1–4 g of the drug in 100–150 ml of boiling water, three times daily between meals; as a liquid extract, 1–4 ml three times daily. Extracts containing ethanol must carry the appropriate labelling. If symptoms persist longer than two weeks, consult a doctor or pharmacist. Use under 12 has not been established.

What science says

There is no clinical trial of Roman chamomile in the monograph indication (digestion). In a quite different field, however, there is a single-ingredient, placebo-controlled trial: a double-blind randomised study published in 2016 in the Journal of the European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology divided 60 patients with oral lichen planus into two groups, giving one a 2% Chamaemelum nobile gel and the other a placebo; at four weeks it reported significant improvement in pain, burning, itching and clinical scores. One trial, 60 people, one centre — hence an evidence level of Limited. A Bayesian network meta-analysis published in 2025 in BMC Oral Health (64 randomised trials, 2,839 participants, 24 interventions) puts corticosteroids and immunosuppressants first for pain control in oral lichen planus, and counts chamomile among the effective plant-based options with a favourable safety profile for early-to-mid stage symptoms. There is a reading trap here: in the literature "chamomile" is used for both Matricaria chamomilla and Chamaemelum nobile, so which species a review actually measured has to be checked trial by trial — the monograph’s "must not be confused" warning applies not only on the shelf but in reading the sources.

Safety

Caution

The interaction section of the Turkish monograph says "must not be used together with" for three groups of medicines (detailed below); that is stricter wording than many plants in this catalog carry, and it means anyone on regular medication should not use this plant without asking their doctor. Hypersensitivity can occur to the active substance and to the daisy family — sunflower, chrysanthemum, mugwort and marigold are in the same family. Allergic reactions such as itching and redness have been reported with external use. If symptoms last longer than two weeks, worsen, or adverse effects appear, consult a doctor or pharmacist. Use under 12 has not been established; not recommended in pregnancy or lactation. Extracts may contain ethanol.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Acetylsalicylic acid, warfarin and other anticoagulantsThe Turkish monograph says it must not be used together with these medicines. Anyone on a blood thinner or taking aspirin regularly should not use this plant.
Diazepam and other benzodiazepinesThe monograph states it may increase the effect of these medicines and should not be used together with them. That means sedation deepening unexpectedly, with drowsiness and loss of attention.
Medicines metabolised by CYP3A4The monograph states that because of certain compounds it contains, the plant may inhibit the CYP3A4 enzyme, and that it should not be used together with medicines metabolised by that enzyme. CYP3A4 processes a large share of the medicines in use, so the warning is broad in scope, and asking a pharmacist who knows the medication list is the most practical route.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing in its interaction section. That two agencies use such different wording for the same plant shows interaction sections are filled from different sources agency by agency; taking the more protective one as the guide is the right approach.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Roman chamomile.
