Iceland moss

Cetraria islandica (L.) Ach.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

Its English name is "Iceland moss" — but it is neither a moss nor a plant. A lichen is an organism formed by a fungus living together with an alga (or a cyanobacterium): the fungus provides structure and water, the alga photosynthesises. That is why its page carries no botanical record; Plants of the World Online is a list of vascular plants, and lichens are not in it. The word "herbal" on a product label does not mean what is inside is a plant in the botanical sense.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the name "İzlanda likeni"; the synonyms are Physcia islandica and Lichen islandicus. ⚠️ The "moss" in the English name is misleading: this is neither a moss nor a plant but a lichen — a symbiosis of a fungus and an alga. Its page carries no POWO record, because POWO is a checklist of vascular plants.

In short

What is Iceland moss?

Iceland moss (Cetraria islandica (L.) Ach.) is a plant of the Parmeliaceae family. Traditions that use it: Europe. Part used: the thallus.

What is Iceland moss used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places both indications under "traditional use": (1) as a demulcent for the symptomatic treatment of oral or pharyngeal irritation and the associated dry cough; (2) temporary loss of appetite. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no randomised clinical trial of Iceland moss in humans.

Is Iceland moss safe?

Safety label: caution. People with a gastric or intestinal ulcer should not use it; the Turkish monograph notes it can irritate the digestive system. Prolonged use at high doses is not safe, because the lichen can be contaminated with lead — lichens accumulate substances from the air, and this is an unusual warning in this catalog whose source lies directly in the organism’s biology. Hypersensitivity and allergic reactions to the drug can develop; abdominal pain and nausea have been reported. During oral use, see a doctor if dyspnoea, fever or purulent sputum occur — these can mean the cough belongs to something more serious. The age limit varies by preparation: tea 12, soft extract 6, tincture 18. Not to be used in pregnancy or lactation. If symptoms last more than a week, consult a doctor or pharmacist.

Traditional use

Europe — two indications

The EMA monograph places both indications under "traditional use": (1) as a demulcent for the symptomatic treatment of oral or pharyngeal irritation and the associated dry cough; (2) temporary loss of appetite. Both come from the same chemistry: the lichen’s mucilage soothes, its bitter compounds open the appetite.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the whole or cut and dried thallus". It sets different age limits by preparation: tea 12 years, soft extract 6 years, tincture 18 years. It adds two warnings: people with a gastric or intestinal ulcer should not use it, and prolonged use at high doses is not safe because of possible lead contamination.

Northern Europe

Iceland moss grows on acidic, open ground in Scandinavia, Iceland and the northern mountains. It is known to have been used for flour in times of famine — its high mucilage content is the source of both the food value and the demulcent effect. It has no wild distribution in Turkey.

In nature

Where it grows
Acidic, open, cool ground in Scandinavia, Iceland, the Alps and across the northern hemisphere. No wild distribution in Turkey; imported as a product.
Harvest season
Gathered year-round, most often harvested and dried in summer.
Local names
İzlanda likeni — the Turkish monograph gives one Turkish name.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ The real confusion is of category: the word "moss" in the name misleads; this is a lichen. Other lichens (Usnea species, for instance) and true mosses are also sold under similar names; the EU monograph covers Cetraria islandica only. Because lichens accumulate substances from the air, the pollution of the place they were gathered matters — which is where the monograph’s lead warning comes from.

Preparation

The monograph describes oral and oromucosal preparations of the thallus, with the age limit varying by form (tea 12, soft extract 6, tincture 18). Take it half an hour to an hour apart from other medicines, because it can delay their absorption. If symptoms last more than a week, consult a doctor or pharmacist.

What science says

There is no randomised clinical trial of Iceland moss in humans. All the recent literature is at laboratory level: activity against influenza and other viruses in cell culture, antioxidant and cytotoxicity measurements, anti-ageing measurements for cosmetics. These show what is in the lichen; they do not show whether it relieves throat irritation or loss of appetite in a patient. The EU monograph rests both indications on long use. The mechanism the monograph gives is plausible and modest: the mucilage acts as a demulcent — it coats the irritated surface; it does not treat an infection. That same mucilage is also the source of the monograph’s warning that it may delay drug absorption. The evidence level is Traditional.

Safety

Caution

People with a gastric or intestinal ulcer should not use it; the Turkish monograph notes it can irritate the digestive system. Prolonged use at high doses is not safe, because the lichen can be contaminated with lead — lichens accumulate substances from the air, and this is an unusual warning in this catalog whose source lies directly in the organism’s biology. Hypersensitivity and allergic reactions to the drug can develop; abdominal pain and nausea have been reported. During oral use, see a doctor if dyspnoea, fever or purulent sputum occur — these can mean the cough belongs to something more serious. Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
All oral medicines (delayed absorption)Both the EU and the Turkish monograph state this and give the practical advice: the lichen may delay the absorption of medicines taken with it, so as a precaution take it half an hour to an hour before or after other medicines. This is a general effect of mucilaginous drugs; it applies to ribwort plantain and marshmallow in this catalog too.
Ulcer and acid-suppressing medicinesNot a reported interaction, but the monograph says the lichen is not for people with an ulcer. Anyone taking medication for one should ask their doctor about this product.
No other interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing beyond the delayed absorption.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Iceland moss.
