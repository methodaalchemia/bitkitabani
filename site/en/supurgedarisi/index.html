Sorghum

Sorghum bicolor (L.) Moench
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

Every broom that touches the floor is really the flowering head of a cereal: both the Turkish name of sorghum and its shape come from that use.

Other names
In Turkish süpürge darısı (broom millet), koca darı, akdarı; English sorghum, broomcorn, great millet; Arabic "dhura". The form used for making brooms is a variety of this species. Neither the European Medicines Agency nor the Turkish agency has a monograph for this species; it is regulated as a cereal, not as a herbal medicine.

In short

What is Sorghum?

Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor (L.) Moench) is a plant of the Poaceae (grass family) family. Traditions that use it: Africa, Anatolia and the Mediterranean. Part used: grain (food); the flowering panicle for brooms.

What is Sorghum used for?

Traditional use: A review published in the Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry in 2023 writes that sorghum is among the world’s top five cereals by production and planted area, and that its tolerance of drought and saline soil lets it be grown where other cereals fail. Scientific evidence: traditional. In the sources relied on here there is no human study testing this plant in any condition; the sorghum literature is an agricultural and breeding literature.

Is Sorghum safe?

Safety label: caution. The grain is safe and is a staple food across much of the world. The real warning concerns the green parts of the plant: Sorghum bicolor is cyanogenic. Its leaves contain dhurrin, which releases hydrogen cyanide when broken down. The amount rises in young shoots and in regrowth after drought or frost; this is why fatal poisonings occur in livestock. Tea or juice is not made from the leaves and young shoots. Processing and cooking the grain removes the problem. Because there is no monograph there is no official list of contraindications, and no separate assessment for pregnancy, lactation or drug interactions — eating it as food is not the subject of these warnings.

Traditional use

Africa and Asia — a staple cereal

A review published in the Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry in 2023 writes that sorghum is among the world’s top five cereals by production and planted area, and that its tolerance of drought and saline soil lets it be grown where other cereals fail. The same review notes that the plant is divided into grain, energy and silage sorghum according to the direction of its domestication.

Anatolia — the broom itself

The Turkish name comes straight from the use: the stalks of the panicle, dried and stiffened, are bound into brooms. The form grown for this is a different variety from grain sorghum, selected for a long and flexible panicle.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew’s records its range covers 243 regions — it comes from Africa and is now sown in every hot part of the world. Its tolerance of drought and saline soil lets it grow where other cereals cannot.
Harvest season
In Turkey it is sown as a summer crop and the panicles ripen at the end of summer; the sources relied on here give no exact harvest calendar for Turkey.
Local names
Süpürge darısı, koca darı, akdarı.

Mix-up risk

The Turkish word darı covers more than one plant: proso millet (*Panicum miliaceum*) and foxtail millet (*Setaria italica*) are separate species. This record is for *Sorghum bicolor* only.

Preparation

No official monograph sets an accepted dose; sorghum is a cereal. The grain is milled into flour, cooked as porridge and bread, and fermented into drinks in Africa. The sources relied on here give no medicinal preparation or measure.

What science says

In the sources relied on here there is no human study testing this plant in any condition; the sorghum literature is an agricultural and breeding literature. The 2023 review deals with the plant’s agronomic traits and the genes that regulate them. A study published in Genes in 2022 confirms that the plant is cyanogenic — its leaves carry a compound called dhurrin that can release hydrogen cyanide; that study also screened wild Sorghum species.

Safety

Caution

The grain is safe and is a staple food across much of the world. The real warning concerns the green parts of the plant: Sorghum bicolor is cyanogenic. Its leaves contain dhurrin, which releases hydrogen cyanide when broken down. The amount rises in young shoots and in regrowth after drought or frost; this is why fatal poisonings occur in livestock. Tea or juice is not made from the leaves and young shoots. Processing and cooking the grain removes the problem. Because there is no monograph there is no official list of contraindications, and no separate assessment for pregnancy, lactation or drug interactions — eating it as food is not the subject of these warnings.
