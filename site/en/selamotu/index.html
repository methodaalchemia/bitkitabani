Lovage

Levisticum officinale W.D.J. Koch
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

The same plant’s leaf has been a soup herb in Europe for centuries while its root is a diuretic drug. The English name lovage looks as though it has something to do with love; it does not — it is a French-mediated corruption of the Latin ligusticum, "Ligurian". Even the plant’s name is the product of a confusion.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the names "selam otu, yaban kerevizi" (wild celery); Ligusticum levisticum L. and Hipposelinum levisticum (L.) Britton & Rose are synonyms. In English: lovage. A plant with the scent of celery and larger than celery; its leaf is a spice in European cooking, its root the drug in the monograph.

In short

What is Lovage?

Lovage (Levisticum officinale W.D.J. Koch) is a plant of the Apiaceae (carrot family) family. Traditions that use it: Europe, Asia and Siberia. Part used: the root; the essential oil.

What is Lovage used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": to increase the amount of urine to achieve flushing of the urinary tract, as an adjuvant in minor urinary complaints. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no single-ingredient clinical trial of lovage in humans.

Is Lovage safe?

Safety label: caution. See a doctor if fever, painful urination, spasms or blood in the urine appear — these are not symptoms to manage with a flushing tea. Not for people with oedema or blood pressure problems (Turkish monograph, citing sodium retention). Not recommended in kidney inflammation, impaired kidney function or febrile illness. Furanocoumarins may increase photosensitivity: avoid prolonged sun exposure during use; this is a common trait in the carrot family. Not for anyone allergic to the carrot family or to anethole. Not longer than 2–4 weeks; not recommended under 18, in pregnancy or lactation.

Traditional use

Europe — one indication

The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": to increase the amount of urine to achieve flushing of the urinary tract, as an adjuvant in minor urinary complaints. Contraindicated in hypersensitivity to the carrot family and to anethole; not recommended under 18; 2–4 weeks’ use.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph lists the root and the essential oil and adds limits absent from the EU one: not to be used in fluid retention due to oedema or, because it raises sodium levels, by people with blood pressure problems; not recommended in febrile illness, kidney inflammation, impaired kidney function or blood in the urine; it may increase photosensitivity because of its furanocoumarins. Not to be used longer than four weeks.

European cooking

Lovage has been a garden plant in central and southern Europe for centuries; its leaf gives soups and meat dishes a celery flavour, and its German name "Maggikraut" (soup herb) comes from that use. It is thought to originate in Iran and Afghanistan; it has no wild distribution in Turkey and is rare even as a garden plant.

In nature

Where it grows
Considered native to Iran and Afghanistan; widespread as a garden plant in Europe. No wild distribution in Turkey.
Harvest season
Yellow-green umbels in early summer; the root is lifted in autumn of the second or third year.
Local names
Selam otu, yaban kerevizi — both appear in the Turkish monograph.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ Roots of the carrot family should not be gathered from the wild: the family contains lethal species such as hemlock, and the roots look alike. The name "yaban kerevizi" is used for other plants as well.

Preparation

For adults the EU monograph gives a single dose: 2–3 g of the comminuted root infused in 150 ml of boiling water, twice daily (4–6 g daily). Because the indication is flushing of the urinary tract, drinking plenty of fluid is part of the treatment. Not to be used longer than 2–4 weeks. If symptoms persist, consult a doctor or pharmacist. Not recommended under 18.

What science says

There is no single-ingredient clinical trial of lovage in humans. The only clinical publication visible on PubMed is a 2026 case series on a homeopathic Arnica/Levisticum mixture for musculoskeletal symptoms in breast-cancer patients — neither a single ingredient, nor randomised, nor the monograph indication. The EU monograph rests the indication on long use; a large part of the effect belongs to the water drunk for flushing. The evidence level is Traditional.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Antihypertensives and diureticsThe Turkish monograph states it interacts with both groups; the reason matches the monograph’s warnings on sodium and fluid balance: a diuretic tea stacks on a diuretic drug, and a sodium-retaining plant works against a blood-pressure drug.
AnticoagulantsOn the Turkish list, sourced from a consumer medicines database. It is a warning often repeated for coumarin-bearing members of the carrot family; anyone on a blood thinner should tell their doctor.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing in its interaction section.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Lovage.
