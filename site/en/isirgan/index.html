Stinging nettle

Urtica dioica
Evidence: LimitedGenerally safe

Striking the skin with nettles (urtication) has been known since ancient Rome. The same practice is used in Anatolia and the Andes, independently of each other.

Other names
Ortiga (Spanish), ısırgan (Turkish)
Part used
Leaves, root, seeds
Region
Anatolia and the Mediterranean
Andes
Use
Healing, Food, Ritual and shamanic
In short

What is Stinging nettle?

Stinging nettle (Urtica dioica) is a plant of the Urticaceae family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Andes. Part used: leaves, root, seeds.

What is Stinging nettle used for?

Traditional use: In spring the young leaves go into soups, pastries and sautés. Scientific evidence: limited. The EMA recognises the leaf for joint pain and as a diuretic, and the root for urinary complaints linked to benign prostate enlargement, as traditional uses.

Is Stinging nettle safe?

Safety label: generally safe. The fresh plant causes itching and welts. Because it is a diuretic, people taking blood pressure or diabetes medicines should be careful.

Traditional use

Anatolia

In spring the young leaves go into soups, pastries and sautés. In folk medicine it is used to “build blood” and for rheumatism; aching joints are struck with a fresh stem.

Andes

In Andean folk healing, ortiga is lightly struck on the body during a limpia. It is believed to open the circulation and draw out susto, the illness caused by fright.

In nature

Where it grows
Damp, nitrogen-rich soil all over Türkiye, by barns, hedges and streams. In the Andes, in damp valleys.
Harvest season
Young leaves March–May, seeds in late summer, roots in autumn.
Local names
ısırgan, ısırgan otu, dalağan, gezgen, cırtlağan

Mix-up risk

Dead-nettles (Lamium) look similar but do not sting and have showy flowers. Nettle leaves are hairy and sting when touched.

Preparation

Leaves lose their sting when blanched. Dried leaves are brewed as tea.

What science says

The EMA recognises the leaf for joint pain and as a diuretic, and the root for urinary complaints linked to benign prostate enlargement, as traditional uses. Clinical trials of the root show moderate results.

How strong is the evidence?

Claims often repeated online about Stinging nettle, and what science says today.

Eases urinary complaints from prostate enlargement (root)Limited evidenceModerate benefit in some controlled trials; recognised by the EMA as traditional. Prostate cancer must first be ruled out by a doctor.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up leaf and root: the root is studied for the prostate, the leaf for joints and urination.
  • Using it alone as a diuretic for swelling caused by heart or kidney failure.
  • Picking without gloves; the leaves lose their sting when blanched or dried.

Frequently asked

What is nettle tea good for?

The EMA recognises the leaf tea for mild joint pain and as an aid to flushing the urinary tract, as traditional uses. Most other claims are unproven.

Does nettle lower blood pressure?

Shown in some animal studies; there is not enough human data. People on blood pressure medicine should ask their doctor.

Can I drink it in pregnancy?

Not recommended; effects on the uterus have been reported.

Safety

Generally safe

The fresh plant causes itching and welts. Because it is a diuretic, people taking blood pressure or diabetes medicines should be careful.
