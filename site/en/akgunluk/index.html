Frankincense

Boswellia sacra, B. serrata
Evidence: LimitedGenerally safe

Oman’s frankincense trees and its ancient port and caravan towns are on the UNESCO World Heritage List as the “Land of Frankincense”. The trees can grow on bare rock with almost no soil.

Other names
Olibanum, boswellia, Indian frankincense (B. serrata), kündür
Part used
Resin (gum)
Region
Asia and Siberia
Use
Incense and protection, Ritual and shamanic, Healing
In short

What is Frankincense?

Frankincense (Boswellia sacra) is a plant of the Burseraceae family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia. Part used: resin (gum).

What is Frankincense used for?

Traditional use: For thousands of years frankincense from southern Arabia (today’s Oman and Yemen) was carried by caravan to the Mediterranean and burned as incense in Egyptian temples, in Jerusalem and in Rome. Scientific evidence: limited. According to NCCIH there is not enough high-quality evidence to determine whether frankincense is useful for any health condition.

Is Frankincense safe?

Safety label: generally safe. According to NCCIH oral use is likely safe; doses up to 1,000 mg a day have been used for up to 6 months in studies. The safety of medicinal doses in pregnancy and breastfeeding is unknown. People taking asthma medicines or receiving cancer treatment should ask their doctor first. Burning incense for long periods indoors can irritate the airways.

Traditional use

Arabia and the eastern Mediterranean

For thousands of years frankincense from southern Arabia (today’s Oman and Yemen) was carried by caravan to the Mediterranean and burned as incense in Egyptian temples, in Jerusalem and in Rome. In the Gospel it is one of the gifts the Magi brought to Jesus; churches still burn it today.

Ottoman lands and India

In Ottoman times it was burned in homes and mosques as “kündür” and “buhur” and added to pastes. In India, B. serrata resin (salai guggul) is used in Ayurveda for joint pain.

In nature

Where it grows
B. sacra on dry rocky slopes in Oman, Yemen and Somalia; B. serrata in the dry forests of India. Overtapping threatens some species.
Harvest season
Resin oozes from cuts made in the trunk in the hot months; the tears are collected after drying for a few weeks.
Local names
akgünlük, günlük, buhur, kündür

Mix-up risk

In Anatolia the name “günlük” is also given to the oriental sweetgum (Liquidambar orientalis) and its resin; they are different plants. Myrrh (Commiphora myrrha) is another resin from the same family.

Preparation

As incense, grains of resin are placed on embers or incense charcoal. For oral use, ready products with a standardised extract are preferable.

What science says

According to NCCIH there is not enough high-quality evidence to determine whether frankincense is useful for any health condition. A meta-analysis (2020) of 7 controlled trials (545 patients) in knee osteoarthritis reported that extracts may reduce pain and stiffness and improve function, but larger, better studies are needed. Small asthma studies are not rigorous enough.

Safety

Generally safe

According to NCCIH oral use is likely safe; doses up to 1,000 mg a day have been used for up to 6 months in studies. The safety of medicinal doses in pregnancy and breastfeeding is unknown. People taking asthma medicines or receiving cancer treatment should ask their doctor first. Burning incense for long periods indoors can irritate the airways.
