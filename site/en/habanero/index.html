BT-305 · Solanaceae (nightshade family)

Habanero

Capsicum chinense Jacq.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

Its name means "Chinese pepper", yet the plant has never seen China: it was domesticated in the Amazon basin and recorded in the eighteenth century with the wrong geography.

Other names
Known in Turkish as habanero; the variety of the same species grown in India is called bhut jolokia or naga chilli. English habanero, Scotch bonnet, ghost pepper. The species name "chinense" misleads: the plant comes not from China but from the Amazon basin. The European Medicines Agency’s Capsici fructus monograph does not belong to this species: it covers Capsicum annuum var. minimum and small-fruited varieties of Capsicum frutescens, and C. chinense does not appear in its text. The Turkish agency has no monograph for this species either.

What is Habanero?

Traditional use

Tropical America — spice and folk medicine

A review in the International Journal of Molecular Sciences states that C. chinense is one of the five domesticated species of Capsicum and records the fruits being used as food additives for toothache, parasitic infections, coughs, wound healing, sore throat and rheumatism. The same review notes the plant is rich in provitamin A, vitamins E and C, carotenoids and capsaicinoids.

North-east India — the naga chilli

A review in the Journal of Ethnopharmacology writes that the "naga chilli" or "bhut jolokia" variety of this same species, indigenous to north-east India, has long been used by the region’s peoples for various ailments, and describes it as the hottest chilli variety in the world. The review also notes that knowledge of the plant’s biology and cultivation is scattered and scanty.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew’s records its range covers 21 regions: the warm, humid parts of the Amazon basin, the Caribbean and Central America. It tolerates neither drought nor cold.
Harvest season
It does not grow wild in Turkey; it is raised in greenhouses and pots, with fruit ripening in late summer and autumn.
Local names
No established Turkish regional name; it appears as habanero, naga biberi, bhut jolokia.

Mix-up risk

Chilli pepper (C. annuum var. minimum), small-fruited C. frutescens varieties and rocoto (C. pubescens) are separate species. A monograph and its restrictions do not belong to a relative: the European Medicines Agency’s pepper monograph was written for C. annuum var. minimum and C. frutescens, not for this species.

Preparation

No official monograph sets an accepted dose for this species; the habanero is a spice. The measures used for topical capsaicin products in muscle pain come from the monograph of other Capsicum species and do not apply to this record. The sources relied on here describe no home preparation; because the pungency is very high, it is used in very small amounts in the kitchen as well.

What science says

A study in Current Drug Delivery encapsulated a semi-purified capsaicinoid extract of bhut jolokia in nanolipid vesicles; efficacy was tested in rats in carrageenan- and formaldehyde-induced inflammation and Freund’s adjuvant arthritis models, while in humans a phase I study measured only the acceptability of the formulation. What that study shows in humans is therefore not an analgesic effect but that the product can be used without being abandoned because of irritation. Reviews list possible effects of capsaicinoids on pain, body-temperature regulation, weight and cancer, but present them as areas warranting research. The sources relied on here contain no randomised controlled trial measuring efficacy of this species in any condition.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Other topical products on the same area (warming balms, analgesic gels)Capsaicin may increase irritation, so applying them to the same spot at the same time is best avoided.
Heat, showers and sunBurning on the treated area intensifies with heat.
ACE inhibitorsCough has been reported with topical capsaicin use, and cough is already common with these medicines.
