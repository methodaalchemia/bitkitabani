Shepherd’s purse

Capsella bursa-pastoris (L.) Medik.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

The plant’s name gives the same picture in four languages: "çoban çantası" in Turkish, shepherd’s purse in English, Hirtentäschel in German, bursa-pastoris in Latin — all meaning the shepherd’s pouch. The comparison comes not from the flower but from the fruit: small flat triangular-heart-shaped pouches up the stem. A name derived from the same detail in this many languages suggests the detail really does help you recognise the plant — and the thing to look at is not the flower but the fruit.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the names "çoban çantası, çıngıldaklı ot"; Thlaspi bursa-pastoris L. is a synonym. The Turkish and English names say the same thing: the small triangular fruits were likened to the pouch a shepherd wears at the belt.

In short

What is Shepherd’s purse?

Shepherd’s purse (Capsella bursa-pastoris (L.) Medik.) is a plant of the Brassicaceae (cabbage family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the aerial parts.

What is Shepherd’s purse used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": reduction of heavy menstrual bleeding in women with regular menstrual cycles, after serious conditions have been excluded by a medical doctor. Scientific evidence: limited. This plant has something many records in this catalog do not: a randomised clinical trial testing the monograph’s indication directly.

Is Shepherd’s purse safe?

Safety label: caution. Hypersensitivity to the active constituents can occur. Not recommended under 18, and the EU monograph gives the reason: heavy bleeding at that age is a condition requiring medical advice. Not recommended in pregnancy or lactation for lack of data. If symptoms worsen during use, or if adverse effects appear, consult a doctor. No undesirable effects are reported in the monographs, but that does not mean there are none: the EU monograph’s wording is "none known". The most important warning is not about the plant but about the condition attached to the indication: the monograph restricts use to women with regular cycles, after serious conditions have been excluded. Irregular or inter-cycle bleeding should not be managed with a herbal product; it should be shown to a doctor. Read the label on extracts containing ethanol.

Traditional use

Europe — reducing menstrual bleeding

The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": reduction of heavy menstrual bleeding in women with regular menstrual cycles, after serious conditions have been excluded by a medical doctor. Both conditions are in the monograph’s own sentence and both matter: irregular bleeding can signal something else.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the aerial parts". Its interaction section has a single, candid sentence: "there is insufficient data". Not recommended under 18.

Anatolia — in the kitchen

Shepherd’s purse is among the wild greens gathered and eaten in spring in Turkey, common along field margins, roadsides and in gardens. Being a brassica, its leaves are slightly bitter and pungent; they are boiled or used in pastry. That food use is independent of the monograph’s indication.

In nature

Where it grows
Spread through almost every temperate region of the world; field margins, roadsides, gardens and waste ground. Very common in Turkey.
Harvest season
It flowers very early: in mild places it can be found in flower from late winter to autumn. The aerial parts are gathered in flower and fruit; for eating, the young leaves are gathered in spring.
Local names
Çoban çantası, çıngıldaklı ot — both appear in the Turkish monograph. "Çıngıldaklı", roughly "the rattling one", comes from the sound the dry fruits make when shaken.

Mix-up risk

It is confused with other small white-flowered plants of the same family; the distinguishing feature is the fruit — shepherd’s purse pouches are flat and triangular-heart-shaped, while its relatives’ fruits are round or long. When gathering to eat, the safest route is to identify a plant that has set fruit and then gather the young ones.

Preparation

The monograph describes oral preparations of the aerial parts: herbal tea, infusion, decoction or macerate. Use is periodic, during menstruation. Extracts containing ethanol must carry the appropriate labelling. If symptoms worsen, consult a doctor. Not recommended under 18.

What science says

This plant has something many records in this catalog do not: a randomised clinical trial testing the monograph’s indication directly. In a triple-blind randomised trial published in 2018 in the Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine, 84 women with heavy menstrual bleeding were divided into two groups. The design is an add-on: both groups took mefenamic acid (two 500 mg capsules every eight hours); the experimental group additionally took two shepherd’s purse capsules every twelve hours, the control group placebo instead. Blood loss was measured with a pictorial blood loss assessment chart (PBLAC), over two consecutive cycles, from the first day of menstruation for up to seven days. The result: bleeding decreased significantly in both groups, but the decrease was significantly greater in the shepherd’s purse group (p<0.001). The authors’ own conclusion is measured: the extract appears effective in reducing menstrual bleeding, but further research on efficacy and safety is required. The limits are clear: one trial, 84 patients, one centre, and the plant was tested not alone but on top of a drug — so this data does not say "shepherd’s purse instead of the medicine", it says "adding it to the medicine made a difference". The same group published a clinical trial in early postpartum haemorrhage in 2017. The evidence level is Limited.

Safety

Caution

Hypersensitivity to the active constituents can occur. Not recommended under 18, and the EU monograph gives the reason: heavy bleeding at that age is a condition requiring medical advice. Not recommended in pregnancy or lactation for lack of data. If symptoms worsen during use, or if adverse effects appear, consult a doctor. No undesirable effects are reported in the monographs, but that does not mean there are none: the EU monograph’s wording is "none known". The most important warning is not about the plant but about the condition attached to the indication: the monograph restricts use to women with regular cycles, after serious conditions have been excluded. Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Turkey: insufficient dataThat single sentence is all the Turkish monograph’s interaction section contains. Many monographs in this catalog say "none reported"; this one goes a step further in candour and says the data is not there. The practical consequence is the same: interactions are unknown, so anyone on regular medication should tell their doctor.
Painkillers (mefenamic acid and the like)Not an interaction warning but a note from the trial design: in the randomised trial above, shepherd’s purse was tested not alone but added on top of mefenamic acid, and no problem with adverse effects was reported in either group. So combined use was examined in that study — which is nonetheless a single study.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing.
