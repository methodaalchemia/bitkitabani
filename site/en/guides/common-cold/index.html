Herbs for the common cold

When winter comes, linden, rose hip and mountain tea move into Turkish kitchens. None of these herbs cures a cold, but some are accepted in Europe for traditional use in easing its symptoms.

In short

Echinacea may slightly shorten a cold, but results are inconsistent. Elderflower, linden and mountain tea are used by tradition to ease symptoms.

The plants, strongest evidence first

Echinacea Echinacea purpurea

Evidence: LimitedGenerally safe

It may have a small effect in preventing or shortening colds, but results are inconsistent. Short-term use is accepted in Europe.

Elder Sambucus nigra

Evidence: LimitedCaution

Elderflower is recognised for traditional use in the early symptoms of a cold. The berries must be cooked; raw berries are poisonous.

Elder: safety, interactions and sources →

Thyme and oregano Thymus

Evidence: LimitedGenerally safe

Recognised as a traditional herbal medicine for productive cough.

Thyme and oregano: safety, interactions and sources →

Eucalyptus Eucalyptus globulus

Evidence: LimitedCaution

Its steam and oil are recognised for traditional use in cough from colds. The oil must not be applied to children’s faces.

Eucalyptus: safety, interactions and sources →

Ginger Zingiber officinale

Evidence: LimitedGenerally safe

In China fresh ginger root is boiled and drunk for colds; this use rests on tradition.

Rose hip Rosa canina

Evidence: LimitedGenerally safe

The fresh fruit is very rich in vitamin C, but drying and boiling destroy much of it.

Linden Tilia tomentosa

Evidence: TraditionalGenerally safe

Traditional use for cold symptoms is accepted.

Evidence: TraditionalGenerally safe

Recognised for traditional use in cough from colds.

When to see a doctor: See a doctor for a high fever that does not come down, shortness of breath or symptoms lasting more than a week.
