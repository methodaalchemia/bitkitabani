Safety guide

Poisonous plants in Turkey and their most common look-alikes: how to tell them apart

Many plant poisonings happen when an edible or medicinal plant is confused with a poisonous look-alike. Below are the poisonous plants most often confused in Anatolia, how to tell them apart, and folk practices that lead to poisoning.

If you suspect poisoning: in Turkey call the National Poison Information Centre on 114 (24/7, free), or 112 if the situation is serious; elsewhere call your local poison centre or emergency number. Take a piece or a photo of the plant with you; do not try to induce vomiting or give milk or oil without advice.

The main causes of plant poisoning in Turkey

The Turkish Ministry of Health phytotherapy book summarises plant poisonings in Turkey under these causes:

  • Taking too much of a plant used for treatment
  • Using a poisonous look-alike instead of a medicinal plant
  • Eating a poisonous species by mistake instead of a plant gathered as a vegetable or herb
  • Eating a part of a plant without knowing that it is poisonous
  • Using poisonous plants to induce miscarriage

According to the book, most cases are in children aged 1–12 who eat poisonous species by mistake.

Folk practices that lead to poisoning

Mad honey (Pontic rhododendron)

Honey made by bees in the eastern Black Sea region from the flowers of Rhododendron ponticum (locally komar, kara ağu) and the yellow-flowered R. luteum (sarı ağu). Xenophon of Athens tells how Greek soldiers passing through the region around 400 BCE were poisoned by it. Today eating it by the spoonful as a “cure” is the main cause of poisoning: dizziness, nausea, sweating, a slow pulse and low blood pressure. It is treated in hospital (Baytop).

Oleander

Grown everywhere in parks and gardens. Boiling its leaves and drinking the liquid for diabetes or cancer causes serious heart poisoning; even a small amount is dangerous.

Opium poppy

According to the Turkish Ministry of Health phytotherapy book, ethnobotanical studies still record opium being used to put children to sleep, a practice that goes back to Ottoman times. It can stop breathing in babies.

Bitter almond and apricot kernels

The amygdalin they contain turns into cyanide in the body. Even a few can be dangerous for children; bitter apricot kernels sold as a “cancer treatment” carry the same risk.

High-risk Anatolian plants in the catalog

These plants are poisonous or have serious side effects; they are in the catalog for their cultural and historical importance and are not recommended for use.

  • Autumn crocus
  • Ephedra
  • European mistletoe
  • Henbane
  • Jimsonweed
  • Mandrake
  • Mapacho
  • Oleander
  • Opium poppy
  • Pennyroyal
  • Rue
  • Syrian rue
