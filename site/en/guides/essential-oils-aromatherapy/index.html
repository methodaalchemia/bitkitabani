Essential oils and aromatherapy: what does the evidence say, and where to be careful?

Essential oils are concentrated extracts that carry a plant’s scent; they are usually inhaled, diluted for massage or added to a bath. Clinical studies suggest that some may help with anxiety and nausea, but because they are concentrated they can also cause harm when used wrongly.

In short

Lavender oil is the most studied. Clinical trials and a meta-analysis suggest that inhaled or massaged lavender can reduce pre-operative anxiety and sleep problems; bitter orange and rose oil appear in similar studies. Inhaled peppermint and ginger oil have been tried for nausea. Evidence that essential oils reduce inflammation comes mostly from cell and animal studies; there is not yet enough evidence in humans.

The plants, strongest evidence first

Lavender Lavandula angustifolia

Evidence: ClinicalGenerally safe

Studies show that inhaled or massaged lavender reduces anxiety before surgery, during dental treatment and in dialysis. Standardised oral lavender oil capsules are used as a medicine for anxiety.

When to see a doctor: If you have swallowed an essential oil, got it in your eyes, or develop widespread skin redness or shortness of breath, seek medical help at once. Talk to a doctor before using essential oils with young children or in pregnancy.

Safety when using essential oils

Although natural, essential oils can cause allergy, contact dermatitis, sensitivity to light and eye irritation. They should not be applied undiluted to the skin; try a new oil on a small patch before using it on a larger area.

Citrus oils such as lemon and bergamot can cause light burns that may leave lasting marks if applied to the skin before going out in the sun.

Do not drink essential oils without expert advice: they can cause digestive problems and depress the nervous system. If oil gets into the eyes, rinse with plenty of water.

Essential oils break down in air over time and harmful substances can form; keep them tightly closed in a cool, dark place and do not use old oil.

Essential oils for inflammation?

A systematic review of studies from 2017–2022 examined the anti-inflammatory effect of 48 plant essential oils. The effects were shown in cell and animal experiments, but because there are few clinical studies in humans, these oils cannot be said to be effective or safe in the long term against inflammation.

Sources

  1. Vora et al., “Essential oils for clinical aromatherapy: A comprehensive review”, Journal of Ethnopharmacology 330, 2024
  2. Zhao et al., “Molecular mechanism of the anti-inflammatory effects of plant essential oils: A systematic review”, Journal of Ethnopharmacology 301, 2023
