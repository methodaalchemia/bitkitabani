Agrimony

Agrimonia eupatoria L.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

The plant’s three indications are applied by three different routes — by mouth, as a gargle and on the skin — yet all rest on one mechanism: tannins forming an astringent layer on a surface. To that effect the gut lining, the lining of the mouth and the skin are the same thing: an irritated surface. A plant whose three seemingly distant uses all follow from a single chemical property is common in phytotherapy and rarely said out loud.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the names "koyunotu, fıtıkotu" and lists four synonyms: Agrimonia elata, A. officinarum, A. sulcata and A. vulgaris.

In short

What is Agrimony?

Agrimony (Agrimonia eupatoria L.) is a plant of the Rosaceae (gülgiller) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the aerial parts.

What is Agrimony used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places all three indications under "traditional use": (1) symptomatic treatment of mild diarrhoea (oral); (2) as a gargle for symptomatic relief of minor inflammations of the mouth and throat; (3) relief of minor skin inflammation and small, superficial wounds (topical or as a bath additive). Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no randomised clinical trial of agrimony in humans.

Is Agrimony safe?

Safety label: caution. Not for anyone sensitive to the active substance or to the Rosaceae family; that family is large and includes apple, peach, strawberry and almond. The plant material is not to be applied as it is, and pharmaceutical forms must be prepared to the European Pharmacopoeia — the Turkish monograph states this separately. Because oral use may change blood glucose levels it should be used with care, and people on antidiabetic treatment are advised not to use it for that reason. Because of the tannin-like structures it contains, care is needed where tannins have to be used alongside. For the diarrhoea indication, if recurrent diarrhoea or bloody stools occur, see a doctor. Not recommended under 12, nor in pregnancy or lactation. The duration of use is short: three days for diarrhoea, a week for the other indications.

Traditional use

Europe — three indications

The EMA monograph places all three indications under "traditional use": (1) symptomatic treatment of mild diarrhoea (oral); (2) as a gargle for symptomatic relief of minor inflammations of the mouth and throat; (3) relief of minor skin inflammation and small, superficial wounds (topical or as a bath additive). All three follow from the astringent effect of the tannins.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the aerial parts" and stresses two things. First the preparation: the plant material is not to be applied as it is, and pharmaceutical forms must be prepared to the European Pharmacopoeia. Second blood sugar: because oral use may change blood glucose levels it should be used with care, and people on antidiabetic treatment are advised not to use it for that reason.

Anatolia

Agrimony is common in meadows, roadsides and forest clearings in Turkey, recognisable by its long spike of yellow flowers. Its hooked fruits spread by catching on animal fur and clothing.

In nature

Where it grows
Meadows, roadsides and forest clearings of Europe, North Africa and western Asia. Common in Turkey.
Harvest season
Yellow flowers in a long spike from June to September; the aerial parts are gathered in flower.
Local names
Koyunotu, fıtıkotu — both appear in the Turkish monograph.

Mix-up risk

Several Agrimonia species grow in Turkey and all are called koyunotu; the EU monograph covers A. eupatoria only. The name "fıtıkotu" is also used in Turkish for other plants — the Latin name on the label settles it.

Preparation

The monograph describes a separate route for each indication: oral for diarrhoea, a gargle for inflammation of the mouth and throat, and topical application or a bath additive for the skin. The duration is short: consult a doctor or pharmacist if symptoms persist beyond three days for diarrhoea or a week for the other two. Tinctures and extracts containing ethanol must carry the appropriate labelling. Not recommended under 12.

What science says

There is no randomised clinical trial of agrimony in humans. All the recent literature is at laboratory level: chemical profiling, antiviral and antiproliferative measurements in cell lines, combination trials for wound repair, work on intracellular signalling pathways. These show what is in the plant and what it does at cell level; they do not show whether it relieves diarrhoea or a sore throat in a patient. The EU monograph rests all three indications on long use. The known mechanism comes from the tannins and explains all three at once: tannins form an astringent layer on a surface — the same effect in the gut, in the mouth and on the skin. The Turkish monograph’s blood-sugar warning rests on a separate observation and is worth noting, because a blood-sugar warning is uncommon in a herbal monograph. The evidence level is Traditional.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Diabetes medicinesThe Turkish monograph states it may interact with antidiabetic drugs and goes a step further: people on antidiabetic treatment are advised not to use the plant for that reason. The grounds are in the monograph’s own sentence: oral use may change blood glucose levels.
Other tannin-rich productsBecause of the tannin-like structures it contains, the Turkish monograph asks for care where tannins have to be used alongside. Using tannin-rich drugs together stacks the astringent effect and can increase gastrointestinal complaints.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing; the Turkish one reports one drug group and adds an advice against use.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Agrimony.
