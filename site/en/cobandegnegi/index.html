Knotgrass

Polygonum aviculare L.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

None of the four Turkish names on the official list speaks of the plant’s healing — only of its ordinariness: the herb birds eat, that grows in the village, that the shepherd makes a stick from. Tolerating trampling, it lives in pavement cracks and on threshing floors — a plant everyone in Anatolia steps on every day. The EU monograph gives three indications, yet there is not a single clinical trial: the typical case in this catalog of a plant everyone knows and no one has measured.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the names "kadımalak, kuşekmeği, köyotu, çobandeğneği" and lists no synonyms. In English: knotgrass, knotweed. A prostrate, jointed-stemmed weed of pavement cracks and village squares — all its names speak of that ordinariness.

In short

What is Knotgrass?

Knotgrass (Polygonum aviculare L.) is a plant of the Polygonaceae (knotweed family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe, Asia and Siberia. Part used: the flowering aerial parts.

What is Knotgrass used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places all three under "traditional use": (1) relief of symptoms of common cold (tea); (2) symptomatic treatment of minor inflammations of the mouth or throat (gargle); (3) flushing of the urinary tract as an adjuvant in minor urinary complaints (decoction). Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no clinical trial of knotgrass in humans; no randomised study appears on PubMed.

Is Knotgrass safe?

Safety label: caution. Not for use in serious cardiac disease where reduced fluid intake is advised — the urinary indication requires drinking plenty of water. In urinary use, see a doctor if fever, painful urination, spasms or blood in the urine appear; in cold and throat use, if symptoms worsen. Not recommended under 12, in pregnancy or lactation. Hypersensitivity to the active substance can occur. No adverse effects known.

Traditional use

Europe — three indications

The EMA monograph places all three under "traditional use": (1) relief of symptoms of common cold (tea); (2) symptomatic treatment of minor inflammations of the mouth or throat (gargle); (3) flushing of the urinary tract as an adjuvant in minor urinary complaints (decoction). Three preparations, three doses.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph follows the EU one: not for use in serious cardiac disease where reduced fluid intake is advised; see a doctor if fever, dysuria, spasms or blood in the urine appear; not established under 12; not recommended in pregnancy or lactation. The interaction section is empty.

Anatolia

Knotgrass grows everywhere in Turkey, on trodden ground, roadsides and threshing floors. The name "kuşekmeği" (bird’s bread) comes from birds eating its seeds; "kadımalak" and "köyotu" (village herb) from where it lives. In folk use its tea is drunk for diarrhoea and the urinary tract; the EU monograph’s three indications are exactly where that folk use overlaps.

In nature

Where it grows
The temperate northern hemisphere; trodden ground, roadsides, pavement cracks. Common throughout Turkey.
Harvest season
Small pink-white flowers in the leaf axils from June to October; the flowering aerial parts are gathered in summer.
Local names
Kadımalak, kuşekmeği, köyotu, çobandeğneği — all four appear in the Turkish monograph.

Mix-up risk

Many species of Polygonum and related genera look alike; the monograph is for P. aviculare only. The name "çobandeğneği" is also used for other plants in Turkish.

Preparation

Common cold: 1.5–2 g of the comminuted drug infused in 150 ml of boiling water, 3–4 times daily. Mouth and throat: 1.5 g decocted in 200–250 ml of water, gargled 4–5 times daily. Urinary tract: 3 g decocted in 200 ml of water, twice daily; plenty of fluid is essential in that indication. See a doctor if no better within a week for the first two or two weeks for the third. Not recommended under 12.

What science says

There is no clinical trial of knotgrass in humans; no randomised study appears on PubMed. The EU monograph rests all three indications on long use. The known chemistry is classic: flavonoids and tannins, an astringent action explaining the gargle and diarrhoea uses; in the urinary indication most of the effect belongs to the water drunk for flushing. The evidence level is Traditional.

Safety

Caution

Not for use in serious cardiac disease where reduced fluid intake is advised — the urinary indication requires drinking plenty of water. In urinary use, see a doctor if fever, painful urination, spasms or blood in the urine appear; in cold and throat use, if symptoms worsen. Not recommended under 12, in pregnancy or lactation. Hypersensitivity to the active substance can occur. No adverse effects known.
