White micromeria

Clinopodium serpyllifolium (M.Bieb.) Kuntze
Evidence: LimitedCaution

The same plant has been published under three different genus names: it appears as Micromeria in older sources, as Clinopodium in the current Kew list, and as Nepeta (Nepeta serpyllifolia M.Bieb.) in the form in which the species name was first published. For this reason the old name has to be used when searching for publications about the plant.

Other names
In most publications it appears under its old name: Micromeria fruticosa (L.) Druce. The Kew world plant checklist treats this name as a synonym of Clinopodium serpyllifolium subsp. fruticosum (L.) Bräuchler; Micromeria fruticosa subsp. serpyllifolia is Clinopodium serpyllifolium itself, and subsp. brachycalyx is likewise a subspecies of the same species. English white micromeria, white-leaved savory. It has no monograph on the EMA or TİTCK lists.

In short

What is White micromeria?

White micromeria (Clinopodium serpyllifolium (M.Bieb.) Kuntze) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Asia and Siberia, Europe. Part used: leaves and flowering aerial parts; the essential oil.

What is White micromeria used for?

Traditional use: The 2008 ethnobotanical study carried out with 190 informants in the northern West Bank counts Micromeria fruticosa among the ten wild edible plants of highest cultural importance. Scientific evidence: limited. There is no clinical trial in humans on the species; the data come from test-tube and animal studies.

Is White micromeria safe?

Safety label: caution. ⚠️ The essential oil of the plant is rich in pulegone. According to the EMA’s public statement on pulegone and menthofuran (2016), the main target organ of these substances is the liver; drinking about 10 ml of pennyroyal oil, which is high in pulegone, has caused moderate to severe poisoning, and more than 15 ml has caused death. The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies pulegone in Group 2B (possibly carcinogenic to humans). The EMA recommends 0.75 mg/kg body weight per day as the acceptable limit for the sum of pulegone and menthofuran in herbal medicines; this comes to 37.5 mg a day in life-long use for a 50 kg adult and is scaled down with body weight in children. The essential oil should not be drunk; medicinal use of the plant should be avoided in pregnancy, while breastfeeding and in children, because there are no safety data for the species. People with liver disease should keep away from strong teas and extracts. If the essential oil has been swallowed, call 114 (the National Poison Information Centre of Türkiye) or 112.

Traditional use

Palestine — a wild edible herb and folk remedy

The 2008 ethnobotanical study carried out with 190 informants in the northern West Bank counts Micromeria fruticosa among the ten wild edible plants of highest cultural importance. A 2012 study on the plant collected from Nablus writes that the species is widely used in folk medicine for abdominal pain, diarrhoea, eye infections, heart disorders, high blood pressure, colds and wounds.

Mediterranean — inflammatory conditions

The introduction of a 2013 animal study states that the plant is widely used in many Mediterranean regions for various inflammatory conditions. This is the traditional use that the study tested; it is not an effect confirmed in humans.

Türkiye — a commercial product from the Tortum Valley

A 2026 study in the journal Food and Health examined dried Micromeria fruticosa obtained commercially from the Tortum Valley of Erzurum. Another study published the same year by researchers in Türkiye tested the essential oil of the plant in animals as a wound ointment.

In nature

Where it grows
According to the Kew world plant checklist the native range of the species is eastern Spain, Crimea and the Eastern Mediterranean: Spain, Italy, the north-western Balkans, Crimea, Türkiye and Lebanon-Syria. More than one subspecies occurs in Türkiye (subsp. brachycalyx, subsp. giresunicum). The publications examined samples collected from the West Bank and from the Tortum Valley of Erzurum.
Harvest season
The studies were carried out on the flowering aerial parts and on the leaves; the sources do not give a specific collecting month.
Local names
No Turkish local name is given in the sources examined.

Mix-up risk

Pulegone is also found in the essential oil of other members of the mint family such as pennyroyal (EMA). More than one subspecies has been studied in the publications under the name Micromeria fruticosa, and the composition of the oil varies with the region where it was collected; the pulegone content measured in one sample does not hold for another sample.

Preparation

Among the people its leaves are infused as a tea or eaten as a fresh herb. Because there is no monograph for this species on the EMA or TİTCK lists, there is no accepted dose. The main constituent of its essential oil is pulegone (42.81 per cent and 58.5 per cent in the samples measured); for pulegone and menthofuran the EMA recommends that total daily intake should not exceed 0.75 mg per kilogram of body weight. There is no safe recipe for oral use of the essential oil; this page gives no recipe for the essential oil.

What science says

There is no clinical trial in humans on the species; the data come from test-tube and animal studies. In the essential oil of the Nablus sample oxygenated compounds were found at 87.4 per cent and pulegone at 58.5 per cent; in the 2026 study in Türkiye the main constituents were pulegone (42.81 per cent) and piperitenone (29.73 per cent). In mice the aqueous extract at a dose of 200 mg/kg significantly reduced carrageenan-induced paw oedema and the gastric lesions caused by indomethacin; at doses of 100 and 200 mg/kg it was also effective in pain tests. The essential oil inhibited growth in colon and breast cancer cell lines in vitro (IC50 10 and 12.7 µg/ml). A nanoemulsion containing 5 per cent essential oil gave an inhibition zone of 16.1 mm and a minimum inhibitory concentration of 1.25 mg/ml against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, and was tested for 12 days in an infected wound model in diabetic rats. The oils of samples from three regions of the West Bank turned out to differ from one another in antioxidant and antimicrobial activity. These findings have not been tested in humans.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Drug interactionsNo drug interaction study has been carried out for this species. The EMA statement notes that pulegone targets the liver; for this reason people with liver disease and those taking medicines that can affect the liver should not use pulegone-containing oil and concentrated extracts without consulting a doctor.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying White micromeria.
