What is Umckaloabo?

Umckaloabo (Pelargonium sidoides DC.) is a plant of the Geraniaceae (geranium family) family. Traditions that use it: Africa. Part used: the root.

Traditional use

South Africa — its origin

The plant is native to the upland grasslands of South Africa and Lesotho; its root was used in local folk medicine for respiratory complaints. It was carried to Europe at the beginning of the 20th century and today is among cold products.

Europe — the common cold

The European Medicines Agency monograph places the indication under the heading "traditional use": relief of the symptoms of the common cold. The monograph also gives doses for children — ages 3–5, 6–12 and over 12 are written separately — which is not something often met in traditional-use monographs.

Türkiye — official list

The TİTCK monograph gives the part used as "roots", requires the drug to comply with the European Pharmacopoeia monograph (ref. 2264), and writes the hepatotoxicity warning as a separate item.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to the high grasslands of South Africa and Lesotho. It has no wild distribution in Turkey and is imported as an extract.
Harvest season
Deep red-purple flowers through spring and summer; the root is lifted during dormancy.
Local names
Güney Afrika sardunyası in Turkish, the name the regulatory monograph also uses.

Mix-up risk

Garden geraniums belong to the same genus but are different species; no product is made from the root of a balcony geranium. The monograph also covers Pelargonium reniforme. The extract code on the label (usually EPs 7630) and the Latin name are what settle it.

Preparation

The monograph gives doses of the liquid preparation by age: 1.4 ml three times daily (4.2 ml a day) for adolescents over 12, adults and the elderly; 0.9 ml three times daily for children aged 6–12; 0.4 ml three times daily for ages 3–5. Oral use under 3 is not recommended unless a doctor advises it. If symptoms last longer than a week, consult a doctor or pharmacist.

What science says

The 2008 Cochrane review addresses this plant directly and its conclusion is cautious: "P. sidoides may be effective in alleviating symptoms of acute rhinosinusitis and the common cold in adults, but doubt exists." Three trials in adults with acute bronchitis (746 patients) and three in children (819 children) showed substantial heterogeneity for all relevant outcomes — the trials do not agree with one another. One detail the review identifies matters in practice: a possible cause of that heterogeneity is the type of preparation, because it was not effective in tablet form. Four trials were excluded for excessive risk of bias. Adverse events were more common with P. sidoides, but none were serious. In short: there may be an effect, but the trials are inconsistent and which preparation is used changes the result. The evidence level is Limited.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No interactions reported in either monographNeither the EMA nor the Turkish monograph lists anything under interactions. That is a statement about what has been studied, not evidence that no interaction exists.
Medicines that affect the liverNo interaction study confirms a problem, but because both monographs report hepatotoxicity and hepatitis, anyone taking liver-affecting medicines should tell their doctor. The issue here is not two substances reacting with each other but one organ being burdened from two directions.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Umckaloabo.
