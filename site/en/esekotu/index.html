BT-236 · Onagraceae (willowherb family)

Evening primrose

Oenothera biennis L.; Oenothera lamarckiana L.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

In Turkey this oil is sold as "çuha çiçeği yağı", and the name is wrong: the çuha çiçeği (Primula) is a spring-flowering plant of a quite different family, while the oil comes from the evening-flowering Oenothera. The confusion comes from English — "evening primrose" was translated word for word and the translation settled into the product name. Even the Turkish regulator lists it among the Turkish names. So one of the market’s best-selling herbal oils is known by a plant that is not in it — and in its one approved indication it is, according to Cochrane, no different from placebo.

Other names
Turkey has published separate but identical monographs for the two species; the Turkish names are "eşek otu, akşam sefası, güneş damlası, çuha çiçeği yağı". In English: evening primrose, evening star. ⚠️ The market name "çuha çiçeği yağı" (primrose oil) is misleading: the primrose (Primula) is a different plant in a different family; the oil comes from this plant. The drug is the fixed oil of the seed.

In short

What is Evening primrose?

Evening primrose (Oenothera biennis L.; Oenothera lamarckiana L.) is a plant of the Onagraceae (willowherb family) family. Traditions that use it: North America. Part used: the fixed oil obtained from the seeds.

What is Evening primrose used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": symptomatic relief of itching in acute and chronic dry skin conditions — orally, 4–6 g of oil daily. Scientific evidence: limited. Evening primrose oil is the plant in this catalog where fame and evidence diverge most sharply.

Is Evening primrose safe?

Safety label: caution. People with epilepsy must not use it (Turkish monograph) — there is a long-standing concern that evening primrose oil may lower the seizure threshold, and the monograph writes it as a firm contraindication. Stop two weeks before surgery, and an interaction with anticoagulants is reported: the Cochrane review also cites a study finding increased bleeding in people on warfarin. Indigestion, nausea, softened stool, raised temperature, rash and headache have been reported; frequencies unknown. Avoid UV exposure during use. A case report cited in the Cochrane review warns of a potential risk of inflammation, thrombosis and immunosuppression with use longer than a year — which the short-term trials do not measure. Not orally under 12; not recommended in pregnancy or lactation.

Traditional use

Europe — one indication

The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": symptomatic relief of itching in acute and chronic dry skin conditions — orally, 4–6 g of oil daily. The monograph lists adverse effects at length: indigestion, nausea, softening of stool, rise in temperature, exanthema, headache. Not established under 12; see a doctor if no better after eight weeks.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph adds limits absent from the EU one: people with epilepsy must not use it; stop two weeks before surgery; avoid UV exposure during use; not near the eyes or on open wounds; not orally under 12. Interactions: anticoagulants, anticonvulsants, phenothiazines.

From North America to Europe

Evening primrose is native to North America; it reached Europe as an ornamental in the 17th century and spread along roadsides. Its flowers open in the evening — hence the Turkish "akşam sefası" and the English name. In the second half of the 20th century the seed oil became one of the supplement market’s big products as a source of gamma-linolenic acid.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to North America; naturalised along roadsides, dunes and waste ground in Europe and Turkey.
Harvest season
Biennial; yellow flowers opening in the evenings of the second summer, seed capsules in autumn. The oil is pressed from the ripe seed.
Local names
Eşek otu, akşam sefası, güneş damlası, çuha çiçeği yağı — all four appear in the Turkish monograph.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ Confusion of name: "çuha çiçeği" is the name of the genus Primula, not this plant. "Akşam sefası" is also used in Turkish for Mirabilis jalapa, another separate plant. When buying, look for the Latin name (Oenothera) and the words "seed oil".

Preparation

For those over 12 the EU monograph gives a single dose of 2–3 g and a daily dose of 4–6 g of seed oil, orally. If symptoms persist beyond eight weeks, consult a doctor or pharmacist. Stop two weeks before surgery (Turkish monograph). Not used orally under 12.

What science says

Evening primrose oil is the plant in this catalog where fame and evidence diverge most sharply. The indication the EU monograph approves — itching in dry skin — is exactly the territory of eczema, and the 2013 Cochrane review pooled 27 trials and 1,596 participants there to conclude: oral evening primrose oil lacks effect on eczema; improvement was the same as placebo. The difference in participant-rated global symptoms was −2.22 points (on 0–100; 95% CI −10.48 to 6.04), and −3.26 (−6.96 to 0.45) on doctors’ ratings. Most trials were at low risk of bias and the confidence intervals are narrow — so this is not "no evidence" but "no effect". A meta-analysis published in 2025 in Dermatitis repeats the finding: evening primrose oil showed no benefit beyond placebo. For breast pain, a 2021 meta-analysis likewise found no difference against placebo, topical NSAIDs, danazol or vitamin E. The monograph does not deny any of this: it rests the indication not on clinical evidence but "exclusively on long-standing use". We record the evidence level as Limited and state its direction plainly: the good-quality clinical evidence that exists points to no effect.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
AnticoagulantsReported by the Turkish monograph, and the Cochrane review cites a study finding increased bleeding in people on warfarin. Anyone on a blood thinner should not use this oil or should ask their doctor; it is also the reason for the two-week rule before surgery.
Anticonvulsants and phenothiazinesOn the Turkish list; part of the same concern as the epilepsy contraindication. Phenothiazine antipsychotics can lower the seizure threshold; stacking the two effects is unwanted.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing in its interaction section; the Turkish one reports three groups.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Evening primrose.
