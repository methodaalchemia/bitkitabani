Summer savory

Satureja hortensis L.
Evidence: LimitedGenerally safe

In Georgia a dry aqueous extract obtained from the leaf of summer savory was licensed as a medicine under the name “Saturin”, in capsules of 0.33 grams; the Kutateladze Institute of Pharmacochemistry in Tbilisi reports that the product was approved for use in type 2 diabetes either alone or together with other medicines (Georgian Medical News, 2012). The abstract of the publication does not give the number of patients or the outcome measures of the clinical trial.

Other names
Turkish sater, sater otu, bahçe sateri; in trade it is gathered together with other Satureja species under the name “sivri kekik”. English summer savory, garden savory. Its synonyms include Satureja officinarum Crantz and Satureja pachyphylla K.Koch (POWO). It has no monograph on the EMA or TİTCK lists; this record rests on peer-reviewed reviews, one randomised clinical trial and two publications from Türkiye.

In short

What is Summer savory?

Summer savory (Satureja hortensis L.) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe, Asia and Siberia. Part used: leaves and flowering branches (aerial parts); the essential oil.

What is Summer savory used for?

Traditional use: Summer savory grows wild in many parts of Türkiye, including Erzurum; even so, it is not widely cultivated (Bairamian Danalou and Özer, 2022). Scientific evidence: limited. Most of the research consists of laboratory and animal studies.

Is Summer savory safe?

Safety label: generally safe. The 2018 review notes that side effects of herbal savory products are very rare. The same review writes that savory products are not recommended for pregnant women and children, and that people with diabetes, low blood sugar, high blood pressure or a bleeding disorder should use them with caution. The sources contain no data on breastfeeding. The pure essential oil can irritate the skin and mucous membranes; its oral LD50 has been reported as 1370 mg/kg in rats and its dermal value as 340 mg/kg in guinea pigs. Because the leaf extract is licensed in Georgia as a blood-sugar-lowering medicine, people taking diabetes medication should not use concentrated extracts without consulting a doctor.

Traditional use

Anatolia — spice, tea and folk remedy

Summer savory grows wild in many parts of Türkiye, including Erzurum; even so, it is not widely cultivated (Bairamian Danalou and Özer, 2022). A 2003 paper in the Journal of Ethnopharmacology records that the plant is used as a folk remedy in Eastern Anatolia for various infectious diseases and disorders. Satureja species are consumed in Türkiye as herbal tea and as a spice (Satıl et al., 2008).

Türkiye — the “sivri kekik” trade

A study published in the journal Ekoloji in 2008 documented the trade by interviewing collectors in 17 provinces, 20 towns and 40 villages where the genus Satureja is widespread. About 700–800 tonnes of sivri kekik are collected from the wild in Türkiye each year; the main collecting areas are the Aegean and Mediterranean regions. Summer savory is one of the four species collected commercially; the others are S. cuneifolia, S. thymbra and S. spicigera. Collecting is mostly done by low-income villagers, and the product goes through middlemen to processing companies in the provincial cities and from there to the domestic market and to export. The authors recommend restricting collection of the commercial species.

Europe — a kitchen herb

According to a 2018 review (Molecules), the leaves of summer savory are used as a seasoning in stuffings and meat dishes, especially in Europe; its sprigs are boiled together with peas or cabbage. The plant is one of the components of the “Herbes de Provence” spice blend. The same review reports that in folk medicine it is used as an antispasmodic, carminative, expectorant and diuretic, and also for stomach pain.

In nature

Where it grows
Its native range extends from South-East Europe to Kazakhstan and North-West Iran; it is a plant of temperate climates (POWO). It grows wild in many parts of Türkiye, but its cultivation is not widespread. In the Erzurum trial the dry herb yield was found to be 425–610.21 kg per decare; the genotype of Konya origin gave the highest dry herb and essential oil yield.
Harvest season
It is an annual plant (POWO). In the field trial in Erzurum the time to flowering was measured at 73.80–91.25 days and plant height at 29.42–40.83 cm, depending on the genotype.
Local names
Sater, sater otu; the common trade name of the genus Satureja is sivri kekik.

Mix-up risk

In trade four Satureja species are gathered together under the name “sivri kekik” (S. hortensis, S. cuneifolia, S. thymbra, S. spicigera); the name does not point to a single species. POWO describes summer savory as an annual species. The composition of the essential oil also varies from sample to sample: the carvacrol content has been reported at between 11 and 67 per cent.

Preparation

The dried leaves and flowering branches are added to food as a spice or infused to make a herbal tea. Because there is no monograph for this species on the EMA or TİTCK lists, there is no accepted dose. The form tested in the clinical trial was not a product taken by mouth: a 1 per cent gel of the essential oil was applied twice a day for two weeks to the palatal tissue under the denture. The 2018 review notes that the pure essential oil can irritate the skin and mucous membranes. In the 15 genotypes trialled in Erzurum the essential oil content of the dry herb was measured at between 0.72 and 1.14 per cent.

What science says

Most of the research consists of laboratory and animal studies. According to the 2018 review, the main constituents of the essential oil are carvacrol (11–67 per cent), γ-terpinene (15.3–39 per cent), thymol (0.3–28.2 per cent) and p-cymene (3.5–19.6 per cent); the proportions vary with climate, season and genotype. The review summarises antioxidant, antimicrobial and insecticidal effects at the cell and animal level; it reports no clinical trial carried out in humans. In the 2003 study on samples from Eastern Anatolia the methanol extract, at a concentration of 300 µg/ml, inhibited 23 strains of 11 bacterial species and 6 isolates of Candida albicans, while it had no effect on 83 per cent of the microorganisms tested. The main human data come from Iran: in a randomised, placebo-controlled trial in 80 patients with denture stomatitis, a 1 per cent essential oil gel significantly reduced palatal redness (P<0.0001) and the Candida colony count (P=0.001) in two weeks. A 2026 systematic review examined eight randomised trials (347 participants) testing essential oils in denture stomatitis; the summer savory trial is one of them, and because of the differences between the trials the findings could only be combined in narrative form. No clinical trial testing the use of summer savory as a tea or a spice appears in these sources.

Safety

Generally safe

The 2018 review notes that side effects of herbal savory products are very rare. The same review writes that savory products are not recommended for pregnant women and children, and that people with diabetes, low blood sugar, high blood pressure or a bleeding disorder should use them with caution. The sources contain no data on breastfeeding. The pure essential oil can irritate the skin and mucous membranes; its oral LD50 has been reported as 1370 mg/kg in rats and its dermal value as 340 mg/kg in guinea pigs. Because the leaf extract is licensed in Georgia as a blood-sugar-lowering medicine, people taking diabetes medication should not use concentrated extracts without consulting a doctor.
