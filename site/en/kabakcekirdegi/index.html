Pumpkin seed

Cucurbita pepo L.
Evidence: LimitedGenerally safe

Most plants in these monographs have no business in a kitchen. Pumpkin seed is the exception: it is eaten by the kilo as a snack in Turkey, and the same seed carries an indication in the EU monograph. The monograph does not ignore the double identity — it separates it: no concern is reported for eating them, while extracts prepared from the seed are not recommended in pregnancy. Eating a thing and using a preparation made from it are not, in the regulator’s eyes, the same act.

In short

What is Pumpkin seed?

Pumpkin seed (Cucurbita pepo L.) is a plant of the Cucurbitaceae (gourd family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, North America. Part used: the seed and its fatty oil.

What is Pumpkin seed used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": relief of lower urinary tract symptoms related to benign prostatic hyperplasia or to an overactive bladder — after serious conditions have been excluded by a medical doctor. Scientific evidence: limited. Here the evidence is now pooled, and the answer is measured.

Is Pumpkin seed safe?

Safety label: generally safe. No serious safety problem is reported in either monograph, which fits a plant that is also a food. Not for anyone hypersensitive to the active substance. Mild gastrointestinal complaints have been reported, and the EU monograph puts the frequency at common (4%) — a numbered frequency is unusual in these monographs, which makes it worth noting. Not recommended in children and adolescents under 18, because lower urinary tract symptoms require medical supervision. In pregnancy and lactation: no concern is reported from eating pumpkin seeds and the oil, but extracts prepared from the seed are not recommended for lack of data. If complaints worsen, or if fever, spasms, blood in the urine, painful urination or difficulty passing urine occur, consult a doctor or pharmacist. The most important warning is not about toxicity: because the same symptoms can belong to other and serious conditions, the diagnosis has to come before the herbal product.

Traditional use

Europe — prostate and bladder

The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": relief of lower urinary tract symptoms related to benign prostatic hyperplasia or to an overactive bladder — after serious conditions have been excluded by a medical doctor. That condition is in the monograph’s own sentence and it matters especially here, because the same symptoms can also be symptoms of prostate cancer.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the seed, the fatty oil". It is not recommended under 18, and the reason is instructive: lower urinary tract symptoms require medical supervision. The restriction comes not from toxicity but from the principle that such a symptom at that age should not be managed with a herbal product alone.

Anatolia — between food and medicine

Pumpkin seeds are eaten widely as a snack in Turkey, which sets this plant apart from most in the monographs. The monograph keeps the distinction: it notes that no concern about malformation in humans has arisen from eating pumpkin seeds and the fatty oil, while not recommending extracts prepared from the seed during pregnancy and lactation. A plant whose line between food and medicine is drawn this plainly is rare.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to Central and North America, now grown throughout the temperate world. Widely cultivated in Turkey, with varieties grown specifically for snack seeds.
Harvest season
Flowers in summer, fruit ripens in autumn; the seeds are taken from the ripe fruit and dried.
Local names
Sakız kabağı, çekirdek kabağı, balkabağı — all three appear in the Turkish monograph; they name different cultivars of botanically the same species.

Mix-up risk

Different species of the same family get mixed up at the point of sale: melon, watermelon and pumpkin seeds sit side by side as snacks. The monograph covers Cucurbita pepo only. Ornamental gourds (also C. pepo cultivars) can be bitter and high in cucurbitacins; a squash that tastes bitter should not be eaten.

Preparation

The monograph describes preparations of the seed and the fatty oil. The use is long-term by nature: if complaints worsen, or if fever, spasms, blood in the urine, painful urination or difficulty passing urine occur, see a doctor. Not used under 18.

What science says

Here the evidence is now pooled, and the answer is measured. A systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2026 in Acta Cirúrgica Brasileira compared pumpkin seed separately against two things in men with lower urinary tract symptoms from benign prostatic hyperplasia: placebo, and alpha-blockers. Eight randomised trials, 2,170 men. Versus placebo the international prostate symptom score (IPSS) fell significantly at three months (mean difference −0.62, 95% CI −1.15 to −0.09), but not at six months. Maximum urinary flow improved against placebo only. Against alpha-blockers no outcome differed significantly. In one head-to-head trial adverse events were fewer with pumpkin seed. The review’s own reading is plain-spoken: pumpkin seed offers at most a small, sub-clinical IPSS benefit over placebo, not maintained at six months, and no significant difference from alpha-blockers — but equivalence is not established, and certainty was low to very low. So "as good as the drug" is not what this data says; what it says is that a difference could not be shown.

Safety

Generally safe

No serious safety problem is reported in either monograph, which fits a plant that is also a food. Not for anyone hypersensitive to the active substance. Mild gastrointestinal complaints have been reported, and the EU monograph puts the frequency at common (4%) — a numbered frequency is unusual in these monographs, which makes it worth noting. Not recommended in children and adolescents under 18, because lower urinary tract symptoms require medical supervision. In pregnancy and lactation: no concern is reported from eating pumpkin seeds and the oil, but extracts prepared from the seed are not recommended for lack of data. If complaints worsen, or if fever, spasms, blood in the urine, painful urination or difficulty passing urine occur, consult a doctor or pharmacist. Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
LithiumThe Turkish monograph reports one interaction, and it is lithium. Because lithium is cleared by the kidney and its effect depends on a narrow range of blood levels, adding a product that acts on the urinary tract is worth taking seriously. Anyone on lithium should tell their doctor.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing. "None reported" is not evidence of no interaction; it means not studied.
Prostate medicinesThere is no interaction study, and the real issue here is not interaction but substitution. In the meta-analysis pumpkin seed did not differ significantly from alpha-blockers, but the review states explicitly that this does not establish equivalence. Replacing a prescribed prostate medicine with pumpkin seed is a decision for a doctor.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Pumpkin seed.
