BT-225 · Malvaceae (mallow family)

Kola nut

Cola nitida (Vent.) Schott et Endl. and its varieties; Cola acuminata (P. Beauv.) Schott et Endl.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

The monograph gathers two Cola species under one heading — yet the culture the plant comes from distinguishes them sharply. The species recognised by international trade and by medicines lists is Cola nitida; in Igbo culture that is called Hausa kola (gworo), and a study published in 2017 in the International Journal of Arts and Humanities records that it is not used in serious rituals: splitting in two, it carries no ceremonial meaning. The ceremonial nut is the other species, Cola acuminata (ojı Igbo); its value comes from the varying number of lobes, each number carrying a meaning — four lobes being "ojı udo na ngozi", the kola of peace and blessing. The same study records that kola is offered to a guest before anything else, that the eldest says the prayer, and that who breaks the nut varies by region. What the official monograph passes over in the words "and its varieties" is, on the ground, two separate plants.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the name "kola" and treats two species together. Synonyms of C. nitida: Cola vera K.Schum., Sterculia cola Pers., Sterculia nitida Vent.; of C. acuminata: Sterculia acuminata P.Beauv., Cola pseudoacuminata Engl. In English: cola, kola tree. In Igbo ojı (oji), in Hausa gworo.

In short

What is Kola nut?

Kola nut (Cola nitida (Vent.) Schott et Endl. and its varieties; Cola acuminata (P. Beauv.) Schott et Endl.) is a plant of the Malvaceae (mallow family) family. Traditions that use it: Africa. Part used: the seeds.

What is Kola nut used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": symptoms of temporary fatigue and sensation of weakness. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no trial of kola on its own in the monograph indication — temporary fatigue and weakness — and the EU monograph rests the indication directly on long use.

Is Kola nut safe?

Safety label: caution. Not for use in gastric and duodenal ulcers, in cardiovascular disorders such as hypertension and arrhythmia, or in hyperthyroidism — both the EU and the Turkish monograph say so, and the reason in all three cases is caffeine. Not recommended before bedtime; insomnia, over-stimulation, restlessness and stomach complaints have been reported. Alcohol should not be taken while using it (Turkish monograph). Use under 18 is not recommended. Not recommended in pregnancy or lactation; the monograph notes separately that caffeine passes readily into breast milk and across the placenta into the fetal circulation. Tinctures and extracts may contain ethanol. If there is daily coffee, tea or energy drink consumption, the total caffeine load has to be counted — a warning the monograph does not carry but that is unavoidable.

Traditional use

Europe — one indication

The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": symptoms of temporary fatigue and sensation of weakness. The contraindications fall where they would for a stimulant: gastric and duodenal ulcers, cardiovascular disorders such as hypertension and arrhythmia, hyperthyroidism.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the same contraindications and adds practical warnings: it is not recommended before bedtime, alcohol should not be taken while using it, and use under 18 is not recommended. It also states that caffeine passes readily into breast milk and across the placenta into the fetal circulation. Insomnia, over-stimulation, restlessness and stomach complaints are among the reported effects.

West Africa — market and ceremony

The kola nut has been chewed in West Africa for centuries and is one of the oldest goods of the trans-Saharan trade routes. In Igbo culture ojı (kola) is offered to a guest before anything else; its sharing, its prayer and the order of its breaking are all formally governed. But there is a distinction right here that the monograph does not see — see below.

In nature

Where it grows
The rainforest belt of West Africa; now cultivated across the tropics. It does not grow in Turkey and arrives as a product.
Harvest season
It fruits through much of the year in the tropics; the seeds are chewed fresh and also sold dried.
Local names
Kola — the Turkish monograph gives one Turkish name. Local names: Igbo ojı, Hausa gworo.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ The two species the monograph covers (C. nitida and C. acuminata) hold different ceremonial value but are treated together in the medical monograph. The name also causes confusion with cola soft drinks: today’s commercial colas do not take their flavour from this nut, and their caffeine comes from other sources.

Preparation

For adults the EU monograph gives: powdered herbal substance 1–3 g three times daily; as a herbal tea, 1–3 g of the powder as a decoction in 150 ml of water three times daily; liquid extract 0.6–1.2 ml three times daily; tincture 1–4 ml three times daily. If symptoms persist longer than a week, consult a doctor or pharmacist. It is not recommended before bedtime. Use under 18 is not recommended.

What science says

There is no trial of kola on its own in the monograph indication — temporary fatigue and weakness — and the EU monograph rests the indication directly on long use. Where its effect comes from, however, is not in doubt: the seed carries caffeine, and caffeine’s effect on alertness is well-established pharmacology. The entire set of warnings and interactions in the monographs follows from that single substance — insomnia, palpitations, contraindication in ulcer and arrhythmia, the weakening of sedatives, the amplification of sympathomimetic side effects. So here the "traditional" label does not mean ineffective; it means the effect has not been measured as a herbal product, but is known through the substance the plant carries. The evidence level is Traditional.

Safety

Caution

Not for use in gastric and duodenal ulcers, in cardiovascular disorders such as hypertension and arrhythmia, or in hyperthyroidism — both the EU and the Turkish monograph say so, and the reason in all three cases is caffeine. Not recommended before bedtime; insomnia, over-stimulation, restlessness and stomach complaints have been reported. Alcohol should not be taken while using it (Turkish monograph). Use under 18 is not recommended. Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
MAO inhibitorsBoth the EU and the Turkish monograph single out this group; the Turkish one gives furazolidone, procarbazine and selegiline as examples. The EU monograph says it should be used with caution.
Sedative medicines and sympathomimeticsThe EU monograph’s wording is direct: kola, like other caffeine-containing preparations, reduces the action of sedative drugs and increases the side effects caused by sympathomimetic drugs. It weakens the drug in one direction and strengthens its side effects in the other.
CYP1A2 inhibitors and quinolone antibioticsOn the Turkish monograph’s list, with a clear mechanism: caffeine is broken down largely by the CYP1A2 enzyme, so drugs that inhibit it (including some quinolone antibiotics) can lengthen how long caffeine stays in the body. That means more effect and more side effect without any increase in dose.
The other groups the Turkish monograph reportsAntidepressants, anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs, antidiabetics, beta-adrenergic agonists, stimulants, diuretics and contraceptives are also listed. Combining it with stimulants and beta agonists stacks two things acting in the same direction; contraceptives can slow caffeine metabolism.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Kola nut.
