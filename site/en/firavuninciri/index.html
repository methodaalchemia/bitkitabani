Sycamore fig

Ficus sycomorus L.
Evidence: TraditionalGenerally safe

The fruit has to be gashed before it will ripen — a job so much its own that in the Book of Amos the prophet introduces himself as "a dresser of sycamore trees".

Other names
Arabic "jummayz"; English sycamore fig, fig-mulberry; Hebrew "shikma". In Turkish it is called firavun inciri (pharaoh’s fig) or yabani incir. Neither the European Medicines Agency nor the Turkish agency has a monograph for this species.
Part used
Fruit; in traditional use also the leaf, bark and latex
Region
Africa

Use
Food, Ritual and shamanic
In short

What is Sycamore fig?

Sycamore fig (Ficus sycomorus L.) is a plant of the Moraceae family. Traditions that use it: Africa, Anatolia and the Mediterranean. Part used: fruit; in traditional use also the leaf, bark and latex.

What is Sycamore fig used for?

Traditional use: According to a study published in the Journal of Aswan Faculty of Arts in 2024, the sycamore fig was one of the most important trees of ancient Egypt. Scientific evidence: traditional. In the sources relied on here there is no controlled trial in humans.

Is Sycamore fig safe?

Safety label: generally safe. The fruit is a food and has been eaten for thousands of years; the sources relied on here report no harm from it. Because there is no monograph there is no official list of contraindications. The latex of figs and the mulberry family can irritate the skin and may mark it in sunlight; this is worth remembering when picking from the tree. There are no data for pregnancy, lactation, children or drug interactions, so concentrated products made from parts other than the fruit are not recommended.

Traditional use

Ancient Egypt — from temple to coffin

According to a study published in the Journal of Aswan Faculty of Arts in 2024, the sycamore fig was one of the most important trees of ancient Egypt. The goddess Hathor is called "the lady of the sycamore". Its wood was used to make coffins, its fruit was a principal item on offering tables, and its remains have been found at Egyptian archaeological sites. The same study records that the fruit has to be cut for it to ripen and to keep insects away.

Ethiopia — a multipurpose tree

A field study published in Frontiers in Pharmacology in 2025, carried out with 110 informants in the Metema district of north-western Ethiopia, ranked the sycamore fig among the three most multipurpose plants of the area. A 2024 study in the Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine from Arsi Robe likewise counts it among the wild edible plants with several uses. Both studies document use; neither measures efficacy.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew’s records its range covers 48 regions: most of Africa together with Egypt and the eastern Mediterranean coast. It is a broad-trunked, wide-crowned tree of hot, dry country, growing near water.
Harvest season
The sources relied on here give no collection calendar for Turkey; the tree does not grow there naturally.
Local names
In Turkish, firavun inciri (pharaoh’s fig) and yabani incir; in Arabic "jummayz".

Mix-up risk

It is the same genus as the fig (*Ficus carica*) but a different species: the fig bears its fruit singly at the tips of the shoots, the sycamore fig in clusters on the trunk itself. A species’ monograph and restrictions do not belong to its relative.

Preparation

No official monograph sets an accepted dose; the fruit is a food and is eaten fresh. The sources relied on here give no measure for a home preparation. Concentrated extracts of the leaf, bark and latex are not food, and these sources give no dose for their use in people.

What science says

In the sources relied on here there is no controlled trial in humans. Both studies are ethnobotanical fieldwork: they record who uses what and for what, not whether it works. The Metema study writes in its own conclusion that the documented plants call for further pharmacological investigation — that is, their effects have not yet been studied.

Safety

Generally safe

The fruit is a food and has been eaten for thousands of years; the sources relied on here report no harm from it. Because there is no monograph there is no official list of contraindications. The latex of figs and the mulberry family can irritate the skin and may mark it in sunlight; this is worth remembering when picking from the tree. There are no data for pregnancy, lactation, children or drug interactions, so concentrated products made from parts other than the fruit are not recommended.
