Sweet clover

Melilotus officinalis (L.) Lam.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

The history of modern blood thinners begins with this plant’s mouldy hay. In 1930s North America, cattle began dying of internal haemorrhage after eating sweet clover hay that had gone mouldy because it was not dried properly; the condition was called sweet clover disease. In 1939 the team in Karl Link’s laboratory isolated the substance causing the bleeding: the mould had converted the plant’s scent-giving coumarin into dicoumarol, which blocks an enzyme needed to activate vitamin K. The team modified that substance into a more potent compound, and in 1948 it went on sale as warfarin. A class of drug used by millions today came out of a field plant going bad — and the Turkish monograph’s sentence "not for anyone taking anticoagulants" is a continuation of the same story.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the names "kokulu yonca, taş yoncası, sarı taş yoncası"; Sertula officinalis is a synonym. In English: melilot, sweet clover. The scent in its name comes from coumarin — and coumarin explains both this plant’s history and its warnings.

In short

What is Sweet clover?

Sweet clover (Melilotus officinalis (L.) Lam.) is a plant of the Fabaceae (baklagiller) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the aerial parts.

What is Sweet clover used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places both indications under "traditional use": (1) relief of discomfort and heaviness of the legs related to minor venous circulatory disturbances; (2) treatment of minor inflammations of the skin. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no randomised clinical trial of sweet clover on its own; human studies measure multi-ingredient products containing it (combinations with diosmin and bromelain, for instance).

Is Sweet clover safe?

Safety label: caution. Not for anyone taking anticoagulants (blood thinners). Not for anyone with liver disease or a history of it. Those two sentences in the Turkish monograph follow from the plant’s coumarin chemistry and should be read together: clotting factors are made in the liver and depend on vitamin K. Hypersensitivity to the active constituents can develop. Gastrointestinal complaints have been reported with oral use and allergic reactions with topical use; frequencies unknown. If cardiac or renal insufficiency, inflammation of the skin, thrombophlebitis, varicose veins, numbness or sudden swelling of the legs appear, see a doctor at once — these can signal conditions far more serious than heavy legs. Not used under 18; not recommended in pregnancy or lactation.

Traditional use

Europe — two indications

The EMA monograph places both indications under "traditional use": (1) relief of discomfort and heaviness of the legs related to minor venous circulatory disturbances; (2) treatment of minor inflammations of the skin.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the aerial parts" and sets two firm limits: people with liver disease, or a history of it, and people taking anticoagulants must not use it. Read together, those two contraindications describe the plant’s chemistry.

Anatolia

Sweet clover is common along field margins, roadsides and waste ground in Turkey, its small yellow pea flowers set in spikes. The sweet hay scent it gives off on drying is what names it.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to Europe, North Africa and Asia, now spread through the temperate world. Common in Turkey.
Harvest season
Spikes of small yellow flowers from May to September; the aerial parts are gathered in flower and dried.
Local names
Kokulu yonca, taş yoncası, sarı taş yoncası — all three appear in the Turkish monograph.

Mix-up risk

White-flowered Melilotus albus of the same genus grows alongside it in Turkey and looks much the same; the EU monograph covers M. officinalis only. The Turkish word "yonca" is also used for Medicago and Trifolium species, which are different genera. The most important practical point, though, is not the species but the condition: mouldy hay is dangerous and should not be used as animal feed either.

Preparation

The monograph describes oral and topical preparations of the aerial parts. If symptoms do not resolve, or adverse effects appear, consult a doctor. Not used under 18.

What science says

There is no randomised clinical trial of sweet clover on its own; human studies measure multi-ingredient products containing it (combinations with diosmin and bromelain, for instance). Such a trial measures the mixture, not any single plant. The EU monograph rests both indications on long use. The plant’s chemistry, by contrast, is very well known, and it holds a place of its own in the history of medicine. The substance that gives it its scent is coumarin; coumarin itself does not thin the blood, but if the hay goes mouldy the mould converts coumarin into dicoumarol, and dicoumarol blocks an enzyme needed to activate vitamin K — so the blood cannot clot. That explains why the Turkish monograph sets its anticoagulant and liver contraindications: clotting factors are made in the liver and depend on vitamin K. The evidence level is Traditional.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Anticoagulants and coumarin-containing medicinesThe Turkish monograph reports interaction with this group, and the same monograph says people on anticoagulants must not use the plant. The reason is chemical: drugs like warfarin are themselves coumarin derivatives and target the same pathway — the activation of vitamin K. Anyone on a blood thinner should not use this plant.
Other sources of coumarinNot a reported interaction but the same logic: cinnamon (cassia in particular) and some other drugs also carry coumarin. The total load is worth accounting for, especially where the liver is concerned.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing; the Turkish one reports one group and adds a contraindication.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Sweet clover.
