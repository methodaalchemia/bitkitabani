Self-heal

Prunella vulgaris L.
Evidence: LimitedGenerally safe

Its Chinese name xiakucao, according to the review by Pan and colleagues, comes from the plant flowering in spring and withering after summer. The names self-heal and heal-all in Western languages rest on its reputation as a wound herb in European folk medicine.

Other names
English self-heal, heal-all, common selfheal; Chinese xiakucao (夏枯草). The drug name in Chinese medicine is Prunellae Spica (the dried fruit spike). According to Kew’s plant list its native range covers Europe, Türkiye, North Africa, the temperate parts of Asia and North America. There is no EMA, WHO or TİTCK monograph; it is listed in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2020).

In short

What is Self-heal?

Self-heal (Prunella vulgaris L.) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: Europe, Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Asia and Siberia, North America. Part used: in chinese medicine the dried fruit spike; in europe the flowering aerial parts.

What is Self-heal used for?

Traditional use: A 2022 review (Pan et al.) reports that the plant was first mentioned in the Shennong materia medica, dated to the Eastern Han period (AD 25–220), and recommended for scrofula, goitre and head sores. Scientific evidence: limited. Human studies of self-heal are concentrated on thyroid diseases, and in all of them the plant was given not alone but alongside the standard drug.

Is Self-heal safe?

Safety label: generally safe. According to the review by Pan and colleagues the plant is not regarded as toxic and reports of adverse effects are rare; one case of contact dermatitis is on record. The same review writes that excessive use may irritate the stomach and bowel and cause diarrhoea and abdominal pain. In the thyroid studies the plant affected hormone levels; people with thyroid disease and those taking thyroid medication should not use it without consulting their doctor. There are no safety data on use in pregnancy, breastfeeding or children; it should not be used in these periods. The review also notes that adverse effects need to be monitored more carefully in the clinic. Anyone on regular medication should consult their doctor or pharmacist.

Traditional use

Chinese medicine — xiakucao

A 2022 review (Pan et al.) reports that the plant was first mentioned in the Shennong materia medica, dated to the Eastern Han period (AD 25–220), and recommended for scrofula, goitre and head sores. The 2020 edition of the Chinese Pharmacopoeia lists the drug for red and painful eyes, headache and vertigo, breast abscess, scrofula and high blood pressure. These are uses based on the concepts of traditional Chinese medicine itself.

Europe, Türkiye, Iran and Japan — folk medicine

According to the review by Pan and colleagues, in 17th-century European folk medicine the plant was known as “self-healing” and was used to soothe sore throat, reduce fever and speed up wound healing. The same review writes that the plant is used in Turkish folk medicine for rheumatism, colds and heart complaints; in Iran for sore throat, bleeding, headache, dysentery and eye inflammation; and in Japan as a diuretic.

Southern China — a cooling herbal tea

According to the review, drinking herbal tea is part of the everyday diet in hot and humid Guangdong province, and self-heal is among the ingredients of ready-made herbal teas on the market. In March 2010 China’s National Health Commission added the plant to the list of plants regarded as “both medicine and food”, with a limited amount and range of use.

In nature

Where it grows
Sunny, well-drained sandy loam; dry slopes, grasslands, roadsides and field margins. It is a low, creeping, non-aromatic perennial herb 5–30 cm tall with a square stem; its leaves are in opposite pairs. It is widespread in the temperate belt of the northern hemisphere.
Harvest season
Flowers in spring and early summer; in Chinese medicine the spikes are collected in summer, when they turn brownish-red.
Local names
No established local name in Turkish.

Mix-up risk

According to the review, its white-flowered (var. leucantha) and narrow-leaved (var. lanceolata) varieties are easily confused with the typical plant. Unlike most of the mint family it has no scent.

Preparation

The traditional form is a decoction or infusion (tea) of the dried spikes or the aerial parts. In Chinese medicine the spikes are collected in summer, when they turn brownish-red, and dried in the sun. The review by Pan and colleagues reports that the medicinal dose in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia is generally 9–15 g. There is no EMA, WHO or TİTCK monograph; therefore there is no accepted dose in Europe or Türkiye. What was used in the clinical studies was not tea but ready-made preparations manufactured in China.

What science says

Human studies of self-heal are concentrated on thyroid diseases, and in all of them the plant was given not alone but alongside the standard drug. A 2021 meta-analysis (Han et al.) pooled 13 randomised controlled trials and 1468 patients in thyroid nodules; it found that the preparation given together with levothyroxine increased the clinical efficacy rate (risk ratio 1.22) and reduced nodule diameter. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis (Wei et al.) examined 17 studies and 1366 patients in hyperthyroidism; it reported that the preparation added to the thyroid drug lowered hormone levels and the frequency of adverse events more than the drug alone, but that the strength of evidence was low and the result needs to be confirmed by higher-quality trials. All of the studies were carried out in China. The review by Pan and colleagues reports that more than 250 compounds have been identified from the plant, while the effects on inflammation, tumours, viruses and blood pressure have been shown at the cell and animal level.

Safety

Generally safe

According to the review by Pan and colleagues the plant is not regarded as toxic and reports of adverse effects are rare; one case of contact dermatitis is on record. The same review writes that excessive use may irritate the stomach and bowel and cause diarrhoea and abdominal pain. In the thyroid studies the plant affected hormone levels; people with thyroid disease and those taking thyroid medication should not use it without consulting their doctor. There are no safety data on use in pregnancy, breastfeeding or children; it should not be used in these periods. The review also notes that adverse effects need to be monitored more carefully in the clinic. Anyone on regular medication should consult their doctor or pharmacist.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Thyroid medicines (levothyroxine, antithyroid drugs)In the meta-analyses, preparations of the plant changed thyroid hormone levels when given together with these drugs. Adding it on one’s own may upset the adjustment of the drug dose; medical monitoring is needed.
Blood pressure and diabetes medicines (theoretical)The review reports blood pressure- and blood sugar-lowering effects in animal and cell studies; there is no interaction study in humans.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Self-heal.
