Alder buckthorn

Rhamnus frangula L.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

Fresh bark is not used; it must be dried or heated first. The reason is chemical: the anthrone forms in fresh bark cause severe nausea and vomiting, and storage or heat converts them into gentler forms. Drying here is not a way of keeping the bark but the preparation itself.

What is Alder buckthorn?

Alder buckthorn (Rhamnus frangula L.) is a plant of the Rhamnaceae (cehrigiller) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the stem and branch bark.

What is Alder buckthorn used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places the bark under "well-established use" and keeps the indication narrow: short-term use in occasional constipation. Scientific evidence: limited. What separates the regulatory category from the independent literature here is not whether there is an effect but which plant it was shown for.

Is Alder buckthorn safe?

Safety label: caution. Contraindicated in intestinal obstruction and stenosis, atony, appendicitis, inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn’s, ulcerative colitis), abdominal pain of unknown origin, and severe dehydration with water and electrolyte depletion. Not used under 12. Not to be used in pregnancy or lactation, because of the genotoxicity risk of some anthranoids. Use beyond two weeks is not recommended; prolonged use can disturb water and electrolyte balance and lead to albuminuria and haematuria, and potassium loss can cause muscle weakness and cardiac arrhythmia. Chronic use may cause pigmentation of the intestinal mucosa (pseudomelanosis coli), which usually recedes on stopping. It can colour the urine yellow or red-brown and distort biochemical test results. Anyone taking cardiac glycosides, antiarrhythmics, QT-prolonging medicines, diuretics, corticosteroids or liquorice root should consult a doctor first. The Turkish monograph also requires supervision by a doctor or pharmacist trained in herbal treatment.

Traditional use

Europe — short-term laxative

The EMA monograph places the bark under "well-established use" and keeps the indication narrow: short-term use in occasional constipation. The active compounds are hydroxyanthracene derivatives, calculated as glucofrangulin A, which stimulate the bowel wall.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "stem and branch bark" and its warning list is longer than the EMA one: not to be used for more than two weeks, not recommended under 12, not to be used in pregnancy or lactation because of the genotoxicity risk of some anthranoids, and to be used under the supervision of a doctor or pharmacist trained in herbal treatment.

European Union — medicine and food supplement treated differently

The line between medicine and food supplement is sharp for this plant. As a medicine its monograph is in force. On the food side, the European Food Safety Authority stated in 2018 that it could not set a safe daily intake for hydroxyanthracene derivatives; evidence of genotoxicity was found for emodin, aloe-emodin and the related substance danthron, and epidemiological data suggesting a link between laxative use and colorectal cancer was noted. Regulation (EU) 2021/468 then prohibited aloe-emodin, emodin and danthron in food, and placed preparations from the bark of Rhamnus frangula containing hydroxyanthracene derivatives under Union scrutiny. The distinction matters: this is not a ban on the medicine. What is restricted is taking it as a food supplement, without a time limit.

Where the Turkish name comes from

Barut ağacı means "gunpowder tree" and has nothing to do with medicine: charcoal from its wood was among the most sought after for making black powder.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to Europe, western Asia and North Africa. In Turkey it grows in the moist woodland and streamsides of northern Anatolia. Unlike its relative the buckthorn, it has no thorns.
Harvest season
The bark is stripped in spring when the sap is rising, then stored for at least a year.
Local names
Barut ağacı, akdiken, cehri. The naming is muddled: "cehri" is used for other Rhamnus species too.

Mix-up risk

It can be confused with Rhamnus purshiana (cascara) of the same genus; both are anthranoid laxatives and both are on the same EU scrutiny list. The riskier confusion is insufficiently aged bark: fresh bark of the same plant causes severe nausea.

Preparation

The monograph gives a maximum daily dose of 30 mg of hydroxyanthracene glycosides, and describes a preparation equivalent to 10–30 mg calculated as glucofrangulin A, taken once daily at night, for adolescents over 12, adults and the elderly. The correct dose is the smallest one that produces a comfortable soft-formed motion — that is the monograph’s own wording. Taking it two or three times a week is normally enough. Use beyond one to two weeks requires medical supervision. Not used under 12. Fresh bark is not used: it must be dried or heat-treated first, or it causes severe nausea and vomiting.

What science says

What separates the regulatory category from the independent literature here is not whether there is an effect but which plant it was shown for. The EMA treats alder buckthorn bark as well-established use. The 2021 updated systematic review of over-the-counter therapies for chronic constipation (41 randomised trials, American Journal of Gastroenterology) gives good evidence (grade A) among the anthranoid laxatives to senna alone, alongside polyethylene glycol. Alder buckthorn and rhubarb are not assessed separately there. The mechanism is established for the class, but the randomised evidence comes through the leading member of that class rather than through this plant. The evidence level is therefore Limited rather than Clinical.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Cardiac glycosidesThe potassium loss caused by prolonged laxative use potentiates the action of cardiac glycosides. This is not a direct interaction between two substances: the laxative lowers potassium, and low potassium makes the heart medicine dangerous.
Antiarrhythmics and QT-prolonging medicinesThe same potassium loss interacts with antiarrhythmic medicines and with medicines that restore sinus rhythm (quinidine, for example), and raises the risk alongside QT-prolonging drugs.
Other potassium-lowering agents: diuretics, corticosteroids, liquorice rootThe monograph names all three. Each causes potassium loss, and together with a laxative the electrolyte imbalance deepens. Liquorice root being on this list is worth noting — it is a herbal product itself.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Alder buckthorn.
