Wild thyme

Thymus serpyllum L.
Evidence: LimitedGenerally safe

According to the review by Jarić and colleagues, the species name serpyllum comes from the Greek word meaning “to creep”, a reference to the plant’s trailing stems. The same review reports that in the Middle Ages women gave knights thyme leaves as a gift to bring them courage.

Other names
English wild thyme, creeping thyme, Breckland thyme; the pharmaceutical drug name is Serpylli herba (Herba Thymi serpylli). It belongs to the same genus as garden thyme (Thymus vulgaris) but is a separate species. According to Kew’s plant list its native range runs from northern and central Europe to West Siberia; Türkiye is not within the native range on that list. In publications from India and Pakistan the same name is also used for thymes growing in the Himalaya.

In short

What is Wild thyme?

Wild thyme (Thymus serpyllum L.) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: Europe, Asia and Siberia. Part used: flowering aerial parts (fresh or dried); the essential oil.

What is Wild thyme used for?

Traditional use: The flowering tops of wild thyme have been used in European folk medicine mostly for respiratory and digestive complaints. Scientific evidence: limited. Most studies on wild thyme are at the cell and animal level.

Is Wild thyme safe?

Safety label: generally safe. It is well tolerated in the amounts used as a spice and a tea. The 2024 systematic review writes that there are no known contraindications, side effects or drug interactions, and that the WHO adverse-effect database holds only one report (2011, acute kidney injury in a woman over 75). In the 2026 clinical trial no product-related adverse events were reported over 8 weeks. There are no safety data on medicinal doses in pregnancy, breastfeeding or children; in these periods culinary amounts should not be exceeded. The essential oil is a concentrated product: it is not taken by mouth and not applied undiluted to the skin. People allergic to the mint family (thyme, mint, sage) should be careful. Anyone on regular medication should consult their doctor or pharmacist.

Traditional use

Europe — a herb for cough and colds

The flowering tops of wild thyme have been used in European folk medicine mostly for respiratory and digestive complaints. A 2015 review (Jarić et al.) reports that the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia lists bronchitis, bronchial catarrh, sore throat and especially whooping cough as indications for the plant. According to the same review the plant is a component of ready-made antitussive mixtures and of drops for colds. These are traditional uses; they do not amount to proven effect.

The Balkans and the Alps — calming tea, rheumatism

The review by Jarić and colleagues reports that in the Western Balkans the plant is used as a sedative and to support blood circulation. In the Alpine part of north-eastern Italy an infusion or decoction of the flowering aerial parts is drunk for rheumatism. Externally it appears as an antiseptic for wounds, eczema and swelling. In some parts of Italy it is also used to flavour meat and fish dishes.

Middle Ages and Renaissance — written records

According to the same review, the Chilandar Medical Codex, dated to the 15th–16th centuries, mentions wild thyme for headache caused by colds, laryngitis and diseases of the digestive organs, and as an antitussive. In medieval Europe it was placed beneath pillows to aid sleep and keep nightmares away. Internal use for malaria and epilepsy is also recorded for the Renaissance period; these uses have no support today.

Himalayan foothills — a thyme under the same name

A 2023 review (Salaria et al.) writes that the plant is used traditionally for respiratory and digestive complaints in the upland parts of India. Jarić and colleagues report its use for menstrual disorders in some regions of India and against intestinal worms in northern Pakistan. Whether the plants in these publications are the same as the European species is botanically disputed.

In nature

Where it grows
Dry, stony ground, sandy heaths and grasslands. It is a small, prostrate perennial shrub with a stem woody at the base; its leaves are 4–6 mm long. Its native range is northern and central Europe and West Siberia.
Harvest season
Flowers from May to September; the flowering tops are gathered in this period.
Local names
No established local name in Turkish.

Mix-up risk

Garden thyme (Thymus vulgaris) is a separate species and the EMA monograph belongs to that species; wild thyme creeps along the ground. Thymus is a taxonomically difficult genus; products sold as “wild thyme” in different countries may be other Thymus species.

Preparation

The traditional forms are an infusion (tea) of the flowering tops, a decoction, a syrup and a tincture. The EMA’s herbal committee (HMPC) has no monograph for wild thyme; therefore there is no dose accepted in an official monograph. The review by Jarić and colleagues reports that the reference book PDR for Herbal Medicines recommends 4–6 g of the drug per day; this is handbook information, not an official monograph dose. According to the same review the European Pharmacopoeia requires at least 1.2% essential oil in the drug and at least 40% carvacrol plus thymol in the oil. The essential oil is not taken by mouth.

What science says

Most studies on wild thyme are at the cell and animal level. The 2015 review by Jarić and colleagues attributes the laboratory activity of the essential oil against bacteria and moulds to carvacrol and thymol; the oil content varies between 0.1 and 1% depending on the source, and there are many chemotypes. In a rat study reported in the same review an aqueous extract (100 mg/kg) lowered blood pressure in hypertensive rats and did not affect it in normal rats. A 2024 systematic review (Jalil et al.) states that effects on intestinal inflammation, liver protection and metabolism were seen predominantly in rodent models, that clinical trials are few, and that the composition of most extracts was poorly characterised. In a multicentre, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial published in 2026, 178 adults with digestive discomfort took a standardised wild thyme extract or placebo for 8 weeks; the total symptom score fell more in the extract group than with placebo. This is a single trial, and some of its authors are linked to the extract’s manufacturer; the result needs confirmation by independent studies.

Safety

Generally safe

It is well tolerated in the amounts used as a spice and a tea. The 2024 systematic review writes that there are no known contraindications, side effects or drug interactions, and that the WHO adverse-effect database holds only one report (2011, acute kidney injury in a woman over 75). In the 2026 clinical trial no product-related adverse events were reported over 8 weeks. There are no safety data on medicinal doses in pregnancy, breastfeeding or children; in these periods culinary amounts should not be exceeded. The essential oil is a concentrated product: it is not taken by mouth and not applied undiluted to the skin. People allergic to the mint family (thyme, mint, sage) should be careful. Anyone on regular medication should consult their doctor or pharmacist.
