In short

What is Juniper?

Juniper (Juniperus excelsa) is a plant of the Cupressaceae family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Asia and Siberia. Part used: twigs, berries, tar.

What is Juniper used for?

Traditional use: Yörük nomads burn juniper twigs to smudge the home, the fold and the sick. Scientific evidence: traditional. The EMA recognises juniper berry as a mild diuretic and digestive aid as traditional uses.

Is Juniper safe?

Safety label: caution. Long-term or high-dose use of the berries can irritate the kidneys. Avoid in pregnancy and kidney disease. Juniper tar must not be swallowed.

Traditional use

Anatolia

Yörük nomads burn juniper twigs to smudge the home, the fold and the sick. Cade oil from prickly juniper is applied to scabies, eczema and animal wounds.

Central Asia

In Kyrgyz, Kazakh and Mongol shamanism, arça smoke drives away evil spirits; places and people are cleansed with it before ceremonies.

In nature

Where it grows
Dry mountains of the Taurus and Central and Southern Anatolia; Greek juniper climbs to about 2,000 m.
Harvest season
Berries in autumn and winter; twigs all year.
Local names
ardıç, boylu ardıç, katran ardıcı, andız, arça

Mix-up risk

Some junipers (such as Juniperus sabina) are poisonous. Never use internally without knowing the exact species.

Preparation

Twigs are burned as incense; berries are steeped; tar is applied externally.

What science says

The EMA recognises juniper berry as a mild diuretic and digestive aid as traditional uses. Antimicrobial effects have been seen in the lab.

Safety

Caution

Long-term or high-dose use of the berries can irritate the kidneys. Avoid in pregnancy and kidney disease. Juniper tar must not be swallowed.
