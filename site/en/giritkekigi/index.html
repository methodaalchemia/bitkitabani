BT-237 · Lamiaceae (mint family)

Dittany of Crete

Origanum dictamnus L.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

In ancient sources "dictamnus" is the plant that heals arrow wounds; the 2015 ethnopharmacological analysis shows the name covered plants from at least three families over the centuries, and that the plant behind European use was most likely not dittany of Crete but Dictamnus albus of the rue family. So the "long-standing use" the EU monograph approves is partly the legacy of a confusion of names. This plant is an example of how a legend can attach to the wrong plant — and how even a regulator can inherit that legacy.

Other names
The TİTCK monograph gives the Turkish name as "Girit kekiği"; its synonyms are Amaracus dictamnus Benth. and Majorana dictamnus (L.) Kostel. In English dittany of Crete, Cretan dittany, hop marjoram. Greek díktamo. ⚠️ The kekik record in our catalog deals with the genus Origanum in general; this record is only for the monograph of this species endemic to Crete.

What is Dittany of Crete?

Dittany of Crete (Origanum dictamnus L.) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: Europe, Anatolia and the Mediterranean. Part used: the flowering aerial parts.

What is Dittany of Crete used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places all three under "traditional use": (1) relief of cough associated with cold; (2) relief of mild gastrointestinal disorders; (3) minor skin inflammations and bruises — as a wet dressing. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no clinical trial of dittany in humans; recent publications concern essential-oil composition and laboratory work.

Is Dittany of Crete safe?

Safety label: caution. See a doctor if dyspnoea, high fever or purulent sputum occurs — the red line of the cough indication. Not for anyone hypersensitive to the mint family (mint, thyme, lavender, sage). Not orally under 18 or cutaneously under 12; not recommended in pregnancy or lactation. For the skin indication, see a doctor if no better in a week or if signs of infection appear. No adverse effects known; no interaction data.

Traditional use

Europe — three indications

The EMA monograph places all three under "traditional use": (1) relief of cough associated with cold; (2) relief of mild gastrointestinal disorders; (3) minor skin inflammations and bruises — as a wet dressing. The first two as tea (4.5–21 g daily), the third as a decoction of 30–75 g per litre.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph follows the EU one exactly: hypersensitivity to the mint family; not orally under 18 or cutaneously under 12; see a doctor if dyspnoea, high fever or purulent sputum occurs. Interaction section: "insufficient data".

Crete — plant of the cliffs

Dittany is an endemic that grows only on the limestone cliffs of Crete; its soft woolly leaves and pink bracts set it apart from other oreganos at once. It counts as a symbol of the island; because gathering it means dangerous cliffs, the gatherers have a word of their own (erondades). It does not grow in Turkey; the Origanum species on the Turkish side of the Aegean are different species.

In nature

Where it grows
Endemic to Crete; limestone cliffs and rock crevices. Cultivated. It does not grow in Turkey.
Harvest season
Pink-bracted flowers in midsummer; the flowering aerial parts are gathered then.
Local names
Girit kekiği — the Turkish monograph gives one name; Greek díktamo, érontas.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ The name "dictamnus" has been confused through history with burning bush (Dictamnus albus, Rutaceae) and Ballota species; the Turkish word "kekik" likewise covers Origanum, Thymus and Satureja at once. The monograph is for O. dictamnus only.

Preparation

As tea: 1.5–7 g of the comminuted drug in 150 ml of boiling water, steeped 2–4 minutes, three times daily. Cutaneous: 30–75 g of the drug in 1 l of water as infusion or decoction, applied as a wet dressing 2–5 times daily. See a doctor if no better within a week for cough, two weeks for digestion, a week for skin. Not orally under 18, not cutaneously under 12.

What science says

There is no clinical trial of dittany in humans; recent publications concern essential-oil composition and laboratory work. The EU monograph rests all three indications on long use, and that long use is documented: a historical and ethnopharmacological analysis published in 2015 in the Journal of Ethnopharmacology shows plants called "dictamnus" used since the 4th century BCE for gynaecological and other problems, entering complex preparations for 35 diseases between 1000 and 1700 CE. But the same study carries a warning: "dictamnus" is not one plant but a plant complex — alongside dittany of Crete, Dictamnus albus (Rutaceae) and Ballota species went by the name, and in the authors’ view the plant behind most European use was probably not the Cretan endemic but the widespread D. albus. So there is a long history of use, but whose it is is not certain in every record. The evidence level is Traditional.
