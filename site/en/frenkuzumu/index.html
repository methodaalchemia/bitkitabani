Blackcurrant

Ribes nigrum L.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

The third plant in this catalog (after strawberry and oat) whose fruit everyone knows but which stands on the official list by its leaf. Blackcurrant leaf has been drunk as "cassis" tea for joint pain in France for centuries, and the EU monograph approves exactly that use. One of its Turkish names, "karagat", carries the plant’s migration route: coming from Russian and Caucasian languages, it says the fruit entered Anatolia from the north, by way of the Black Sea.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the names "siyah frenk üzümü, frenk üzümü, karagat" and lists no synonyms. In English: blackcurrant. The monograph’s drug is not the fruit but the leaf; the fruit (jam, syrup, liqueur) and the seed oil are separate products outside the monograph.

What is Blackcurrant?

Blackcurrant (Ribes nigrum L.) is a plant of the Grossulariaceae (currant family) family. Traditions that use it: Europe, Asia and Siberia. Part used: the leaves.

What is Blackcurrant used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places both indications under "traditional use": (1) relief of minor articular pain; (2) to increase the amount of urine to achieve flushing of the urinary tract, as an adjuvant in minor urinary complaints. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no clinical trial of blackcurrant leaf in humans; recent publications are laboratory screens of leaf and fruit extracts and agricultural genetics.

Is Blackcurrant safe?

Safety label: caution. Joint pain accompanied by swelling, redness or fever should be examined by a doctor — it may signal inflammatory joint disease and should not be managed with a herbal tea. Not for use in serious cardiac or renal disease where fluid intake is restricted. In urinary use, see a doctor if fever, painful urination, spasms or blood in the urine appear. Not recommended under 18; not to be used in pregnancy or lactation (Turkish monograph, firmly). Hypersensitivity to the active substance can occur.

Traditional use

Europe — two indications

The EMA monograph places both indications under "traditional use": (1) relief of minor articular pain; (2) to increase the amount of urine to achieve flushing of the urinary tract, as an adjuvant in minor urinary complaints. The limit on the joint indication: articular pain accompanied by swelling, redness or fever should be examined by a doctor.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph lists the leaf and gives one interaction in firm terms: it must not be used together with the diuretics used in heart failure and kidney disease. Contraindicated where fluid intake is restricted; not recommended under 18; not to be used in pregnancy or lactation.

Northern Europe

Blackcurrant is native to northern and central Europe and Siberia; its fruit is part of the kitchen in Britain and in France (cassis). In Turkey it is grown in the Black Sea region; the name "karagat" is of Russian/Caucasian origin and says the plant came to Anatolia from the north.

In nature

Where it grows
Northern and central Europe, Siberia; it wants a cool, damp climate. Grown in the Black Sea region of Turkey.
Harvest season
The leaves are gathered and dried in spring and early summer, before the fruit ripens; the fruit ripens in July.
Local names
Siyah frenk üzümü, frenk üzümü, karagat — all three appear in the Turkish monograph.

Mix-up risk

In Turkish "frenk üzümü" also covers redcurrant (Ribes rubrum) and gooseberry (Ribes uva-crispa); the monograph is for the black species (R. nigrum) only. And the drug is the leaf; fruit, syrup and seed-oil products fall outside the monograph.

Preparation

The EU monograph gives forms for adults. For joint pain: 2–4 g of the comminuted leaf infused in 200 ml of boiling water, three times daily; dry extract (DER 7:1, water) 170 mg 1–3 times daily; powdered drug 340 mg 3–5 times daily. For the urinary tract, the same extract and powder doses; in that indication plenty of fluid is essential. If joint pain persists longer than four weeks, consult a doctor. Not recommended under 18.

What science says

There is no clinical trial of blackcurrant leaf in humans; recent publications are laboratory screens of leaf and fruit extracts and agricultural genetics. Human studies do exist on the fruit and the seed oil — anthocyanins, eye fatigue, the gamma-linolenic acid of the seed oil — but they do not belong to the leaf, which is the monograph’s drug; this catalog’s rule is not to carry one part’s data over to another. The EU monograph rests both indications on long use. The evidence level is Traditional.

Safety

Caution

Joint pain accompanied by swelling, redness or fever should be examined by a doctor — it may signal inflammatory joint disease and should not be managed with a herbal tea. Not for use in serious cardiac or renal disease where fluid intake is restricted. In urinary use, see a doctor if fever, painful urination, spasms or blood in the urine appear. Not recommended under 18; not to be used in pregnancy or lactation (Turkish monograph, firmly). Hypersensitivity to the active substance can occur.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
DiureticsThe Turkish monograph says it must not be used together with the diuretics used in heart failure and kidney disease. The logic is plain: a diuretic tea stacked on a diuretic drug can upset fluid and electrolyte balance — precisely where the patients taking those drugs are most fragile.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing in its interaction section; the Turkish one reports a group in firm terms.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Blackcurrant.
