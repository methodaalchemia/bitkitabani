In short

What is Water mint?

Water mint (Mentha aquatica L.) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: Europe, Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Asia and Siberia, Africa. Part used: aerial parts (leaves and flowering tops); the essential oil.

What is Water mint used for?

Traditional use: A 2024 critical review (Pires et al.) writes that water mint has long been used in folk medicine, mainly for respiratory complaints such as the common cold. Scientific evidence: limited. There are no clinical studies of water mint in humans; the data are at the cell and animal level.

Is Water mint safe?

Safety label: generally safe. No serious known harm has been reported at culinary and tea amounts. In the 2026 study from Morocco no deaths or signs of poisoning were seen when the decocted extract was given to mice by mouth at up to 8 g/kg; at doses of up to 1 g/kg a day for 28 days there were no changes in blood, biochemistry or tissue findings. This is animal data; there are no safety studies in humans. There are no data on medicinal use in pregnancy, breastfeeding or children; in these periods culinary amounts should not be exceeded. The essential oil is a concentrated product; it is not taken by mouth and not applied undiluted to the skin. Plants gathered from the water’s edge may carry microbes and heavy metals from dirty water; they should be gathered from a clean source and washed well. Anyone on regular medication should consult their doctor.

Traditional use

Europe and the Mediterranean — a mint for colds and the stomach

A 2024 critical review (Pires et al.) writes that water mint has long been used in folk medicine, mainly for respiratory complaints such as the common cold. A 2024 study from Algeria reports that the plant is used around the world as a folk remedy and functional food for digestive and lung complaints and for certain mental and nervous conditions such as epilepsy and depression. These are traditional uses.

Iran — a wild vegetable and kitchen herb

A 2021 review (Asadollah-Pour et al.) describes water mint as a wild vegetable and culinary herb. According to the same review the plant has been used among the people as a tonic and a sedative, and for constipation, diarrhoea, stomach and liver complaints, biliousness, blood pressure problems and to expel intestinal worms in children. The review records these uses; it does not confirm their effectiveness.

Brazil and Morocco — digestion and pain

A 2022 study carried out in Brazil notes that the plant is popularly used for digestive problems and tests this use in animal models. A 2026 study from Morocco, in turn, examines a decocted extract of the aerial parts in models of inflammation and pain on the basis of traditional use.

In nature

Where it grows
The shallow margins of streams, rivers and channels, and wet meadows. It is a perennial. Its native range is Europe, Türkiye, the Caucasus, Iran, North Africa, and eastern and southern Africa; it was later carried to the Americas.
Harvest season
Flowers in the summer months; in the study carried out in Algeria the flowering plant was collected in August.
Local names
No established local name in Turkish.

Mix-up risk

It is confused with the other mint species (Mentha); within the genus the species resemble one another and the essential oil composition varies with the source. A carvone-dominated chemotype has been reported from Brazil, and in another study a chemotype dominated by linalool and linalyl acetate. Plants growing at the water’s edge that do not smell of mint should not be gathered.

Preparation

The traditional forms are an infusion (tea) and a decoction of the fresh or dried leaves; the leaves are used in the kitchen like mint. Because there is no EMA, WHO or TİTCK monograph, there is no accepted dose. The essential oil is obtained by steam distillation; the yield in the Brazilian study was 0.17%. The essential oil is not taken by mouth.

What science says

There are no clinical studies of water mint in humans; the data are at the cell and animal level. In the 2022 study from Brazil the essential oil, with carvone as its main constituent (75 mg/kg), gave more than 90% protection against alcohol-induced gastric ulcer in rats and reduced the volume and acidity of gastric secretion; it was not protective, however, against acute colitis in mice. In the 2026 study from Morocco the decocted extract showed dose-dependent anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects in rats at 200 and 400 mg/kg. In a 2019 cell study the extract (50 μg/mL) protected human liver cancer cells against oxidative damage; in the Algerian study 57 compounds were identified and the extracts showed antioxidant activity and α-glucosidase inhibition in the test tube. The review by Pires and colleagues stresses that many cosmetic products containing the plant are on the market, whereas studies for medicinal use are still insufficient.

Safety

Generally safe

No serious known harm has been reported at culinary and tea amounts. In the 2026 study from Morocco no deaths or signs of poisoning were seen when the decocted extract was given to mice by mouth at up to 8 g/kg; at doses of up to 1 g/kg a day for 28 days there were no changes in blood, biochemistry or tissue findings. This is animal data; there are no safety studies in humans. There are no data on medicinal use in pregnancy, breastfeeding or children; in these periods culinary amounts should not be exceeded. The essential oil is a concentrated product; it is not taken by mouth and not applied undiluted to the skin. Plants gathered from the water’s edge may carry microbes and heavy metals from dirty water; they should be gathered from a clean source and washed well. Anyone on regular medication should consult their doctor.
