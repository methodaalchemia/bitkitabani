Turkish oregano

Origanum onites L.
Evidence: LimitedGenerally safe

In English it is known by the name “Turkish oregano”. According to Kew’s world plant checklist its native range extends from Sicily to Türkiye; it also covers Crete, Greece and the East Aegean islands.

Other names
English Turkish oregano; in Turkish publications İzmir kekiği. Its synonym is Majorana onites (L.) Benth. (Kew world plant checklist). There is no separate monograph for this species on the EMA or TİTCK lists; this record rests on one review, one clinical study carried out in Türkiye and laboratory studies.
Part used
Leaves and flowering branches (aerial parts); the essential oil and the aqueous distillate
Region
Anatolia and the Mediterranean
Europe
Use

In short

What is Turkish oregano?

Turkish oregano (Origanum onites L.) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: leaves and flowering branches (aerial parts); the essential oil and the aqueous distillate.

What is Turkish oregano used for?

Traditional use: According to a 2025 paper in the Mustafa Kemal University Journal of Agricultural Sciences, Turkish oregano is the one with the highest export volume among the thyme and oregano species cultivated or collected from the wild in Türkiye. Scientific evidence: limited. The main constituent of the essential oil is carvacrol: in the 2022 study carvacrol was found at 65.5–91 per cent, linalool at 0.3–17.9 per cent, γ-terpinene at 2.8–4.6 per cent and thymol at 0.5–1.5 per cent.

Is Turkish oregano safe?

Safety label: generally safe. No serious problem has been reported with use as a spice and tea. The 2016 review writes that no significant toxicity or mutagenicity was seen in Drosophila and Salmonella tests, but that safety needs to be tested separately in mammalian cells. Allergy is possible: in a case reported from Türkiye a 13-year-old child developed angioedema with Turkish oregano, sage and mint; the authors noted that cross-sensitivity is common within the mint family. It has been reported that anaemia was common among Taurus villagers who consume thyme excessively. There are no safety data for medicinal use in pregnancy, breastfeeding or children. The sources contain no human safety data on oral use of the pure essential oil; it is appropriate for people taking cholesterol or diabetes medication not to use concentrated products without consulting a doctor.

Traditional use

Türkiye — the most exported oregano

According to a 2025 paper in the Mustafa Kemal University Journal of Agricultural Sciences, Turkish oregano is the one with the highest export volume among the thyme and oregano species cultivated or collected from the wild in Türkiye. The plant is both collected from the wild and grown in fields. A 2022 study in Chemistry & Biodiversity writes that the species is used as a culinary spice and as a herbal tea.

Anatolia — folk medicine

A 2016 review (Chemistry & Biodiversity), surveying publications between 1988 and 2014, reports that the plant is traditionally used in conditions such as gastrointestinal disorders, diabetes, high cholesterol and bronchitis. These are records of traditional use; the review does not say that efficacy has been proven.

Taurus Mountains — an everyday tea and food herb

It has been reported that villagers on the outskirts of the Taurus Mountains who make their living from thyme add it to their tea and to many of their dishes (Toxicology and Industrial Health). The same paper reports that anaemia was common among these villagers, and examines the species Origanum onites, Thymbra spicata and Satureja cuneifolia together under the name “thyme”.

In nature

Where it grows
Its native range is Sicily, Greece, Crete, the East Aegean islands and Türkiye; it was introduced later to Cyprus and Tunisia (Kew). In Türkiye it is collected from the wild and cultivated. In the fertiliser trial under Çukurova conditions plant height was found to be 29.06–32.5 cm and dry herb yield 51.94–81.11 kg per decare.
Harvest season
It is a perennial herbaceous plant. The sources do not give a specific harvest month; the essential oil and yield studies were carried out on the flowering aerial parts.
Local names
İzmir kekiği; among the people simply kekik. No other local name is given in the sources.

Mix-up risk

In Türkiye the name “kekik” is used for many species from the genera Origanum, Thymus, Thymbra and Satureja; in the Taurus study three different species were referred to by the same name. The oil of Turkish oregano is rich in carvacrol (65.5–91 per cent); in some of the Satureja cuneifolia samples, on the other hand, the main constituent is thymol.

Preparation

The dried leaves and flowering branches are used as a spice or infused to make tea. Because there is no separate monograph for this species on the EMA or TİTCK lists, there is no accepted dose. In the only clinical study patients were given 25 ml of aqueous distillate after each meal for three months; this is a research protocol, not an official dose recommendation. In the field trial in Çukurova the essential oil content of the dry leaf was measured at 3.6–4.0 per cent and the carvacrol content of the oil at 74.12–81.68 per cent; the pure essential oil is therefore a very concentrated product.

What science says

The main constituent of the essential oil is carvacrol: in the 2022 study carvacrol was found at 65.5–91 per cent, linalool at 0.3–17.9 per cent, γ-terpinene at 2.8–4.6 per cent and thymol at 0.5–1.5 per cent. The 2016 review summarises that antimicrobial, antioxidant, insecticidal, antidiabetic and anti-inflammatory effects have been shown at laboratory level in extracts and in individual compounds. The only human study comes from Bursa: of 48 patients with mild hyperlipidaemia not requiring drug therapy, 32 were given the distillate for three months in addition to lifestyle and dietary advice, and 16 patients formed the control group. In the group taking the distillate HDL cholesterol rose more; LDL cholesterol, apolipoprotein B, lipoprotein(a) and high-sensitivity CRP fell more, and the measurements of artery dilatation also improved more. The study is small and its abstract does not mention randomisation or a placebo; the result has not been confirmed by another study. In rats the essential oil alleviated the learning and memory impairment induced by scopolamine; this finding cannot be carried over to humans.

Safety

Generally safe

No serious problem has been reported with use as a spice and tea. The 2016 review writes that no significant toxicity or mutagenicity was seen in Drosophila and Salmonella tests, but that safety needs to be tested separately in mammalian cells. Allergy is possible: in a case reported from Türkiye a 13-year-old child developed angioedema with Turkish oregano, sage and mint; the authors noted that cross-sensitivity is common within the mint family. It has been reported that anaemia was common among Taurus villagers who consume thyme excessively. There are no safety data for medicinal use in pregnancy, breastfeeding or children. The sources contain no human safety data on oral use of the pure essential oil; it is appropriate for people taking cholesterol or diabetes medication not to use concentrated products without consulting a doctor.
