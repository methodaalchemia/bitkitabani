Cherimoya

Annona cherimola Mill.
Evidence: TraditionalGenerally safe

The cherimoya is taken as evidence of a pre-Columbian sea voyage between Mesoamerica and the Andes: according to the 2021 study, people carried it from Mesoamerica to present-day Peru across the Pacific at least 4,700 years ago — after maize and other crops had spread by land and along the coast. The authors present it as the first known evidence of crop movement across the Pacific between the two regions; maize and other crops had spread earlier by land and along the coast.

Other names
Çerimoya in Turkish; cherimoya in English, chirimoya in Spanish. Neither the European Medicines Agency nor Türkiye’s TİTCK has a monograph for this species. The name is often said to be of Quechua origin; as that could not be confirmed against a reliable linguistic source for this page, no claim about its etymology is made here.

In short

What is Cherimoya?

Cherimoya (Annona cherimola Mill.) is a plant of the Annonaceae family. Traditions that use it: Andes, North America. Part used: ripe fruit flesh.

What is Cherimoya used for?

Traditional use: This is a fruit tree used as a food source by pre-Columbian cultures. Scientific evidence: traditional. The whole weight of this record rests on one question: does the cherimoya share the neurotoxicity file of the soursop (*Annona muricata*)? The answer lies in the detail, and this is the most important section on the page.

Is Cherimoya safe?

Safety label: generally safe. The flesh of the ripe fruit is a widely eaten food, and the safety level of this record is given for it. The seeds are not eaten. Acetogenins have been reported in this species in the seeds, roots, deciduous leaves and stems; they have not been reported in the fruit flesh. Cases of eye injury have been published following the application of seed powder to the hair as a treatment for lice — toxic keratitis, epithelial defects, punctate epithelial erosions — but the species in those cases is *Annona squamosa*, not the cherimoya; it is nonetheless reason enough to keep the seeds away from the eyes. The link with atypical parkinsonism does not belong to this species: the Guadeloupe study names *A. muricata* and *A. squamosa*, and the former is a separate record on this site. Cherimoya fruit pulp was screened by the laboratory that framed the hypothesis and found not to be a source of exposure. The file is nevertheless open: in 2014 the same laboratory detected low levels of acetogenins in a commercial alcoholic beverage made from cherimoya fruit and wrote that the species should be considered a risk factor; that study gives no concentration, and itself calls the presence of these compounds in the fruit pulp "unclear". No body has set a safe intake level or consumption limit for the cherimoya; the only Annonaceae assessment that sought one covers soursop supplements, and even there none could be established. EFSA’s compendium of botanicals does carry a genus-level "Annona spp." entry naming the cherimoya. No assessment specific to this species for pregnancy, breastfeeding or children could be found. Safety level "low", and that label is only for eating the fresh ripe fruit flesh: it does not cover the seeds, skin or leaves, and it does not cover concentrated, fermented or otherwise processed products or supplements — the one direct measurement made in this species’ edible chain came out positive, in a beverage made from the fruit.

Traditional use

The Andes and Mesoamerica — a pre-Columbian fruit

This is a fruit tree used as a food source by pre-Columbian cultures. A 2012 diversity mapping in PLoS ONE places the highest allelic richness and expected heterozygosity in southern Ecuador and northern Peru, with significantly lower diversity in Bolivia and southern Peru.

Its origin: Mesoamerica, not the Andes

A common assumption needs correcting here. A 2017 study in Molecular Ecology examined 1,765 accessions collected from six countries with nine microsatellite markers and suggests "a Mesoamerican origin of the crop, contrary to previous reports". A 2021 study in New Phytologist traces the route: "humans brought cherimoya from Mesoamerica to present Peru through long-distance sea-trade routes across the Pacific Ocean at least 4700 yr bp". The authors present this as the first evidence of pre-Columbian crop movement between Mesoamerica and the Andes. The centre of diversity lies in the Andes, but domestication did not happen there.

In nature

Where it grows
Kew records a range of 42 regions, a figure that of which only four are native: Bolivia, Colombia, Ecuador and Peru. The centre of diversity is southern Ecuador and northern Peru. Spain is recorded by Kew as introduced; that it is also grown in Portugal comes not from Kew but from the 2014 study. Türkiye is not in the native range.
Harvest season
The fruits ripen between autumn and winter; there is no commercial cultivation in Türkiye.
Local names
çerimoya; local name chirimoya.

Mix-up risk

It should not be confused with the soursop (*Annona muricata*) — the neurotoxicity and atypical parkinsonism literature belongs to that species and to *A. squamosa*, not to the cherimoya; that species is a separate record on this site with a different safety level. The atemoya is a hybrid of cherimoya and *A. squamosa* and is a different plant again.

Preparation

The flesh of the ripe fruit is eaten raw. No official monograph sets an accepted dose, and no body has set a consumption limit for cherimoya. The seeds are not eaten or chewed, and the skin is not eaten. Seeds, roots, deciduous leaves and stems are not the part this record covers — acetogenins have been reported in those parts of this species, not in the fruit flesh. This page describes the fresh fruit: because low levels of acetogenins have been detected in a commercial beverage made from the fruit, processed and concentrated products and supplements fall outside the scope of this record.

What science says

The whole weight of this record rests on one question: does the cherimoya share the neurotoxicity file of the soursop (*Annona muricata*)? The answer lies in the detail, and this is the most important section on the page. The 1999 case-control study reporting the cluster of atypical parkinsonism in Guadeloupe names only *Annona muricata* and *Annona squamosa*. The measured quantity of annonacin, and the purified annonacin that caused brain lesions in rats, both come from *A. muricata*. As for the cherimoya, the very laboratory that framed the hypothesis screened the fruit pulp of this species and reported it "not to be a source of exposure" (2011, by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry); the kaurenoic acid abundant in the fruit caused neither neuronal death nor astrogliosis in rat embryonic striatal cultures up to 50 µM. A 2021 phytochemical inventory points the same way: of the 41 acetogenins described in the species, the organs reported are seeds, roots, deciduous leaves and stems — fruit pulp appears as the organ for no compound. But the file is not closed: in 2014 the same laboratory, using a more sensitive method (Orbitrap), detected low levels of acetogenins in a commercial alcoholic beverage made from cherimoya fruit, saw more than 80 of them, identified the 31 most intense and concluded that "this species should be considered as a risk factor"; that study gives no quantitative concentration. The two findings do not contradict each other — they looked at different things: the 2011 work at fruit pulp, the 2014 work at a processed product made from the fruit. But the 2014 study’s own sentence has to be given as it stands: it says the presence of these compounds "in the pulp of its fruit and in derived food products is unclear". The source laboratory, in other words, did not treat the question as settled three years after its own negative screen. On the institutional side, an assessment setting a safe intake level exists only for the soursop: the 2020 assessment published under EFSA covers *A. muricata* supplements, finds "strong indications of neurotoxic potential of certain A. muricata preparations" and states that no safe intake level could be established; the cherimoya does not appear in that document at all. EFSA’s compendium of botanicals does, however, carry a genus-level "Annona spp." entry that names the cherimoya, recording acetogenins in the seed and isoquinoline alkaloids in the bark, leaf, fruit and stem. That entry sets no limit; it lists. Evidence level "traditional".
