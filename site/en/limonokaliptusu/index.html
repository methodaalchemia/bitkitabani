BT-321 · Myrtaceae (myrtle family)

Lemon-scented gum

Corymbia citriodora (Hook.) K.D.Hill & L.A.S.Johnson
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

The active substance in repellent products is not the oil itself but p-menthane-3,8-diol, synthesised from citronellal, the oil’s dominant component; the Özokan study published in Drug and Chemical Toxicology in 2024 synthesised PMD by that route from *Eucalyptus citriodora* oil and compared it with DEET. While plant-derived repellents generally trail the synthetics in reviews, the PMD derived from this species is the one plant-based active substance named in the same sentence as them.

Other names
Kew’s World Checklist of Vascular Plants gives the accepted name as *Corymbia citriodora*; *Eucalyptus citriodora*, still common in the literature and in trade, is a synonym of it. English lemon-scented gum, lemon eucalyptus. p-Menthane-3,8-diol (PMD), synthesised from citronellal, the oil’s dominant component, appears in repellent products as "oil of lemon eucalyptus" (OLE); the Özokan study published in Drug and Chemical Toxicology in 2024 synthesised PMD by exactly that route from *Eucalyptus citriodora* oil. It has no established Turkish name; "limon okaliptüsü" is a descriptive name for its scent. Neither the European Medicines Agency nor the Turkish agency has a monograph for this species.

In short

What is Lemon-scented gum?

Lemon-scented gum (Corymbia citriodora (Hook.) K.D.Hill & L.A.S.Johnson) is a plant of the Myrtaceae (myrtle family) family. Traditions that use it: Oceania. Part used: leaf; the essential oil distilled from the leaf and the pmd derived from that oil.

What is Lemon-scented gum used for?

Traditional use: The Rudin review published in Therapeutische Umschau in 2005 writes that DEET has been the gold standard of repellents for about sixty years, and that among natural active substances the best protection comes from products containing an extract of *Eucalyptus citriodora* — that review never mentions PMD. Scientific evidence: traditional. In the sources available to us there is no repellency trial in humans using this species’ oil; both grounds for repellency are reviews, and the two do not assess the same thing: the 2005 review in Therapeutische Umschau speaks of an extract of *Eucalyptus citriodora*, the 2016 review in Wilderness & Environmental Medicine of PMD synthesised from the oil.

Is Lemon-scented gum safe?

Safety label: caution. What is at issue is a concentrated essential oil. The only measurement of its composition we have is the Aguiar study published in The Scientific World Journal in 2014: in the sample analysed by gas chromatography the dominant component was citronellal (61.78%), with substantial amounts of geraniol, β-citronellol and isopulegol beside it; citronellal gives the lemon scent. Because fragrant aldehydes can sensitise the skin, applying the oil undiluted to the skin is not appropriate. The same study notes that the compositions reported for this species’ oil differ from one another, so a measurement on a single sample cannot be taken to hold for every batch of oil. 1,8-Cineole is not dominant in the measured composition, so the infant contraindication written for cineole-bearing eucalyptus oils — the European Medicines Agency monograph makes use in children under 24 months contraindicated because of the risk of laryngospasm — does not belong to this species directly; even so, because the composition varies from batch to batch, it is appropriate not to use the oil in infants and young children. The sources relied on here contain no separate assessment for pregnancy, breastfeeding, children or drug interactions; as there are no data, it is appropriate not to use the oil in these groups. If a registered repellent product is used, its rules are on its label and this record does not replace them. The monograph and restrictions written for eucalyptus (*Eucalyptus globulus*, *E. camaldulensis*) do not belong to this species. The European Medicines Agency’s monograph on eucalyptus oil covers *E. globulus*, *E. polybractea* and *E. smithii*; this species falls outside it.

Traditional use

Mosquito and tick repellent

The Rudin review published in Therapeutische Umschau in 2005 writes that DEET has been the gold standard of repellents for about sixty years, and that among natural active substances the best protection comes from products containing an extract of *Eucalyptus citriodora* — that review never mentions PMD. The Diaz review published in Wilderness & Environmental Medicine in 2016 writes that for certain exposures — ticks, biting midges, sandflies, blackflies — products containing PMD (p-menthane-3,8-diol, "oil of lemon eucalyptus") may offer better protection than topical DEET alone. Three different things are involved here and should not be confused: the raw essential oil of the leaf, an extract of the leaf, and PMD, the single substance synthesised from a component of the oil. Both are reviews; neither is itself a measurement of efficacy.

Repellent applied to fabric — a laboratory comparison

A study published in PeerJ in 2021 applied four active ingredients (DEET, picaridin, IR3535 and OLE) to fabric and measured tick engagement. All four significantly reduced engagement for at least six hours; at six hours OLE repelled ticks better than the others. This is a laboratory measurement, not a comparison made on human skin.

Denture cleaning — a clinical trial

A randomised, crossover, triple-blind clinical trial published in the American Journal of Dentistry in 2026 tested four experimental dentifrices containing 1% essential oil in twenty-four participants. All of them, including the one with *Eucalyptus citriodora*, significantly reduced biofilm and *Candida albicans* load compared with baseline and were well accepted by users. The trial measures denture cleaning, not the treatment of a disease.

Against nematodes in agriculture

A review published in Plants in 2021 tested sixteen essential oils, *Eucalyptus citriodora* oil among them, against plant-parasitic nematodes in vitro and in soil experiments. The authors conclude that the oils are suitable for new, safe nematicides but that problems of stabilisation and standardisation remain. This is an agricultural application, not human use.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew’s records its natural range is north-eastern Australia; the species is recorded in thirty-two regions, most of them places where it was later planted (Assam, Bangladesh, Burkina Faso, California, the Canary Islands, southern China, the Comoros, Cuba, the Dominican Republic and others). It is a large, straight-trunked tree, tolerant of heat and drought.
Harvest season
The sources relied on here give no collection calendar for Turkey; the plant does not grow there naturally.
Local names
It has no established Turkish local name.

Mix-up risk

It is a close relative of eucalyptus (*Eucalyptus globulus*, *E. camaldulensis*) but a separate species, and today it is placed in a separate genus (*Corymbia*); one species’ monograph and restrictions do not belong to its relative. The scent differs too: the Merghni study published in Microbial Pathogenesis in 2018 gives 1,8-cineole as the main component of *Eucalyptus globulus* oil, while in the 2014 Aguiar measurement the dominant component of this species’ oil is citronellal (61.78%).

Preparation

No dose is accepted in an official monograph. What is used as a repellent is not the leaf itself but PMD derived from the oil, and in registered products its concentration is stated on the manufacturer’s label; the sources relied on here give no measure for a home-made repellent. The essential oil is not taken internally. The sources relied on here give no dose or age limit for children; where a registered product is used, the age and application rules on its label apply.

What science says

In the sources available to us there is no repellency trial in humans using this species’ oil; both grounds for repellency are reviews, and the two do not assess the same thing: the 2005 review in Therapeutische Umschau speaks of an extract of *Eucalyptus citriodora*, the 2016 review in Wilderness & Environmental Medicine of PMD synthesised from the oil. The tick measurement is a laboratory experiment on fabric. The only study in humans concerns denture cleaning, has twenty-four participants, and there the oil is one component of a dentifrice at 1%. The single measurement of the oil’s composition we have is the Aguiar study published in The Scientific World Journal in 2014. Measurements made on the eucalyptus species this one is confused with are not written into this record: the oils tested in vitro against equine wound isolates (Veterinary Sciences, 2023) were *Eucalyptus radiata* and *E. globulus*, and the Myrtaceae oils tested for TNF-α and CCL2 release by stimulated THP-1 cells (Plants, 2020) were *E. globulus* subsp. *maidenii*, *E. radiata* and *E. viminalis*; this species appears in neither study. Those two citations are listed in the record they belong to — the narrow-leaved peppermint (*Eucalyptus radiata*) record in the same batch.
