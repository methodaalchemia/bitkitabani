Pineapple weed

Matricaria discoidea DC.
Evidence: LimitedGenerally safe

In the folklore records of south-eastern Estonia from 1888 to 1996, this weed of North American origin is the second most cited medicinal plant after Scots pine (Plants, 2022). The species name discoidea describes a flower head that has no strap-shaped white florets, only disc florets.

Other names
English pineapple weed, rayless chamomile. The synonyms in Kew POWO include Chamomilla suaveolens (Pursh) Rydb. and Matricaria suaveolens (Pursh) Buchenau; some floras list it as Matricaria matricarioides. The Turkish name "ananas papatyası" is a translation of the English name. The part used is the flowering aerial parts.

In short

What is Pineapple weed?

Pineapple weed (Matricaria discoidea DC.) is a plant of the Asteraceae (daisy family) family. Traditions that use it: North America, Europe. Part used: flowering aerial parts.

What is Pineapple weed used for?

Traditional use: A study published in PLoS One in 2018 writes that the use of the plant as an insect repellent by the Blackfoot and other indigenous groups of North America is documented. Scientific evidence: limited. The sources relied on here contain no clinical trial of this plant in humans; the evidence is at the laboratory and animal level.

Is Pineapple weed safe?

Safety label: generally safe. No safety study has been done in humans; in the 2024 study the dry extracts were not found to be toxic in cell tests or animal experiments. The plant belongs to the daisy family. The EMA monograph on German chamomile treats hypersensitivity to the daisy family as a contraindication; that warning should be kept in mind for this closely related species as well. People allergic to chamomile, wormwood or other members of the daisy family should not use it. There are no data for pregnancy, breastfeeding or children; nothing supports its use in these periods. Plants gathered from roadsides and sprayed areas should not be used. No drug interaction study is known; people on regular medication should consult their doctor.

Traditional use

Native peoples of North America — insect repellent and scent

A study published in PLoS One in 2018 writes that the use of the plant as an insect repellent by the Blackfoot and other indigenous groups of North America is documented. According to the same study, various indigenous peoples also used the plant medicinally and as a perfume. The flora page of the Native Plant Trust likewise mentions it as a medicinal and aromatic plant used by Native Americans.

Estonia — among the most cited plants of folk medicine

A study published in the journal Plants in 2022 examined folklore records from 1888 to 1996 for three districts in south-eastern Estonia. In these records pineapple weed, with 51 use reports, is the second most frequently mentioned medicinal plant after Scots pine (57). It is an example of a North American roadside weed turning into a folk remedy in Europe.

Europe — in place of chamomile

According to a study published in Biomolecules in 2024, the plant’s history in folk medicine goes back to the 19th century; it was used mostly as a tea or tincture, as an anti-inflammatory and antispasmodic. The same study reports that the USSR pharmacopoeia approved pineapple weed as an additional raw material alongside chamomile flower, for external use only.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew POWO it is an annual; its native range runs from subarctic North America to the USA. According to the Native Plant Trust it has spread from north-western North America eastward and to Europe; it grows on roadsides, in yards and on disturbed soil. A 2019 revision counts this species, under the name Matricaria matricarioides, among the four Matricaria taxa recognised in the flora of Türkiye.
Harvest season
In the 2024 study in Biomolecules the plant was gathered in Estonia in July, during flowering. The sources relied on here give no calendar for Türkiye.
Local names
It has no established local name in Turkish.

Mix-up risk

It can be confused with German chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla, synonym Matricaria recutita). According to the Native Plant Trust, the flower head of pineapple weed has disc florets only and no strap-shaped florets; this sets it apart from German chamomile. The EMA monograph covers German chamomile only.

Preparation

No EMA, WHO or Turkish (TİTCK) monograph could be found; there is no accepted dose. The 2024 study in Biomolecules writes that in folk use the plant is prepared as a tea or tincture. In the same study the aerial parts were gathered in July, during flowering, and dried for a week at room temperature in a well-ventilated place. The doses in the EMA monograph on matricaria flower are for German chamomile only; they cannot be transferred to this species.

What science says

The sources relied on here contain no clinical trial of this plant in humans; the evidence is at the laboratory and animal level. The 2024 study in Biomolecules identified 16 phenolic compounds in the dry extracts and 9 main terpenoids in the essential oil. In the same study, extracts given at doses of 25, 50 and 100 mg/kg showed a pain-relieving effect in mice and improved sleep in rats; in cell tests the dry extracts were not found to be toxic. The 2007 study in Phytochemical Analysis measured large amounts of spiroether isomers in the flowers and the highest level of herniarin (a coumarin) in the leaves. In the 2018 study in PLoS One, the plant’s essential oil and, among its components, alpha-terpineol deterred biting by the mosquito Aedes aegypti in a laboratory set-up; the authors write that this supports the traditional insect-repellent use. None of these findings has been tested in humans.

Safety

Generally safe

No safety study has been done in humans; in the 2024 study the dry extracts were not found to be toxic in cell tests or animal experiments. The plant belongs to the daisy family. The EMA monograph on German chamomile treats hypersensitivity to the daisy family as a contraindication; that warning should be kept in mind for this closely related species as well. People allergic to chamomile, wormwood or other members of the daisy family should not use it. There are no data for pregnancy, breastfeeding or children; nothing supports its use in these periods. Plants gathered from roadsides and sprayed areas should not be used. No drug interaction study is known; people on regular medication should consult their doctor.
