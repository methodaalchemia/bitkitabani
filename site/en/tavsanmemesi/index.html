BT-199 · Asparagaceae (asparagus family)

Butcher’s broom

Ruscus aculeatus L.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

The English name comes from a use that has nothing to do with medicine: butcher’s broom. The stiff, spiny stems were bundled to scour butchers’ blocks and benches. The Turkish names look somewhere else entirely: "tavşan memesi" and "tavşan kirazı" describe the bright red berries sitting on the stems through winter. The same plant was named for its function in one language and for its appearance in another.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the names "tavşan memesi, tavşan kirazı" (hare’s teat, hare’s cherry) and lists no synonyms. The English name comes from the stems once being bundled to scour butchers’ blocks.

In short

What is Butcher’s broom?

Butcher’s broom (Ruscus aculeatus L.) is a plant of the Asparagaceae (asparagus family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the root and rhizome.

What is Butcher’s broom used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places both indications under "traditional use": (1) relief of discomfort and heaviness of the legs related to minor venous circulatory disturbances; (2) symptomatic relief of itching and burning associated with haemorrhoids — after serious conditions have been excluded by a medical doctor. Scientific evidence: limited. This is one of the plants in the catalog’s "traditional use" class that nevertheless has a measured clinical effect.

Is Butcher’s broom safe?

Safety label: caution. Not for anyone hypersensitive to the active substance. The most important warnings here are not about the plant but about what the symptom may mean. When used for heavy legs: see a doctor if there is inflammation of the skin, subcutaneous induration, ulcers, sudden swelling of one or both legs, or cardiac or renal insufficiency — these can signal conditions far more serious than heavy legs, such as a clot or heart failure. When used for haemorrhoids: see a doctor if rectal bleeding occurs, and the EU monograph already ties the indication to the condition "after serious conditions have been excluded by a medical doctor". If symptoms last longer than two weeks, consult a doctor or pharmacist. Use under 18 has not been established; not recommended in pregnancy or lactation for lack of data. No undesirable effects are reported in the monographs, but the EU monograph’s wording is "none known".

Traditional use

Europe — two indications

The EMA monograph places both indications under "traditional use": (1) relief of discomfort and heaviness of the legs related to minor venous circulatory disturbances; (2) symptomatic relief of itching and burning associated with haemorrhoids — after serious conditions have been excluded by a medical doctor. Use is oral.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the roots and rhizomes" and sets a separate list of warnings for each indication. For the leg indication: see a doctor if there is inflammation of the skin, subcutaneous induration, ulcers, sudden swelling of one or both legs, or cardiac or renal insufficiency. For the haemorrhoid indication: see a doctor if rectal bleeding occurs.

Anatolia

Butcher’s broom is a spiny evergreen shrub of the Mediterranean and Aegean maquis and woodland floors in Turkey, conspicuous for its red berries in winter. The part used medicinally is the root and rhizome underground.

In nature

Where it grows
Woodland floors and maquis of the Mediterranean basin and western Europe. Common in the Mediterranean, Aegean and Marmara regions of Turkey.
Harvest season
Small greenish flowers in late winter and spring; bright red berries in autumn and winter. The root and rhizome are lifted in autumn.
Local names
Tavşan memesi, tavşan kirazı — both appear in the Turkish monograph.

Mix-up risk

Ruscus hypoglossum and R. hypophyllum of the same genus grow alongside it in Turkey and look similar; the EU monograph covers R. aculeatus only. The distinguishing feature: in R. aculeatus the leaf-like structures are stiff and spine-tipped. The red berries look attractive but are not eaten.

Preparation

The monograph describes oral preparations of the root and rhizome. If symptoms last longer than two weeks, consult a doctor or pharmacist. Use under 18 has not been established.

What science says

This is one of the plants in the catalog’s "traditional use" class that nevertheless has a measured clinical effect. A meta-analysis published in International Angiology compared the main venoactive drugs in venous ankle oedema: ten randomised controlled trials from 1975 to 2009, 1,010 patients in all, with reduction in ankle circumference as the outcome. The results: a reduction of 0.80 cm with micronised purified flavonoid fraction (MPFF), 0.58 cm with ruscus extract, 0.58 cm with hydroxyethylrutoside, 0.20 cm with diosmin alone and 0.11 cm with placebo. Every drug, ruscus included, was significantly superior to placebo (p<0.0001); in the head-to-head comparison MPFF came out ahead, while ruscus and hydroxyethylrutoside were comparable. So the effect has been measured, separated from placebo and shown on an objective outcome (a circumference measurement) — but its size is modest: half a centimetre. A 2024 review in Fitoterapia likewise lists ruscus, alongside horse chestnut and Centella, among the plants with a place in chronic venous disease. The evidence level is Limited: there is a real pooled finding, but one meta-analysis and a small effect size.

Safety

Caution

Not for anyone hypersensitive to the active substance. The most important warnings here are not about the plant but about what the symptom may mean. When used for heavy legs: see a doctor if there is inflammation of the skin, subcutaneous induration, ulcers, sudden swelling of one or both legs, or cardiac or renal insufficiency — these can signal conditions far more serious than heavy legs, such as a clot or heart failure. When used for haemorrhoids: see a doctor if rectal bleeding occurs, and the EU monograph already ties the indication to the condition "after serious conditions have been excluded by a medical doctor". If symptoms last longer than two weeks, consult a doctor or pharmacist. Use under 18 has not been established; not recommended in pregnancy or lactation for lack of data. No undesirable effects are reported in the monographs, but the EU monograph’s wording is "none known".

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No drug interactions reported in either monographBoth the EU and the Turkish monograph leave the interaction section empty. "None reported" is not evidence of no interaction; it means not studied.
Other venoactive productsNot an interaction warning but a note from the meta-analysis: ruscus extract, hydroxyethylrutoside and MPFF are products used for the same problem with effects shown on the same outcome. There is no evidence that combining several adds benefit; which one to use is a question for a doctor.
Blood pressure and heart medicinesThere is no interaction study. The point of care here is different: swelling in the leg can also be a sign of heart failure, and the monographs ask for a doctor in that case. Anyone taking medication for cardiac or renal disease should not manage leg swelling with a herbal product.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Butcher’s broom.
