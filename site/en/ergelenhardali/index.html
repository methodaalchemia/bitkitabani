Hedge mustard

Sisymbrium officinale (L.) Scop.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

The official list carries two Turkish names that say what the plant is for: çalgıcıotu, the musician’s herb, and bülbülotu, the nightingale’s herb. The plant’s centuries-old European reputation as the singers’ herb passed into Turkish with its name. A line further down in the same monograph, though, puts the plant in the same class as foxglove: cardenolide content, a 1 ppm limit, a heart-rhythm warning. The distance between a lozenge sucked for the voice and a heart drug is, in this plant, only a dose.

The Turkish monograph gives the names "ergelen hardalı, çalgıcıotu, bülbülotu"; Erysimum officinale L. is among the synonyms. In English: hedge mustard. In short

What is Hedge mustard?

Hedge mustard (Sisymbrium officinale (L.) Scop.) is a plant of the Brassicaceae (cabbage family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the flowering aerial parts.

What is Hedge mustard used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": relief of symptoms of throat irritation such as hoarseness and dry cough. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no clinical trial of hedge mustard in humans; recent publications are compositional analyses (flavonoid glycosides) and laboratory work.

Is Hedge mustard safe?

Safety label: caution. At high doses, Digitalis-like effects — nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, headache and cardiac arrhythmias — may occur (Turkish monograph). That is why the monograph’s lozenge dose is in milligrams and why products are required to keep cardenolide content below 1 ppm; using a pharmacy product is, for this reason, different from gathering the plant and making tea. Use with caution in potassium deficiency — low potassium amplifies the effect of cardiac glycosides. Those with chronic inflammation of the mouth and pharynx should also be careful. See a doctor if dyspnoea, fever or purulent sputum occurs. Lozenges not under 6, oral use not under 3; not recommended in pregnancy or lactation.

Traditional use

Europe — one indication

The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": relief of symptoms of throat irritation such as hoarseness and dry cough. The form is rare in this catalog: a lozenge dissolved in the mouth without chewing, 10–12 times daily. And the monograph is unusually open to children: lozenges from age 6, oral extract from age 3.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph says something the EU one does not, and it is the most important line of this record: the cardenolide content of herbal preparations must be declared and must not exceed 1 ppm; at high doses, effects resembling the side effects of Digitalis species (nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, headache and cardiac arrhythmias) may occur. Use with caution in potassium deficiency and in chronic inflammation of the mouth and pharynx.

Europe — the singers’ herb

Two of the plant’s Turkish names — çalgıcıotu and bülbülotu — correspond to the French "herbe aux chantres" and Italian "erba dei cantanti" tradition: the herb singers and orators used against hoarseness. That these names made it onto the Turkish official list is a rare case of a folk name carried into an official document.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to Europe and western Asia; roadsides, hedges and waste ground. A common weed in Turkey.
Harvest season
Small yellow flowers from May to September; the flowering aerial parts are gathered then.
Local names
Ergelen hardalı, çalgıcıotu, bülbülotu — all three appear in the Turkish monograph.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ The monograph’s own warning: not to be confused with black mustard (Brassica nigra), white mustard (Brassica alba) or Iberis amara. The yellow-flowered wild mustards look much alike. Because of the cardenolide content, gathering it and making tea is not advised; the monograph’s doses are for pharmacy products.

Preparation

The EU monograph gives two routes. Oromucosal (lozenge, dissolved without chewing): adults and adolescents, dry extract 7.5–10 mg, 10–12 times daily; children 6–11, 5–6 times daily. Oral: adults, dry extract (DER 6–8:1) 82.5 mg 3–4 times daily; children 3–11, 27.5 mg 3–4 times daily. If symptoms persist longer than a week, consult a doctor or pharmacist. Lozenges not recommended under 6, oral use not under 3.

What science says

There is no clinical trial of hedge mustard in humans; recent publications are compositional analyses (flavonoid glycosides) and laboratory work. The EU monograph rests the indication on long use. Something is known about the plant’s chemistry, however, and the Turkish monograph puts it at the centre: like many members of the mustard family, hedge mustard carries cardenolides (cardiac glycosides) — the same class as the substances in foxglove (Digitalis). The monograph’s 1 ppm limit and its "Digitalis-like side effects" warning follow from that. That a lozenge herb is constrained by the logic of a heart drug is a fine example of "natural" and "harmless" not being the same thing. The evidence level is Traditional.

Safety

Caution

At high doses, Digitalis-like effects — nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, headache and cardiac arrhythmias — may occur (Turkish monograph). That is why the monograph’s lozenge dose is in milligrams and why products are required to keep cardenolide content below 1 ppm; using a pharmacy product is, for this reason, different from gathering the plant and making tea. Use with caution in potassium deficiency — low potassium amplifies the effect of cardiac glycosides. Those with chronic inflammation of the mouth and pharynx should also be careful. See a doctor if dyspnoea, fever or purulent sputum occurs. Lozenges not under 6, oral use not under 3; not recommended in pregnancy or lactation.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Digoxin and other digitalis glycosidesReported by the Turkish monograph, with a direct mechanism: the plant carries substances of the same class; stacking two sources of cardenolides increases both effect and the risk of toxicity. Anyone on digoxin should not use this plant.
Diuretics and stimulant laxativesOn the Turkish list. The logic runs through potassium: both deplete it, and low potassium strengthens the effect of cardiac glycosides on the heart — links in the same chain as the monograph’s "caution in potassium deficiency".
Macrolide and tetracycline antibiotics, quinineOn the Turkish list. These are drugs known to raise digoxin levels; the monograph applies the same logic to this plant.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing in its interaction section and carries no cardenolide warning. That one agency gives a whole list built on cardiac-glycoside logic while the other leaves it blank is one of the largest divergences in this catalog; we took the more protective one as the guide.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Hedge mustard.
