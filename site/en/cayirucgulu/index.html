What is Red clover?

Red clover (Trifolium pratense L.) is a plant of the Fabaceae (legume family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe, Asia and Siberia. Part used: the dried inflorescences.

What is Red clover used for?

Is Red clover safe?

Safety label: caution. Not to be used without medical advice in hormone-related disorders, oestrogen-dependent cancers or a family history of them — the WHO monograph’s warning; the plant carries oestrogen-like isoflavones. Contraindicated in pregnancy, lactation and under 12. Traditional use

WHO monograph — the flower

The World Health Organization monograph (Vol. 4, 2009) does something unusual: instead of giving an indication under "uses described in pharmacopoeias" it writes that although numerous clinical trials have assessed red clover extracts for menopausal symptoms, hyperlipidaemia, osteoporosis and prostate cancer, the data are as yet insufficient to support any of these indications. In traditional medicine: topically for psoriasis and eczema, orally for asthma and cough. Uses supported by clinical data: none.

Turkey

There is no monograph for red clover on the Turkish list. The plant is common in Turkish meadows and grown as fodder; this catalog has no sourced folk use. On the supplement market it is sold as "isoflavones for the menopause".

Australia — "clover disease"

The plant’s hormonal effect was first seen in sheep: in Australian flocks grazing heavily on clover, infertility, abnormal lactation and prolapsed uterus — "clover disease", attributed to the oestrogen-like effect of isoflavones. The WHO monograph records this and adds: in human trials no adverse effects were reported at up to 160 mg of isoflavones a day.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to Europe, found worldwide; meadows and roadsides. Common in Turkey and grown as fodder.
Harvest season
Pink-purple globe flowers from May to September; the flower heads are gathered in full bloom and dried.
Local names
Çayır üçgülü, kırmızı yonca, çayır yoncası — common Turkish names.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ The Turkish word "yonca" also covers Medicago, Melilotus and Menyanthes. Sweet clover carries coumarin, red clover does not; confusing the two leads to the wrong warning. White clover (Trifolium repens) is a separate species outside the monograph.

Preparation

The WHO monograph gives an oral dose: 240–480 mg of extract, corresponding to 40–80 mg of isoflavones a day. Crude drug and tablets. Contraindicated in pregnancy, lactation, under 12, and in hormone-associated diseases.

What science says

Red clover is the plant the WHO said "insufficient data" about in 2009 — and in the fifteen years since, data accumulated. A meta-analysis published in 2021 in Nutrients questioned the clinical meaningfulness of the extract’s effect on hot flushes. A GRADE-assessed meta-analysis published in 2026 in the European Journal of Obstetrics & Gynecology and Reproductive Biology pooled 9 randomised trials (women aged 40–65, 37–160 mg isoflavones a day, 12 weeks to 12 months): a small-to-moderate reduction in hot-flush frequency against placebo (SMD −0.45; 95% CI −0.81 to −0.08), most trials at low risk of bias, no publication bias detected. The authors’ conclusion: "modest but statistically significant". For comparison: in this same catalog, soy isoflavones showed no significant effect on hot flushes. Evidence level Limited: an effect exists, it is small, and the WHO’s hesitation of that day has been partly overtaken.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Tamoxifen and other anti-oestrogenic drugsThe WHO monograph summarises conflicting data: some studies suggest isoflavones enhance tamoxifen’s effect, while in rodent models genistein reduced it. The monograph’s conclusion is firm: not recommended in those being treated with tamoxifen or other anti-oestrogenic drugs until further research has been done.
Hormone therapies (theoretical)A plant with oestrogen-like effects works along the same pathway as hormone replacement therapy or the contraceptive pill; the monograph does not list this separately, but it is an extension of its "contraindicated in hormone-associated diseases".

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Red clover.
