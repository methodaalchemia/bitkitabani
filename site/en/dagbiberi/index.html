Mountain pepper

Tasmannia lanceolata (Poir.) A.C.Sm.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

Its name says "pepper" and its taste recalls pepper, but it is not related to pepper: pepper’s pungency comes from piperine, this plant’s from polygodial. The Winteraceae are held to be one of the oldest lineages of flowering plants — so two spices that resemble each other in the kitchen come from two very distant branches of the plant family tree.

Other names
English mountain pepper, Tasmanian pepperberry, Dorrigo pepper. Its former name is *Drimys lanceolata*. It has no established Turkish name; "dağ biberi" is a descriptive rendering of the English one. It has no relation at all to true pepper (*Piper nigrum*). Neither the European Medicines Agency nor the Turkish agency has a monograph for this species.
Part used
Leaf and fruit (as a spice); leaf extract (in cosmetics)
Region
Oceania

In short

What is Mountain pepper?

Mountain pepper (Tasmannia lanceolata (Poir.) A.C.Sm.) is a plant of the Winteraceae family. Traditions that use it: Oceania. Part used: leaf and fruit (as a spice); leaf extract (in cosmetics).

What is Mountain pepper used for?

Traditional use: Its leaf and dried fruit are used in Australia as a spice in place of pepper. Scientific evidence: traditional. The only study in humans concerns the appearance of stretch marks: it is randomised and placebo-controlled, so it is genuine evidence, but it has twenty-nine participants, measures a cosmetic outcome, and some of the researchers are from the company that makes the product’s raw material.

Is Mountain pepper safe?

Safety label: caution. Using the leaf and fruit in spice quantities is ordinary. That said, the species’ major component is polygodial: a sharp, burning sesquiterpene dialdehyde that is the source of the taste and is irritant when concentrated. Concentrated extracts are not the same thing as the spice and do not count as food. There is no safety assessment in humans for this species; the only human study we have concerns a cosmetic product applied to the skin, and no serious adverse events were reported there. The sources relied on here contain no separate assessment for pregnancy, breastfeeding, children or drug interactions; as there are no data, it is appropriate not to use concentrated extracts in these groups. Because its family (Winteraceae) does not occur in the flora of Turkey, Turkish has no established name for it and the record gives it in Latin.

Traditional use

As a spice — leaf and fruit in Australia

Its leaf and dried fruit are used in Australia as a spice in place of pepper. A study published in Foods in 2018 identifies the species’ major active compound as polygodial; its sharp, burning taste comes from that.

On the appearance of stretch marks — a randomised trial in women

A double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled trial published in the Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology in 2021 had twenty-nine women (aged 25–60) apply the leaf extract to the skin daily for eight weeks. In the extract group the skin roughness of stretch marks improved significantly at eight weeks and skin firmness at four weeks; dermal density and thickness increased compared with placebo. In human skin explants, pro-collagen I, elastin and TGF-β levels were higher. It is a small trial, what it measures is the appearance of stretch marks, and some of the researchers are from a cosmetic ingredients company.

Against food spoilage — in vitro

The same 2018 study tested the species’ solvent extracts against two bacteria and six weak-acid-resistant yeasts, reporting broad-spectrum antifungal activity. A study published in Foods in 2023 assessed the species, as one of three underutilised Australian plants, for its phenolic and organic acid profile. These are in vitro measurements.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew’s records its natural range covers three regions: New South Wales, Tasmania and Victoria. It is a shrub or small tree of the cool, moist mountain forests of south-eastern Australia.
Harvest season
The sources relied on here give no collection calendar for Turkey; the plant does not grow there naturally.
Local names
It has no established Turkish local name.

Mix-up risk

It has no relation at all to true pepper (*Piper nigrum*); they are in separate families and what is written for pepper does not apply to this species. In Australia the name "pepperberry" is given to several plants. Because its former name was *Drimys lanceolata* it is also looked up under that name in the literature; *Drimys* is today held to be a separate genus.

Preparation

No dose is accepted in an official monograph. The leaf and fruit are a spice and are used in spice quantities; the sources relied on here give no upper limit for that use. What was applied in the cosmetic trial is a product containing a standardised leaf extract, and its concentration is not given in the source relied on here; it cannot be converted into a home-made preparation. Concentrated extracts are not food.

What science says

The only study in humans concerns the appearance of stretch marks: it is randomised and placebo-controlled, so it is genuine evidence, but it has twenty-nine participants, measures a cosmetic outcome, and some of the researchers are from the company that makes the product’s raw material. The remaining data are in vitro. A study published in Cancers in 2022 reports that polygodial triggers apoptosis in castration-resistant prostate cancer cell lines by inducing oxidative stress — that is a cell line measurement, not a treatment. A review published in the Journal of Ethnopharmacology in 2021 mentions the species among selected Australian plants for its antioxidant and possible therapeutic value; it is a review, not a measurement in itself.

Safety

Caution

Using the leaf and fruit in spice quantities is ordinary. That said, the species’ major component is polygodial: a sharp, burning sesquiterpene dialdehyde that is the source of the taste and is irritant when concentrated. Concentrated extracts are not the same thing as the spice and do not count as food.
