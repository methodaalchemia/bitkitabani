Pistachio

Pistacia vera L.
Evidence: ClinicalCaution

Pistachio is dioecious and carries ZW sex chromosomes of the kind seen in birds and some mammals. About 23 per cent of the W chromosome is a region closed to recombination, and the system arose from an ordinary chromosome roughly 8 million years ago.

Other names

In short

What is Pistachio?

Pistachio (Pistacia vera L.) is a plant of the Anacardiaceae (cashew family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean. Part used: the kernel.

What is Pistachio used for?

Traditional use: Pistachio is a nut tree domesticated in the Fertile Crescent. Scientific evidence: clinical. Pistachio is one of the few nuts trialled in randomised controlled studies in humans.

Is Pistachio safe?

Safety label: caution. Pistachio is a tree nut and its allergy can be severe. The key point is CASHEW CROSS-REACTIVITY: people allergic to cashew are at high risk with pistachio, and the two belong to the same family (Anacardiaceae). Clinical cross-reactivity between mango, pistachio and cashew has been shown in children. Oral immunotherapy for cashew–pistachio allergy has been trialled, but that is a treatment carried out under medical supervision. There is no verified data on drug interactions, pregnancy or breastfeeding.

Traditional use

Domestication in the Fertile Crescent

Pistachio is a nut tree domesticated in the Fertile Crescent. In Türkiye the area around Gaziantep, Şanlıurfa and Siirt is the centre of production; the species’ first reference genome was also sequenced from a female individual of the Siirt cultivar.

Wild relatives in Anatolian folk medicine

The Pistacia species whose folk-medicine use is documented in Anatolian ethnobotanical records are the wild ones, not pistachio itself. In Şırnak the branch and fruit of Pistacia eurycarpa (“bendek”, “kızvan”, “bıttım”) are boiled and used against constipation, haemorrhoids, stomach ache and stomach ulcer. In Mardin bıttım soap is made from the fruit of Pistacia khinjuk (“bıttım”, “kızvan”). Pistacia terebinthus, for its part, is known by the name menengiç, and its fruit and resin are used. No Anatolian folk-medicine record could be found for pistachio itself (Pistacia vera); its cultural weight lies in the kitchen and in trade.

Not found at Göbekli Tepe

In layer III of Göbekli Tepe, fruit remains belonging to the genus Pistacia (124 fragments, an estimated 17 whole) and the largest share of the charcoal (3.14 g, 104 identifications) were found. But the charcoal was identified as Pistacia cf. atlantica: this is not pistachio but a wild Pistacia, that is, a menengiç/buttum species. The sentence “pistachio was found at Göbekli Tepe” is not supported by the source.

In nature

Where it grows
Likes a hot, dry climate and tolerates drought with a deep root system. Grown in Turkey around Gaziantep, Şanlıurfa, Siirt, Adıyaman and Kahramanmaraş, often grafted onto wild Pistacia rootstocks.
Harvest season
Flowers in March and April, fruit ripens in August and September. The tree bears heavily in alternate years.
Local names
Damascus nut

Mix-up risk

None of the species confused with pistachio is the same plant. Menengiç (Pistacia terebinthus), buttum (P. khinjuk, P. eurycarpa) and P. atlantica are separate wild species whose fruit, resin and coffee are used in Anatolia. Mastic is the resin of Pistacia lentiscus and is the only Pistacia with a European Medicines Agency monograph — pistachio has none.

Preparation

There is no official preparation: neither a European Medicines Agency monograph nor an NCCIH page exists. Clinical trials have used roughly 40–70 g of in-shell pistachios a day, a food amount rather than a drug dose.

What science says

Pistachio is one of the few nuts trialled in randomised controlled studies in humans. Trials report improvements in the lipid profile, effects on blood pressure and heart rate variability in type 2 diabetes, and improvements in insulin resistance and inflammatory markers. One crossover trial measured changes in the expression of genes related to DNA oxidation and telomere maintenance. A meta-analysis of randomised trials on metabolic syndrome also exists. This is evidence for pistachio as a food; there is no approved medical indication.

Safety

Caution

Pistachio is a tree nut and its allergy can be severe. The key point is CASHEW CROSS-REACTIVITY: people allergic to cashew are at high risk with pistachio, and the two belong to the same family (Anacardiaceae). Clinical cross-reactivity between mango, pistachio and cashew has been shown in children. Oral immunotherapy for cashew–pistachio allergy has been trialled, but that is a treatment carried out under medical supervision.
