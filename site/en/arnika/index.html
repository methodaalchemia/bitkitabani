Arnica

Arnica montana L.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

Arnica is a good example of how to read the most rigorous trial of a herbal product. In the 136-patient eyelid-surgery study, arnica ointment did not differ from placebo; but the study’s real lesson is elsewhere — the eyelid given no ointment at all did not differ from either. In that setting, what produces the impression that "I put it on and it got better" is not the ointment but the course of healing. That design — leaving an untreated control on the patient’s own body — is rare for herbal products, and it tells you a great deal.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the names "arnika, öküzgözü otu" and lists eight synonyms: Arnica lowii, A. alpina, A. angustifolia, A. helvetica, A. plantaginisfolia, Cineraria cernua, Doronicum arnica and D. oppositifolium. ⚠️ The "Arnica 30C" sold in pharmacies is a homeopathic product and not the same thing as the herbal preparations the monograph covers.

In short

What is Arnica?

Arnica (Arnica montana L.) is a plant of the Asteraceae (daisy family) family. Traditions that use it: Europe. Part used: the flower head.

What is Arnica used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": relief of bruises, sprains and localised muscular pain. Scientific evidence: traditional. Reading the evidence on arnica requires separating two things, because most of the literature is read without that separation: the EU monograph covers herbal preparations of the flower (tincture, ointment, gel), whereas the best-selling "Arnica 30C" is a homeopathic product and, because of the extreme dilution, a different thing.

Is Arnica safe?

Safety label: caution. Oral use is to be avoided — the Turkish monograph says so explicitly. Not for anyone sensitive to the active substance or to other plants of the Asteraceae family; the family is large, so people allergic to chamomile, chrysanthemum or sunflower should take care. It can cause contact dermatitis, which the Turkish monograph supports with three separate references. Allergic reactions such as itching and reddening of the skin, and eczema, may occur; the frequency is not known. Not to be applied to the eyes, to open wounds or to damaged skin — which matters, because the indication is knocks and sprains, and such an injury often comes with broken skin. Not recommended under 12, nor in pregnancy or lactation. The Turkish monograph requires supervision by a doctor or pharmacist trained in herbal treatment.

Traditional use

Europe — bruises, sprains, muscular pain

The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": relief of bruises, sprains and localised muscular pain. Topical use, with one limit stated in the monograph: not to be used on broken skin.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the flower head" and draws four separate lines: not to be applied to the eyes, to open wounds or to damaged skin; oral use is to be avoided; not recommended under 12; not for anyone sensitive to the Asteraceae family. The risk of contact dermatitis is supported by three separate references in the monograph.

The Alpine region

Arnica is a plant of the Alps and the mountains of central Europe; it has no wild distribution in Turkey. Its traditional use comes from those mountain villages, and so does the present indication: falls, knocks, sprains. It reaches Turkey as a product.

In nature

Where it grows
Acidic, nutrient-poor mountain meadows of the Alps, Pyrenees, Carpathians and central Europe. No wild distribution in Turkey. Wild populations in Europe are under collection pressure, and cultivation efforts continue.
Harvest season
Yellow-orange, daisy-like flowers from June to August; the flower head is the part used medicinally.
Local names
Arnika, öküzgözü otu — both appear in the Turkish monograph.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ There are two confusions. The first is the product category: herbal arnica ointment and homeopathic "Arnica 30C" are not the same thing. The second is the name: in Turkish "öküzgözü" (ox-eye) is also used for other plants, including a grape variety and, in some regions, other members of the daisy family. The Latin name on the label settles it.

Preparation

The monograph describes topical preparations of the flower head — tincture, ointment, gel. Not taken by mouth. Not applied to the eyes, to open wounds or to damaged skin. Not used under 12. If symptoms worsen during use, consult a doctor.

What science says

Reading the evidence on arnica requires separating two things, because most of the literature is read without that separation: the EU monograph covers herbal preparations of the flower (tincture, ointment, gel), whereas the best-selling "Arnica 30C" is a homeopathic product and, because of the extreme dilution, a different thing. A systematic review published in 2024 in Plants takes both together across 42 studies and reports negative as well as positive outcomes. A systematic review published in 2026 in Cureus focuses on postoperative recovery: of 27 studies, 55.6% reported a measurable benefit and 33.3% found no significant effect; the review calls arnica’s potential "moderate" and attributes the inconsistency to a lack of standardisation in dose, route and outcome measurement. The single most informative trial here is a negative one: a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled study published in 2016 in Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery gave 10% arnica ointment to 136 upper-blepharoplasty patients, with a notably rigorous design — in each patient one periorbital area was treated and the other side left with no ointment at all as an internal control. At three days, seven days and six weeks there was no difference between arnica and placebo; nor in swelling, bruising, erythema, pain or patient satisfaction. More strikingly, the untreated eyelid did not differ from either — meaning the impression that "it helps when you put it on" is produced by the passage of time, not by the ointment. The evidence level is therefore Traditional: the EU monograph rests the indication on long use alone, and the most rigorous trial shows no effect for the herbal ointment.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Blood-thinning medicinesThe Turkish monograph reports possible interaction with anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs. This is doubly worth noting for arnica: the plant is used to reduce bruising, and people on blood thinners already bruise more easily. The situation where the two meet is exactly the situation where the plant is most sought out.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing; the Turkish one reports one group.
Confusion with homeopathic arnica productsNot an interaction, but the commonest problem in practice: two different product categories carry the same name. Asking for "arnica" in a pharmacy can produce either a herbal ointment or a homeopathic tablet. Check the preparation stated on the label.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Arnica.
