Rupturewort

Herniaria glabra L., H. hirsuta L., H. incana Lam.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

The plant’s names describe a use that appears in no monograph today. The Latin genus name Herniaria comes from hernia; the English "rupturewort" says the same; and the Turkish names "kasık otu", "atyaran", "deli yaran" and "kabayaran" all point the same way. Yet the only indication the EU and Turkish monographs give concerns the urinary tract. A plant’s name sometimes preserves not its current use but an abandoned one — a good reason not to treat the name itself as evidence.

Other names
The Turkish agency has three separate monographs and the EU monograph covers all three species together. The Turkish names differ by species: for H. glabra "kasık otu, atyaran, Türkotu"; for H. hirsuta "deli yaran, tüylü kasıkotu"; for H. incana "sabun otu, kabayaran".

In short

What is Rupturewort?

Rupturewort (Herniaria glabra L.) is a plant of the Caryophyllaceae (pink family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the aerial parts.

What is Rupturewort used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": to increase the amount of urine and so flush the urinary tract, as an adjuvant in minor urinary complaints. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no randomised clinical trial of rupturewort in humans.

Is Rupturewort safe?

Safety label: caution. Not to be used where a reduced fluid intake is advised — in serious cardiac or renal disease, for instance. The reason is direct: the plant works by increasing urine output. Hypersensitivity to the active substance can occur. Fluid intake must be increased during use. If urinary complaints worsen, or if fever, painful urination, spasm or blood in the urine occur, see a doctor — these can signal a urinary infection and should not be managed with a herbal product. Not used under 18; not recommended in pregnancy or lactation. If symptoms do not resolve over two weeks, consult a doctor. It should not be used on the strength of the hernia connection in its name: a hernia is a surgical problem, and no monograph gives such an indication.

Traditional use

Europe — flushing the urinary tract

The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": to increase the amount of urine and so flush the urinary tract, as an adjuvant in minor urinary complaints. The monograph states the condition plainly: adequate fluid intake is required for the amount of urine to increase.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the aerial parts" and repeats the same two things: fluid intake must be increased during use, and it is not to be used in serious cardiac or renal disease where a reduced fluid intake is advised. Not used under 18.

Anatolia

Rupturewort is a small plant that spreads flat over sandy, stony and dry ground in Turkey. Most of its local names describe a use: "kasık otu" (groin herb), "atyaran", "deli yaran", "kabayaran" — and the Latin genus name Herniaria comes from the same place (hernia). That use has no counterpart in today’s monograph.

In nature

Where it grows
Sandy, stony and dry ground and roadsides of Europe, North Africa and western Asia. Common in Turkey.
Harvest season
Very small greenish flowers from June to September; the aerial parts are gathered in flower.
Local names
Kasık otu, atyaran, Türkotu, deli yaran, tüylü kasıkotu, sabun otu, kabayaran — all appear in the Turkish monographs; which name belongs to which species varies by region.

Mix-up risk

The EU monograph covers all three species together (H. glabra, H. hirsuta, H. incana) and all three grow in Turkey, which makes confusion between them harmless at monograph level. But the name "sabun otu" is also used in Turkish for Saponaria officinalis, a different plant.

Preparation

The monograph describes preparations of the aerial parts: herbal tea, infusion, decoction or macerate. The decisive condition is water: fluid intake must be increased for the amount of urine to rise. If symptoms do not resolve over two weeks, consult a doctor. Not used under 18.

What science says

There is no randomised clinical trial of rupturewort in humans. Recent publications are at laboratory level: the anti-inflammatory activity and haemocompatibility of saponin fractions, ethnobotanical reviews. These show what is in the plant; they do not show whether it relieves a urinary complaint in a patient. The EU monograph rests its indication on long use. The one mechanism the monograph gives is an increase in the amount of urine — which explains together why the plant must be used with plenty of water and why it must not be used at all by people with cardiac or renal disease. The hernia connection in its name appears in no monograph today: neither the EU nor the Turkish agency gives such an indication. The evidence level is Traditional.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
LithiumThe Turkish monograph states it may interact with lithium. The reason is mechanical: lithium is cleared by the kidney and a diuretic plant changes the kidney’s handling of fluid and salt; because lithium’s effect depends on a narrow range of blood levels, that matters. The same warning appears for horsetail, pumpkin seed and restharrow in this catalog.
Diuretic medicinesNo interaction is reported, but the mechanism points the same way: using the two together can deepen fluid and electrolyte loss.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing; the Turkish one reports one medicine.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Rupturewort.
