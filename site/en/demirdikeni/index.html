The European regulator worked on this plant for three years and in 2024 wrote this sentence: the only licensed product is an extract sold in Bulgaria since 1981, and that one is "specific". So the whole European market for tribulus — the tubs in gyms, the capsules online — is unlicensed apart from one product in one country. A plant’s market, its regulation and its evidence can live in three separate worlds.

In short

What is Tribulus?

Tribulus (Tribulus terrestris L.) is a plant of the Zygophyllaceae (caltrop family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Asia and Siberia, Europe. Part used: the aerial parts.

What is Tribulus used for?

Traditional use: The European Medicines Agency put the plant on its assessment list in 2021 and announced the outcome in 2024: no monograph can be established. Scientific evidence: limited. Tribulus has two famous claims and the evidence differs for each.

Is Tribulus safe?

Safety label: caution. With no monograph there is no official safety section; in the meta-analysis adverse events were as frequent as with placebo. Two points nonetheless: not in pregnancy or lactation (no data, and a plant carrying hormonal claims); anyone with prostate disease or a hormone-sensitive condition should not use it without asking a doctor — the common rule for "hormone" supplements of uncertain mechanism. On the supplement market the problem of purity and standardisation is bigger than the plant: contents may differ from the label. The fruit’s spines are a real source of injury (bare feet, bicycle tyres).

Traditional use

Europe — no monograph, a public statement

The European Medicines Agency put the plant on its assessment list in 2021 and announced the outcome in 2024: no monograph can be established. The reason is not a missing tradition — the 30-year market requirement is met — but data: "data supporting an acceptable level of safety and a recognized efficacy are not available as published data." The only licensed product in the EU is a standardised extract sold in Bulgaria since 1981 (at least 45% protodioscin), and the committee found that extract "specific" and would not base a general monograph on it.

Turkey

There is no monograph for tribulus on the Turkish list. The plant grows everywhere in Turkey on roadsides, beaches and fallow; on the supplement market it is among the best-selling plants as a "testosterone booster" and sexual-function product. The gap between its absence from the official list and its market volume is one of the widest in this catalog.

Eastern Europe and Asia

Native to dry regions from the Mediterranean to India; in Ayurveda (gokshura) a urinary and sexual-health plant. Its modern fame comes from an extract developed in Bulgaria in the 1970s–80s and from rumours of use by Eastern Bloc athletes — a commercial history, not a scientific one.

In nature

Where it grows
Dry regions from the Mediterranean to central Asia and India; roadsides, beaches, fallow. Common in Turkey.
Harvest season
Small yellow flowers in summer; five-parted, hard-spined fruits towards autumn.
Local names
Demirdikeni, çobançökerten; regional names not extended without a source.

Mix-up risk

Most market products carry the name "Tribulus" but the plant part, extract ratio and saponin content are not standardised; the only product the EU assessed is the Bulgarian standardised extract. The Turkish name resembles "devedikeni" (milk thistle); a separate family.

Preparation

This record gives no dose: there is neither an EU nor a Turkish monograph. The only licensed product in Europe is the Bulgarian standardised extract; the standardisation and saponin content of market supplements vary widely from product to product. Anyone considering it should at least look for a declared protodioscin content.

What science says

Tribulus has two famous claims and the evidence differs for each. Testosterone: a systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2026 in the International Journal of Impotence Research (8 randomised trials) answers this plainly — no significant difference in total testosterone between tribulus and placebo. The claim the market makes loudest does not appear in the pooled evidence. Erectile function: the same meta-analysis finds a difference of 3.23 points on IIEF-5 (95% CI 1.89–4.58) and 14.44 on IIEF-15 against placebo, with adverse events no more frequent than placebo. That is a moderate effect on a symptom scale; but the included trials are small, the extracts differ, and the European Medicines Agency searched the same literature and concluded that "data supporting an acceptable level of safety and a recognized efficacy are not available". We record both readings: evidence level Limited.

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No official interaction listWith no EU or Turkish monograph there are no reported interactions. As with any supplement carrying hormonal claims, it is theoretically right for anyone on hormone therapy, antidiabetics or blood-pressure drugs to tell their doctor.
