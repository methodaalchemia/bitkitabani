Spinach

Spinacia oleracea L.
Evidence: LimitedGenerally safe

Spinach contains calcium, but little of that calcium reaches the body: in the 1988 study 27.6 percent of the calcium in milk was absorbed, and only 5.1 percent of that in spinach. In addition, in the 2007 cohort study more than 40 percent of participants’ oxalate intake came from spinach.

In short

What is Spinach?

Spinach (Spinacia oleracea L.) is a plant of the Amaranthaceae family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia, Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the leaves (fresh or cooked).

What is Spinach used for?

Traditional use: According to Kew’s Plants of the World Online record, the native range of spinach extends from Iran through Central Asia to West Pakistan. Scientific evidence: limited. Knowledge about spinach rests largely on nutrition research.

Is Spinach safe?

Safety label: generally safe. As a food it is safe in ordinary amounts. Four situations call for care. Infants and young children: infants under 3 months are particularly susceptible to methaemoglobinaemia from nitrate and nitrite; according to EFSA a risk cannot be excluded in children aged 1–3 who eat more than 200 grams of spinach in a day, and spinach is not given to a child with a bacterial gastrointestinal infection. If bluish lips or skin appear in an infant, 112 is called. Kidney stones: spinach is rich in oxalate; people with a history of calcium oxalate stones should discuss the amount with their doctor. Warfarin: spinach is rich in vitamin K (below). Eating it raw: in 2006, in an outbreak of *E. coli* O157:H7 linked to fresh spinach in the USA, 225 cases were identified in 27 states, 116 people were hospitalised and 5 died. The sources of this page contain no restriction on eating it as food in pregnancy and breastfeeding.

Traditional use

Iran and Central Asia — origin

According to Kew’s Plants of the World Online record, the native range of spinach extends from Iran through Central Asia to West Pakistan. It is an annual or biennial and grows primarily in the temperate biome. Today it is grown as a leaf vegetable in many parts of the world.

The kitchen — cooked and raw leaves

Spinach is eaten both cooked and raw. A 2016 review (*Food & Function*) places it among the leafy green vegetables regarded as a "functional food" for its vitamin and mineral content and its plant compounds, but also notes that its consumption remains low compared with other leafy greens. The cooking method matters: boiling reduces soluble oxalate more than steaming does, and the oxalate lost passes into the cooking water.

Feeding infants and children — the nitrate warning

Spinach is among the vegetables that accumulate a great deal of nitrate. In 2010 the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) stated that nitrate levels in spinach are not a concern for most children, but that a risk of methaemoglobinaemia cannot be excluded in some children aged 1–3 who eat more than 200 grams of spinach in a day. EFSA recommends that spinach not be given to children with bacterial gastrointestinal infections.

In nature

Where it grows
According to the Kew record its native range extends from Iran through Central Asia to West Pakistan; it grows primarily in the temperate biome. The record does not list Türkiye among its native or introduced ranges; here the plant is a field and garden vegetable. Excessive nitrogen fertilisation increases the nitrate in the leaves.
Harvest season
It is an annual or biennial vegetable. The sources this page relies on give no sowing or harvest months.
Local names
The name ıspanak is in general use; no other local name appears in the sources of this page.

Mix-up risk

Other vegetables with "spinach" in their name are separate species: Malabar spinach (*Basella alba*) and Okinawa spinach (*Gynura bicolor*) are among them. The information on nitrate, oxalate and vitamin K on this page is for *Spinacia oleracea*.

Preparation

It is consumed as food; there is no accepted dose for medicinal use. According to a 2005 study, boiling reduces the soluble oxalate in vegetables by 30–87 percent and steaming by 5–53 percent; because the loss passes into the cooking water, the water is discarded. According to EFSA, keeping cooked leafy vegetables at room temperature for a long time can lead to the conversion of nitrate to nitrite; puréeing speeds up this conversion. For this reason cooked spinach is not kept at room temperature for a long time.

What science says

Knowledge about spinach rests largely on nutrition research. The 2016 review reports that spinach-derived compounds, in cell, animal and human studies, reduce oxidative damage, alter the activity of genes involved in inflammation and metabolism, and limit food intake by increasing the secretion of satiety hormones; these are not evidence for the treatment of a disease. On calcium the result is clear: in a crossover study in 13 healthy adults (1988), calcium absorption averaged 27.6 percent from milk and 5.1 percent from spinach. On kidney stones, a study that assessed three large cohorts together (2007) followed 4,605 new stone cases: in those eating 8 or more servings of spinach a month, compared with those eating fewer than 1 serving a month, the relative risk was 1.30 in men and 1.34 in older women; no association was found in younger women. The authors conclude that these data do not implicate dietary oxalate as a major risk factor for kidney stones.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Warfarin and other vitamin K antagonistsSpinach is rich in vitamin K. In a study of patients on well-controlled warfarin therapy (1986), a single 250 g serving of spinach did not take the clotting test outside the therapeutic range; but when it was given every day for a week, the values tended to exceed the therapeutic limit and dose adjustment was required. A sudden and sustained change in the amount eaten is reported to the doctor.
CalciumBecause the calcium in spinach is bound to oxalate, very little of it is absorbed (5.1 percent; 27.6 percent in milk). Spinach is not counted as a source of calcium.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Spinach.
