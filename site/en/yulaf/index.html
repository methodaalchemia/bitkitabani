Oat

Avena sativa L.
Evidence: ClinicalCaution

Oat’s fame and its official approval do not sit in the same place. Everyone knows oat for cholesterol; Europe’s herbal monographs approve it for sunburn (on the skin) and for sleep (drinking the straw) — and cholesterol appears in neither. The cholesterol side is regulated through a different door: the list of permitted health claims in food law. One plant, two shelves, two languages of evidence. It is one of the cleanest examples of how "officially approved" does not have a single meaning.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the name "yulaf" and lists no synonyms. In English: oats (the grain), oatstraw (the herb). The monographs cover two separate herbal drugs, approved for quite different things: the fruit (grain, oat flour) on the skin, the aerial parts (oatstraw) by mouth.

In short

What is Oat?

Oat (Avena sativa L.) is a plant of the Poaceae (grass family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the dried or fresh aerial parts gathered before flowering; the fruit.

What is Oat used for?

Traditional use: The European Medicines Agency has published two monographs for oat, both under "traditional use". Scientific evidence: clinical. There is evidence for oat — but not where the monograph approves it.

Is Oat safe?

Safety label: caution. People with coeliac disease must not use it — the Turkish monograph’s explicit provision. The EU monograph puts it more softly ("caution is advised because data on the protein content are not available"), but the reasoning is the same: pure oat contains no gluten itself, yet there is a risk of contamination with wheat, barley and rye and uncertainty about the protein content. People with disorders of the oesophagus, stomach or intestine are also advised not to use it. Cutaneous use of the fruit is not recommended in patients with atopic dermatitis, contact dermatitis or a predisposition to allergy — that is, the group for whom oat baths are most often recommended is the group the monograph is most cautious about. Oral use is not recommended under 12, nor in pregnancy or lactation. Oatstraw may affect the ability to drive and operate machinery. Hypersensitivity to the active substance can occur.

Traditional use

Europe — two drugs, two separate indications

The European Medicines Agency has published two monographs for oat, both under "traditional use". The fruit (Avenae fructus): symptomatic treatment of minor inflammations of the skin, such as sunburn, and as an aid in healing minor wounds — cutaneous use. The aerial parts (Avenae herba): relief of mild symptoms of mental stress and to aid sleep — oral use. Note what is absent from both: cholesterol.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph lists both parts and puts its firmest limit first: it must not be used by people with coeliac disease. People with disorders of the oesophagus, stomach or intestine are also advised not to use it. For the aerial parts one drug interaction is reported, and it matters: it may interact with insulin and other antidiabetic medicines and cause blood glucose to fall too far.

As a food — an authorised health claim

Oat is a plant regulated outside the herbal-medicine shelf as well. The European Union’s list of permitted health claims (Regulation 432/2012) carries two claims relating to oat beta-glucan: that beta-glucans contribute to the maintenance of normal blood cholesterol levels (on condition the consumer is told the beneficial effect is obtained with a daily intake of 3 g of beta-glucans), and that oat or barley beta-glucan taken as part of a meal contributes to the reduction of the blood glucose rise after that meal. The same plant, then, assessed on two separate regulatory tracks in two separate languages of evidence.

In nature

Where it grows
A cereal of the temperate zone, grown in Turkey too. It tolerates cool, damp weather better than barley or wheat, so it is widespread in northern climates.
Harvest season
It ripens in summer. The aerial parts used as straw are gathered before flowering; the grain is harvested at maturity.
Local names
Yulaf — the Turkish monograph gives one Turkish name.

Mix-up risk

The grain and the straw are two distinct herbal drugs with separate indications; using one in place of the other departs from the monograph. ⚠️ For coeliac disease the critical point is not species confusion but product contamination: oat is grown in the same fields, milled in the same mills and processed in the same plants as wheat and barley, so an oat product without a gluten-free label cannot be treated as safe for someone with coeliac disease.

Preparation

On the skin: the EU monograph gives 60 g of oat flour for a bath of 150–200 litres (half that dose for children), plus colloidal extracts of the flour at concentrations up to 20–30%, and liquid paraffin with 5% oatmeal. There is no age restriction for cutaneous use. By mouth (the straw): a single dose of 3 g as an infusion, for those over 12. If symptoms do not resolve within a week, consult a doctor or pharmacist. Oatstraw may affect the ability to drive and operate machinery; the Turkish monograph says it must then not be used at all.

What science says

There is evidence for oat — but not where the monograph approves it. A systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2024 in Food & Function pooled 17 randomised trials (1,731 people) lasting at least four weeks in people with dyslipidaemia: oat-based products lowered LDL cholesterol by an average of 0.24 mmol/L (about 9 mg/dL) and total cholesterol by 0.32 mmol/L (about 12 mg/dL), at moderate certainty on GRADE. The effect on HDL and triglycerides was little to none. Three things need reading together: the effect is real but modest; the included trials were judged to be at high risk of bias or to raise some concerns; and no significant difference was shown for major cardiovascular events — so what falls is a number, and what is proven is not an outcome. A meta-analysis published in the same journal in 2026 examined cereal beta-glucan separately in overweight and obese people. The two uses the monograph does approve, meanwhile — mild skin inflammation topically, tension and sleep orally — rest on long use; for those indications there is no trial of oat on its own. We record the evidence level as Clinical, which is exactly why we say plainly which use it is for.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Insulin and other antidiabetic medicinesThe Turkish monograph reports this interaction for the aerial parts: it may cause blood glucose to fall too far. The mechanism is consistent — one of the EU’s own authorised health claims states that oat beta-glucan reduces the rise in blood glucose after a meal. Two things acting in the same direction can take glucose down too far. Someone on diabetes medication does not need to give up oats; they do need to tell their doctor if they start eating a lot of it regularly.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographsNeither European monograph lists anything in its interaction section. "None reported" is not evidence of no interaction — and here the Turkish monograph does report one.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Oat.
