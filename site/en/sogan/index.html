Onion

Allium cepa L.
Evidence: LimitedHigh risk

The World Health Organization wrote a monograph for a vegetable and dealt with the safety section in one sentence: its safety is shown by its being eaten worldwide as a vegetable. No plant in this catalog has safety evidence resting on a larger sample. Yet onion’s best-selling medical product — the scar gel — came out no better than other treatments in meta-analysis. The onion in the kitchen and the onion in the pharmacy are two evidence stories from one plant.

In short

What is Onion?

Onion (Allium cepa L.) is a plant of the Amaryllidaceae (amaryllis family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Asia and Siberia, Europe. Part used: fresh or dried onion bulb.

What is Onion used for?

Traditional use: Onion is in the first volume of the World Health Organization’s medicinal plant monographs. Scientific evidence: limited. Onion has two separate lines of evidence.

Is Onion safe?

Safety label: high risk. The WHO monograph gives only onion allergy as a contraindication and reports no warnings; allergic rhinoconjunctivitis and contact dermatitis (especially in kitchen workers who chop onions) have been reported as adverse reactions. For pregnancy, lactation, children and drug interactions the monograph says "none reported" — no safety problem is expected at vegetable doses. At supplement doses (extract, capsule), because of possible effects on blood glucose and clotting, anyone on antidiabetics or anticoagulants would sensibly tell their doctor — a theoretical note.

Traditional use

WHO monograph (1999)

Onion is in the first volume of the World Health Organization’s medicinal plant monographs. Under "uses supported by clinical data": prevention of age-dependent changes in the blood vessels, and loss of appetite; in pharmacopoeias and traditional systems: bacterial infections such as dysentery, as a diuretic, for wounds and scars, asthma, and as adjuvant therapy in diabetes. Daily dose 50 g fresh onion or 20 g dried drug. The monograph’s safety sentence is the shortest in this catalog: "the level of safety ... is reflected by its worldwide use as a vegetable."

Turkey

There is no monograph for onion on the Turkish list — as a vegetable, none was thought necessary. The use of onion for wounds, boils and coughs in Anatolian folk medicine is widely known; this catalog does not extend those uses without a source.

World — among the oldest vegetables

Onion originates in central Asia; it appears in Egyptian, Mesopotamian and Hittite sources and is today one of the world’s most cultivated vegetables. Onion extract as a scar gel is a commercial product in Europe and America — the science section deals with that product separately.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to central Asia; cultivated everywhere. Turkey is among the large producers.
Harvest season
Biennial; the bulb forms in the first year, is harvested in late summer, dried and stored.
Local names
Soğan, baş soğan, kuru soğan; regional names not extended without a source.

Mix-up risk

Confusion with wild Allium species happens in the field, not the kitchen; some bulbous look-alikes (e.g. Colchicum, autumn crocus) are poisonous and must not be gathered as wild "onion".

Preparation

The WHO monograph gives a daily dose of 50 g fresh onion or 20 g dried drug, with preparations dosed accordingly. Being a vegetable it has no separate "preparation"; the onion extract gels sold for scars are separate products.

What science says

Onion has two separate lines of evidence. Metabolic parameters: a systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2023 in Clinical Nutrition ESPEN pooled 14 randomised trials: onion supplementation significantly improved body fat percentage, LDL and total cholesterol and systolic blood pressure; with use longer than 12 weeks and above 300 mg/day, weight, waist circumference and body mass index differed too. That is the current counterpart of what the WHO wrote in 1999 as "age-dependent changes in the blood vessels". Scars: onion extract gel is the best-selling onion product; but the title of a meta-analysis published in 2021 in the International Wound Journal sums up the result — onion extract gel is not better than other topical treatments in scar management. So the famous product has weaker evidence than the unfamous use. Effect sizes are modest and trial quality mixed; evidence level Limited.

No interactions reported in the WHO monographThe monograph reports no drug interactions. Being of the same genus as garlic, the anticoagulant caution in the garlic record may theoretically be kept in mind for onion at supplement doses; no such caution applies to onion eaten as a vegetable.
