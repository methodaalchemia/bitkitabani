A shaman’s pouch: a thousand-year-old trace of ayahuasca in Bolivia

Looking for plant remains in archaeology usually means washing soil, counting seeds and measuring them. But sometimes the plant itself disappears and only its chemistry is left. A thousand-year-old leather bag found in a rock shelter in southwestern Bolivia is the most striking example: in a sample scraped from the surface of a small pouch inside it, two of the essential components of the drink we now call ayahuasca were detected together. This article is about that find — and just as much about where the four researchers who published it stopped and said “we do not know this”.

A rock shelter at 3,890 metres

The find comes from the Lípez highlands of southwestern Bolivia, in the valley of the Sora River. The valley is a narrow basin squeezed between two parallel ignimbrite surfaces running north to south. One of the rock shelters cut into those surfaces is known locally as Cueva del Chileno; it lies 3,890 metres above sea level. It was excavated in 2008 and 2010 [1].

The shelter holds traces of intermittent occupation spread over the last four thousand years; the earliest measurement reaches 2136–1778 BC. But the bag this article is about is much later: a small sample taken from the leather was dated by accelerator mass spectrometry and returned a calibrated range of AD 905–1170 [1].

One detail deserves care here. What was dated is the leather of the bag. The plant fragment inside it, the fox-snout pouch and the wooden tablets were not dated separately. So it is more accurate to say “a thousand-year-old bag” than “a thousand-year-old compound”.

In period terms this range falls at the time when the Tiwanaku state broke up and regional polities took its place. The paper does not label the pouch as a Tiwanaku find; the style of the snuff tablets is linked to the Tiwanaku sphere, and that is all [1].

What was inside the bag

The paper’s inventory runs as follows: an outer leather bag (280 × 165 millimetres); two wooden snuff tablets, skilfully carved and decorated with anthropomorphic figurines; an anthropomorphic snuff tube with two braids of real human hair tied to it; a hide pouch made by sewing together three Andean fox snouts; two llama bone spatulas; two small dried plant fragments tied to cords of wool and plant fibre; and a multicoloured woven headband [1].

That inventory holds ten items. Outside the pouch, in the debris, loose hair braids, turquoise beads and a grinding stone were also found — these are not part of the pouch [1].

Analysis was carried out on only two of those ten items: the fox-snout pouch and the dried plant fragment. The tablets, the tube, the spatulas, the headband and the leather bag itself were not tested. This is the detail most often skipped when the find is described: the compounds came not from the wooden tablets but from a sample scraped off the pouch’s surface.

The compounds found in the pouch

The method was liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry. Five compounds were detected in the sample scraped from the fox-snout pouch: cocaine, benzoylecgonine (a breakdown product of cocaine), bufotenine, harmine and dimethyltryptamine, that is, DMT [1].

The dried plant fragment yielded only three of those five: cocaine, benzoylecgonine and bufotenine. Harmine and DMT are absent from that sample [1].

A sixth compound, psilocin, is described more cautiously. The paper notes that psilocin was not on the initial screening list, but that a peak corresponding to it appeared and suggests it was present in the sample; it also mentions, as one possibility, that the source could be a psilocin-containing mushroom [1]. The paper’s own abstract does not count psilocin. So “six compounds were found” would be wrong: five compounds were detected, and in addition a peak corresponding to psilocin was observed.

Which compound points to which plant

For cocaine and benzoylecgonine the paper speaks directly: they show that coca leaf was carried in the pouch [1].

Bufotenine is a distinguishing compound. The paper reads it as evidence that seeds of Anadenanthera — the genus of yopo and its relatives — were carried in the pouch [1]. Seeds of this genus have also been identified directly under the microscope in archaeological snuff powders: Anadenanthera colubrina var. cebil has been detected in powdered archaeological material in northwestern Argentina [8].

DMT, by contrast, is not distinguishing. The paper leaves two possibilities open: either the DMT comes from the same Anadenanthera material that produced the bufotenine, or from an independent source — a Psychotria species such as chacruna, for instance [1]. So “DMT was found” does not mean “chacruna was found”.

For harmine the paper’s reasoning is comparative: among South American plants, harmine occurs in the greatest quantity in the ayahuasca vine vine, Banisteriopsis caapi [1]. Honesty is needed here: harmine and harmaline also occur in other plants such as syrian rue, and the main alkaloids of that plant are precisely harmalol, harmaline and harmine [7]. But the paper never discusses Syrian rue or any other source of harmine; it limits its statement to “among South American plants”.

Why two plants are needed

The most discussed aspect of the find is that harmine and DMT appeared together, because these two are the essential components of the drink called ayahuasca. So why two separate plants?

The mechanism was described in 1984. Banisteriopsis caapi contains harmine, harmaline and tetrahydroharmine; the admixture plants supply the DMT. DMT is a powerful hallucinogen but is inactive by mouth, because it is broken down in the digestive system by the enzyme monoamine oxidase. Beta-carbolines are strong and reversible inhibitors of that enzyme; it has been proposed that they protect DMT from breakdown and so make it orally active [3]. The same study adds two further cautions: as of 1984 the mechanism had not been confirmed experimentally, and the combined effect of the beta-carbolines was found to be additive rather than synergistic [3]. The mechanism was measured in humans in 2003 [4].

The content of the plants is not fixed either. In samples collected from twenty-two separate locations in Brazil, harmine, harmaline and tetrahydroharmine were present in detectable amounts in every Banisteriopsis caapi sample; but in some Psychotria viridis samples DMT was either very low or absent [5].

This mechanism also has a direct medical counterpart, and it needs saying here: a warning published in 1998 holds that beta-carbolines inhibiting monoamine oxidase carry a risk of serious adverse interaction with serotonin reuptake inhibitor medicines [6]. What makes these plants orally active is the same thing that makes them risky.

What the paper does not say

Some of the sentences circulating about this find go beyond the paper. The authors’ own cautions are these.

The compounds may be external contamination. The paper writes that the tested sample “may reflect the chemistry of objects that plant surface came into contact with”, and that “some, perhaps all” of these compounds may have come from outside. It also states plainly that more analysis is needed to identify the species of the dried plant fragment [1].

There is no firm evidence for ayahuasca. In the paper’s own words: the results do not provide conclusive evidence for early use of ayahuasca in the form seen among indigenous South American communities today — with Banisteriopsis and Psychotria used together — but they are evidence that this combination may have been in use a thousand years ago [1].

It was not shown that these plants were actually consumed. There are no human remains in the context of the find, and the paper states that there is no evidence of direct consumption [1].

The route of use cannot be read from the chemistry. The two snuff tablets, the tube and the spatulas in the kit point strongly to a snuff route, and the authors propose that route for the Anadenanthera seeds; but for the harmine-and-DMT combination they leave open both a drink and a compound snuff [1].

And this is not “the earliest evidence”. The paper does not say “first” or “earliest”. Its claim is quantitative: the largest number of compounds obtained to date from a single find in this part of the world, and the highest number of psychoactives reported in a single archaeological kit in South America [1]. The phrase “conclusive evidence” is used only for the presence of at least three psychoactive plants; for their being used together, the paper says “may have been” [1].

Not a single find

If Cueva del Chileno stood alone it would be hard to interpret. It does not.

Northeast of Lake Titicaca, in the Eastern Cordillera at about 3,500 metres, a collective tomb at a place called Niño Korin produced five wooden tablets, reed tubes, enema equipment and spatulas; the tablets are in Tiwanaku style. The 1972 monograph that published the find describes it as the kit of a “medicine man” [12]. We could not find a consistent published value for its dating, so we give no date here.

The “Pallqa” pouch, found in 1998 in the Amaguaya valley near La Paz and attributed to the Tiwanaku period, belongs to the same class of find: it has been studied as the medicine bundle of a priest or healer [13].

The chemical side does not rest on a single study either. In northern Chile, harmine has been detected in human hair from the Middle Period (AD 500–1000) [10]. That study carries a notable caution of its own: harmine found alone may point to medicinal rather than hallucinogenic use. The pre-Hispanic snuff powders of San Pedro de Atacama were analysed chemically in 1991 [9]. Which plant supplied the snuff used at Chavín de Huántar has been argued through iconography [11] — the archaeological and historical trace of the san Pedro cactus cactus in that region is a literature of its own.

For coca the chain runs further back still. Coca leaves found on house floors in the Nanchoc valley of northern Peru have been dated to at least 8,000 calibrated years ago; alongside them were pieces of calcite used to extract the alkaloid from the leaf [14]. A distinction matters here: this date comes from plant remains and context, not from hair or dental analysis.

What a palm-sized pouch says

The most thought-provoking thing about the find is not the number of compounds but where they came from. None of the plants the pouch points to grows on that highland at 3,890 metres. In the paper’s own words: the presence together of several plants from disconnected and distant ecological zones of South America suggests that hallucinogenic plants were carried over significant distances, and that a detailed botanical knowledge sat within pre-Columbian ritual practice [1].

So this bag is not a plant list but the evidence of a network. Someone had to know which plant grew where, which worked together with which, and how to have it brought from hundreds of kilometres away.

This article describes an archaeological and historical finding; it is not a recommendation for use. Safety, legal status and drug interaction information for the plants is on their own pages.
