Plants used for the common cold: what they do and what they do not

The common cold is a self-limiting illness, and that single property accounts for both the prevalence of herbal use in this area and its evidence problem. Most of the plants in this review do not touch the course of the illness; they coat the surface of the throat, loosen phlegm or ease a cough. Only a few carry a claim about the illness itself, and it is there that the evidence is most contested. This review covers that distinction, the standing of these plants in the monographs, and the records that call for caution; it contains no dosage information.

Scope and method

The plants in this review were selected on two criteria: either an indication relating to the common cold, cough or throat complaints is covered by a European Medicines Agency (EMA) herbal monograph, or the catalogue holds a recorded traditional use for that purpose [1]. Parts used, safety information and restrictions are given on each plant’s own record page; dosage and preparation have deliberately been left out.

A self-limiting illness

The common cold is an upper respiratory infection caused by several hundred different viruses and cleared by the immune system on its own. Treated or untreated, it passes within a week to ten days.

This property acts on herbal use here in two ways. First, the tendency to associate recovery with whatever was last applied is strongest here; the common cold is accordingly the area in which traditional remedies appear most effective. Second, and more importantly, it is hardest here to show that an intervention works: everyone in the placebo arm recovers too, so large and well-constructed trials are needed for any difference to emerge.

The great majority of the plants below make no claim about the course of the illness in any case. They relieve a symptom, and often they do so mechanically.

Demulcents: mucilage

The effect of this group is physical rather than chemical. The mucilage they contain is a polysaccharide that forms a gel with water and reduces irritation by coating the surface of the throat. This is why they are used for dry cough.

The root of marshmallow, marshmallow (Althaea officinalis), is high in mucilage; the EMA recognises it for irritation of the mouth and throat and for dry cough under traditional use [1]. For common mallow, common mallow (Malva sylvestris), the EMA recognises the same indications. The flower of mullein, mullein (Verbascum spp.), is recorded under traditional use for cough and throat irritation; human studies are few. The inner bark of slippery elm, slippery elm (Ulmus rubra), is accepted by the US FDA as an over-the-counter throat demulcent [2]. The glucomannan in the tuber of salep orchids (Orchis spp.) also soothes the throat; there are no clinical studies for medicinal claims.

This group shares a restriction that is easily overlooked: mucilage delays the absorption of other medicines. People on regular medication need to leave one to two hours between these plants and their medicines. Mullein carries a further restriction: the hairs on its leaves and flowers irritate the throat, and its tea should not be drunk unstrained.

Consuming salep is safe, but the real risk lies with the plant itself: thousands of orchid tubers are dug up for a single kilogram of it.

Cough and the airways

thyme and oregano, thyme (Thymus vulgaris), contains thymol and carvacrol, whose antimicrobial effect has been shown in the laboratory. The EMA recognises thyme as a traditional herbal medicine for productive cough [1]. It is safe in culinary amounts, but pure thyme oil is caustic: it must not be applied undiluted to the skin or swallowed.

The oil of eucalyptus, eucalyptus (Eucalyptus globulus), is recognised by the EMA under traditional use for relieving cough in the common cold; controlled trials have been carried out in bronchitis and sinusitis with its principal constituent, cineole [1]. The most serious safety restriction in this review sits here: even a few millilitres of the volatile oil, if swallowed, can cause seizures and loss of consciousness in children; it must not be applied to the face or nose of infants and small children; and in people with asthma the vapour can trigger an attack.

The root of licorice, liquorice (Glycyrrhiza glabra), is recognised by the EMA under traditional use for cough and mild stomach complaints [1]. Regular consumption in high amounts raises blood pressure, lowers potassium and can cause cardiac arrhythmia; people with hypertension, heart or kidney disease, and pregnant women, should avoid it.

Inflammation of the throat

For greek sage, sage (Salvia officinalis), the EMA recognises inflammation of the mouth and throat under traditional use [1]. Because it contains thujone it must not be used at high doses or over long periods; it is not recommended in pregnancy, breastfeeding or epilepsy. The TİTCK monograph advises against use in people under eighteen and carries a warning for people on diabetes medication, since it lowers blood sugar.

For chamomile, chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla), inflammation of the mouth and throat is among the indications the EMA monograph covers [1]. It can cause allergy in people allergic to the daisy family.

The eugenol in the oil of clove, clove (Syzygium aromaticum), has long been used in dentistry as a local anaesthetic and antiseptic; the EMA recognises clove oil under traditional use for inflammation of the mouth and throat and for toothache [1]. It is safe as a spice, but the pure volatile oil, if swallowed, can cause liver injury and seizures, particularly in children.

The two records that make a claim about the illness

The plants above relieve symptoms. Two records make a claim about the illness itself — about its duration or its onset — and it is precisely where expectation is highest that the evidence is most contested.

For echinacea, echinacea (Echinacea purpurea), the EMA recognises the aerial parts for the short-term treatment of the common cold. NCCIH holds that it may have a small effect in preventing or shortening a cold, but that results are inconsistent [1][3]. It can cause allergy in people allergic to the daisy family, and people with autoimmune disease need to be careful.

The flower of elder, elder (Sambucus nigra), is recognised by the EMA under traditional use for the early symptoms of a cold. Small studies with the fruit extract have reported a shortened duration of influenza; the evidence is weak [1]. Elder carries a separate and firm preparation restriction: the raw fruit, leaves, bark and stems contain cyanogenic compounds and cause nausea and vomiting. The fruit must always be cooked.

The most accurate sentence available for these two records is this: they have not been shown to be ineffective, but neither have they been shown to be effective. In a self-limiting illness that uncertainty is unsurprising.

Rosehip and vitamin C

rose hip, rosehip (Rosa canina), is the most widespread cold-season tea in Anatolia, and the reason given is usually vitamin C. The catalogue record does not confirm that reason as it stands: the fresh fruit really is very rich in vitamin C, but drying and boiling reduce it substantially [2].

The vitamin C content of a tea brewed from dried rosehip is therefore not comparable to that of the fresh fruit. This does not mean the tea should not be drunk; taking warm fluids has a soothing effect of its own in a throat complaint. But that effect does not come from vitamin C, and there is no record of rosehip altering the course of a cold.

The hairs around the seeds irritate the mouth and the intestine and should not be consumed unstrained. The TİTCK monograph carries warnings for diabetes, G6PD deficiency, and disorders of iron metabolism such as haemochromatosis, thalassaemia and anaemia.

Two records that call for caution

ephedra (Ephedra sinica) contains ephedrine and pseudoephedrine, which open the bronchi and stimulate the heart. It is the highest-risk record in this review: it can raise blood pressure and heart rate dangerously, and strokes, heart attacks and deaths have been reported. Ephedra-containing weight-loss products were banned in the United States in 2004 over serious cardiac events [2]. Its place here is not as an option, but because it is the source of the pseudoephedrine in cold medicines and because the plant form cannot be dosed with any precision.

goldenseal, goldenseal (Hydrastis canadensis), appears frequently in supplements sold for colds. NCCIH states that there is no reliable evidence for any use, the common cold included [3]. The interaction side, by contrast, is clear: it slows the hepatic breakdown of many medicines. It must not be used in pregnancy, during breastfeeding, or in newborns.

The difference between monograph types

The phrase “the EMA recognises it under traditional use”, frequent above, is not a claim of efficacy. A herbal product reaches the market in the European Union by different regulatory routes, two of which diverge at the level of the monograph [1].

On the well-established use route (Directive 2001/83/EC, Article 10a), scientific literature must establish that the active substances have been in well-established medicinal use within the European Union for at least ten years, with recognised efficacy and an acceptable level of safety [1].

On traditional use registration (Article 16a(1)), the plant must have been in use for at least thirty years, of which at least fifteen within the European Union. No clinical trials are required; sufficient safety data and plausible efficacy are enough [1].

Almost all the records in this review fall into the second category. In the field of the common cold this is an expected outcome: for a self-limiting illness, the scale of trial needed to demonstrate efficacy has not been reached for most of these plants.

When to see a doctor

A cold resolves on its own, but not every upper respiratory complaint is a cold, and some require treatment. Herbal use should not delay this.

The following should not be deferred by a herbal product: shortness of breath or rapid breathing, chest pain, a high fever lasting more than three days or one that falls and rises again, complaints that fail to improve after ten days or that worsen again after improving, severe one-sided sore throat with difficulty swallowing, marked swelling in the neck, earache, severe headache with neck stiffness, and difficulty feeding with marked lethargy in infants.

Where asthma, heart disease, immunosuppression or pregnancy is involved, the threshold is lower. For people on regular medication, some of the plants in this review carry recorded absorption delays and interactions; the interaction information is given on each plant’s own record page.
