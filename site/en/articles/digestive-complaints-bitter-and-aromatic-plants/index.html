Plants used for digestive complaints: two groups, two mechanisms

The plants used for indigestion and gas across different cultures fall into two sensory groups: the markedly bitter ones and the aromatic ones rich in volatile oils. The distinction corresponds to the amara and carminative classes of traditional pharmacy and is explained by two different mechanisms of action. This review covers both groups, their standing in European Medicines Agency monographs and the current level of clinical evidence; it contains no dosage information.

Scope and method

The plants in this review were selected on two criteria: either the relevant indication is covered by a European Medicines Agency (EMA) herbal monograph, or the catalogue holds a recorded traditional use for this complaint [1]. The parts used, the safety information and the restrictions for each plant are given on its own record page. Dosage and preparation have deliberately been left out of this review; that information should not be read apart from the safety restrictions.

Two groups, two mechanisms

Indigestion is among the oldest complaints in recorded medical history, and every independently developed medical tradition has an answer to it. The plants those traditions reach for are not randomly distributed; they cluster around two sensory properties.

The first group consists of plants with a marked bitterness. The view that bitter compounds stimulate digestive secretions formed the basis of the amara class in European pharmacy. The second group comprises aromatic plants rich in volatile oils; the relaxing effect of these oils on intestinal smooth muscle is the ground of the carminative classification. The first group answers to sluggish digestion, the second to tension and bloating.

Bitter plants

The clearest European example of this group is wormwood, wormwood (Artemisia absinthium); the species name points directly to the bitterness. The EMA monograph for Absinthii herba covers loss of appetite and mild digestive complaints [1]. Gentler members of the same class are dandelion, dandelion (Taraxacum officinale), and yarrow, yarrow (Achillea millefolium); both have monographs, and both were accepted on the basis of traditional use [1].

milk thistle, milk thistle (Silybum marianum), holds a separate position in this group. It is better known for the liver studies conducted on the silymarin in its seeds, yet its monograph covers digestive complaints as well [1].

The evidence for the bitter group can be summarised as follows: the mechanism is defensible, the clinical evidence limited. Experimental data exist on the effect of bitter compounds on secretion; for most of these plants, however, there are no controlled trials on the scale required for a claim of efficacy. That the monographs were issued on a traditional-use basis reflects this.

Aromatic plants

The second group has a far wider geographical distribution. In Anatolia mint, peppermint (Mentha × piperita), fennel, fennel (Foeniculum vulgare), chamomile, chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla), lemon balm, lemon balm (Melissa officinalis), cumin, cumin (Cuminum cyminum), and rosemary, rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus), are used for this purpose. In the Andes, around Cusco, muña (Minthostachys mollis) is recorded for the same function, and in the Amazon basin matico (Piper aduncum).

There is no close kinship among these species, and no data indicating transmission between the communities that use them. What they share is chemical: all are rich in volatile oils, and the relaxing effect of these compounds on smooth muscle is common to them. This constitutes a case of convergence, in which separate traditions arrived at the same group of plants independently of any knowledge of the mechanism.

Peppermint: the strongest clinical evidence in the group

While most of the aromatic plants remain at the level of traditional use, peppermint oil has been the subject of controlled clinical trials. A meta-analysis published in 2019 pooled twelve randomised trials covering 835 patients and found enteric-coated peppermint oil significantly superior to placebo for the symptoms of irritable bowel syndrome [2].

An update published in 2022 renewed the analysis, noting that trials published in the interval had called the result into question. The updated finding again favours peppermint oil; the effect size is stated more cautiously [3]. The catalogue therefore records peppermint at the clinical level of evidence, the only such case in the digestive group.

The same mechanism carries a limitation. Peppermint oil also relaxes the lower oesophageal sphincter, and may worsen symptoms in people with gastro-oesophageal reflux. An effect that benefits one group constitutes a contraindication in another.

Ginger and turmeric

ginger, ginger (Zingiber officinale), does not fall squarely into either group; its pungency comes from gingerol derivatives and its effect is discussed in terms of gastric emptying rate. The EMA monograph for Zingiberis rhizoma covers digestive complaints [1]. The monograph for turmeric, turmeric (Curcuma longa), carries the same indication [1].

For both plants the difference between culinary and supplement use is decisive. The amounts consumed as a spice are not comparable to those in capsules containing standardised extract; cases of liver injury associated with high-dose turmeric capsules have been reported. It cannot therefore be assumed that a plant in common kitchen use carries the same safety profile in supplement form.

Liquorice root and its systemic effects

licorice, liquorice root (Glycyrrhiza glabra), falls outside both sensory groups; it is sweet rather than bitter. The EMA monograph for Liquiritiae radix covers digestive complaints [1].

The plant differs from the other records in this review in its systemic effects. Regular consumption in high amounts raises blood pressure, lowers serum potassium and can lead to cardiac arrhythmia. It is not recommended for people with hypertension, heart failure or kidney disease, or during pregnancy. Assessing liquorice root in the same category as the other digestive plants is therefore not appropriate.

The difference between monograph types

The phrase “the EMA monograph covers”, repeated above, does not by itself amount to a claim of efficacy. A herbal product reaches the market in the European Union by different regulatory routes, two of which diverge at the level of the monograph [1].

On the well-established use route (Directive 2001/83/EC, Article 10a), scientific literature must establish that the active substances have been in well-established medicinal use within the European Union for at least ten years, with recognised efficacy and an acceptable level of safety [1].

On traditional use registration (Directive 2001/83/EC, Article 16a(1)), the plant must have been in use for at least thirty years, of which at least fifteen within the European Union. No clinical trials on safety and efficacy are required; sufficient safety data and plausible efficacy are enough [1].

Most of the plants in this review fall into the second category. That a plant has a monograph therefore shows that its use rests on a regulatory framework; it does not show that its effect has been clinically demonstrated. Keeping the two categories apart is decisive for transmitting knowledge in this field accurately.

When to see a doctor

The great majority of indigestion complaints resolve on their own. This is one of the factors that explains how widespread traditional uses are: when a complaint resolves by itself, there is a tendency to associate the recovery with whatever was last applied.

The following findings are not assessed as indigestion and must not be deferred by herbal use: difficulty swallowing, haematemesis (blood in vomit), melaena (black stools), unintentional weight loss, a complaint lasting more than two weeks without remission, pain radiating to the chest, and a complaint beginning for the first time after the age of fifty.

For people on regular medication, pregnant women and those breastfeeding, some of the plants in this review carry drug interactions and restrictions on use. The interaction information is given on each plant’s own record page.
