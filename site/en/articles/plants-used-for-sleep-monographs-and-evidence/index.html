Plants used for sleep: why monograph level and trial results do not line up

In the plants used for sleep, the level of regulatory acceptance and the results of clinical trials line up the opposite way to what one might expect. The plant with the stronger monograph has mixed sleep trials; the plant with the sounder trials has evidence for anxiety rather than sleep. This review covers that mismatch, the evidence for each plant on the sleep shelf, the plants that disrupt sleep, and the interaction the group shares; it contains no dosage information.

Scope and method

The plants in this review were selected on two criteria: either an indication related to sleep or nervous tension is covered by a European Medicines Agency (EMA) herbal monograph, or the catalogue holds a recorded traditional use for that purpose [1]. In addition, plants not used for sleep but recorded as causing insomnia are treated in a separate section. Parts used, safety information and restrictions are given on each plant’s own record page. Dosage and preparation have deliberately been left out.

Valerian: the stronger monograph, mixed results

valerian, valerian (Valeriana officinalis), stands highest in this group in regulatory terms. The EMA recognises valerian root for mild nervous tension and sleep disorders under well-established use [1]. This is the upper tier of the monograph categories and requires clinical data.

The trials, however, are mixed: some find a small improvement in time to fall asleep, others find no difference from placebo [1]. The plant’s regulatory standing is therefore clearer than the picture drawn by the individual studies.

Its restrictions are mild but definite: it can cause morning drowsiness and headache, it should not be combined with alcohol or sedatives, rare cases of liver injury have been reported, and it should not be taken before driving.

Lavender: the sounder trials, but not for sleep

lavender, lavender (Lavandula angustifolia), occupies a particular place in this review and is easily misread. Standardised oral lavender oil capsules have been found superior to placebo in a number of controlled trials — but those trials concern anxiety disorder [1].

The EMA, for its part, recognises lavender for mild stress and sleep under traditional use only [1]. The statement “lavender has clinical evidence” is therefore accurate, but its subject is not sleep. Where anxiety is what disturbs sleep an indirect connection can be drawn; that is not the same as an effect demonstrated on sleep itself.

The capsule form can cause belching and nausea and may increase the effect of sedative medicines. The Turkish Medicines Agency (TİTCK) monograph advises against use during pregnancy and breastfeeding, and caution alongside blood thinners and antiplatelet medicines.

Lemon balm, chamomile and linden

For lemon balm, lemon balm (Melissa officinalis), the EMA recognises mild stress, sleep disorders and mild digestive complaints under traditional use; small clinical trials have reported improvement in anxiety and sleep [1]. There is a restriction for people taking thyroid medication.

chamomile, chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla), is widely used for insomnia in folk practice, yet the indications its EMA monograph covers are digestive complaints, inflammation of the mouth and throat, and skin irritation; sleep is not among them. Small trials on anxiety have been positive [1]. It can cause allergy in people allergic to the daisy family.

For linden, linden (Tilia spp.), the EMA recognises symptoms of the common cold and mild mental stress under traditional use; clinical studies are few [1]. It has no known side effects, the only firm bar being allergy to the flower; the TİTCK monograph reports an interaction with lithium.

Plants that disrupt sleep

In a complaint of insomnia, what is being taken for other purposes matters as much as what is being taken for sleep. Several plants recorded as causing insomnia are sold as herbal supplements on the same shelves as the sleep ones.

For ginseng (Panax ginseng), insomnia is among the recorded adverse effects; prolonged use at high doses may produce hypertension, insomnia, irritability and muscle stiffness. roseroot, roseroot (Rhodiola rosea), can cause insomnia and restlessness.

Caffeine-bearing plants form a separate cluster: tea, tea (Camellia sinensis), guaraná (Paullinia cupana) and guayusa (Ilex guayusa). Guaraná seeds carry a higher proportion of caffeine than coffee beans, and in energy drinks it may be combined with other stimulants. All three carry restrictions for people with cardiac rhythm problems, anxiety or insomnia.

The first place to look in a complaint of insomnia may therefore not be a sleep plant but the list of what is already being taken.

Ashwagandha: sold on the sleep shelf, evidenced elsewhere

ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) has in recent years become one of the world’s best-selling supplements for stress and sleep. A few small, short controlled trials have reported reductions in stress and anxiety scores; NCCIH notes that these trials were small and short and that long-term safety is unknown [2].

The safety side is clearer than that uncertainty and runs counter to the sales volume: cases of liver injury have been reported, it may raise thyroid hormones, and it must not be used in pregnancy because of the risk of miscarriage. Some European countries have restricted its supplements over safety concerns.

The one interaction the group shares

The sedative plants in this review come from different families and contain different compounds, yet they converge on a single line in the interaction tables: for valerian, lemon balm and lavender alike, a restriction on use alongside alcohol and sedative medicines is recorded.

The practical consequence of this cannot be read off any one plant’s safety profile. Taking several herbal products for sleep together, or adding them to a prescribed sleep medicine, compounds effects that run in the same direction. The warning on valerian’s record against taking it before driving has the same ground.

Opium poppy and a practice that kills children

opium poppy, the opium poppy (Papaver somniferum), owes its species name to sleep, and the latex of its capsule contains morphine and codeine. A significant part of modern analgesics is still derived from the poppy; Türkiye is one of the few countries licensed to produce this raw material [2].

Using the plant as a home remedy for sleep is lethal, particularly in children. The capsule and its latex are addictive and can arrest breathing. Its place in this review is not as an option that may be reached for, but because it has been used for this purpose throughout history and because that use kills.

The difference between monograph types

The phrases “well-established use” and “traditional use” above are not synonyms. A herbal product reaches the market in the European Union by different regulatory routes, two of which diverge at the level of the monograph [1].

On the well-established use route (Directive 2001/83/EC, Article 10a), scientific literature must establish that the active substances have been in well-established medicinal use within the European Union for at least ten years, with recognised efficacy and an acceptable level of safety [1].

On traditional use registration (Article 16a(1)), the plant must have been in use for at least thirty years, of which at least fifteen within the European Union. No clinical trials are required; sufficient safety data and plausible efficacy are enough [1].

This distinction is also the source of the mismatch this review is about. Valerian sits in the upper category while its individual sleep trials are mixed; lavender has the sounder trials but holds only a traditional-use registration on the sleep side. A monograph category and the strength of evidence for a single indication are not the same thing.

When to see a doctor

Insomnia is less often a condition in itself than a symptom of something else. Herbal use should not delay the investigation of that something else.

The following should not be deferred by a herbal product: insomnia lasting more than three months and present on most nights of the week, sleep deprivation severe enough to impair daytime functioning, pauses in breathing during sleep or loud snoring and choking reported by a bed partner, waking regularly with a morning headache, a sleep disturbance accompanied by low mood and loss of interest, and restless legs.

For people taking prescribed sleep medicines or sedatives, some of the plants in this review carry compounding interactions; these products should not be combined without the knowledge of a physician. The interaction information is given on each plant’s own record page.
