Plants used for menstrual complaints and menopause: evidence, restrictions, and a lethal tradition

Some of the plants used for menstrual complaints also appear in folk records as means of “bringing on a period” or of ending a pregnancy. Those plants are included in this review — not as an option, but because deaths have been reported. For someone seeking to end a pregnancy these plants are not a route; in Türkiye that is a procedure carried out by a physician within the legal period. This review covers recorded uses for period pain, premenstrual syndrome and menopausal symptoms, and the level of evidence behind them; it contains no dosage information.

Scope and method

The plants in this review were selected on two criteria: either the relevant indication is covered by a European Medicines Agency (EMA) herbal monograph, or the catalogue holds a recorded traditional use in this area [1]. In addition, plants that appear in folk records here but must not be used because of their toxicity are treated in a separate section.

Three distinct conditions appear together here and should not be conflated: period pain (dysmenorrhoea), premenstrual syndrome (PMS) and menopausal symptoms. These are different presentations and their recorded uses differ accordingly. Parts used, safety information and restrictions are given on each plant’s own record page; dosage and preparation have deliberately been left out.

Chaste tree: well-established use in premenstrual syndrome

chaste tree, chaste tree (Vitex agnus-castus), is the highest-evidence record in this review. Standardised fruit extract has reduced premenstrual syndrome symptoms relative to placebo in several controlled trials, and the EMA regards this use as well established [1].

The limit of that scope matters: the well-established use is for premenstrual syndrome, not for period pain itself. The two are not the same, and the plant’s record covers one and not the other.

Chaste tree has a hormonal effect, and this is the source of its restrictions: it may interact with oral contraceptives, hormone therapy and medicines acting on dopamine. It must not be used during pregnancy or breastfeeding. The TİTCK monograph advises that people who have had, or currently have, an oestrogen-sensitive cancer should not use it without consulting a physician.

Traditional records for period pain

The level of evidence for the plants recorded for period pain itself is markedly lower than for chaste tree. For yarrow, yarrow (Achillea millefolium), the EMA recognises loss of appetite, dyspepsia, period pain and minor wounds under traditional use; human studies are few [1]. Anatolian records note that it is infused for stomach and period pain.

Yarrow’s restrictions are not slight: it can cause allergy in people allergic to the daisy family, it must not be used in pregnancy, and the TİTCK monograph advises stopping it two weeks before surgery; it interacts with antiplatelet medicines, blood thinners and lithium.

abuta (Abuta grandifolia) is used in Amazonian records for period pain, post-partum bleeding and women’s health. Muscle-relaxant and anti-inflammatory effects have been seen in laboratory and animal studies; there are no human studies [2]. Because it may act on the uterus it must not be used in pregnancy, and it can lower blood pressure.

Menopause is a separate matter

Menopausal symptoms do not sit comfortably under the same heading as menstrual complaints; they are a different presentation and a different group of records.

black cohosh, black cohosh (Actaea racemosa), is this area’s well-established use record: the EMA recognises the root extract for menopausal symptoms such as hot flushes. NCCIH, for its part, notes that trial results are inconsistent [1][3]. In North America the Cherokee and other indigenous peoples used the root for women’s health, rheumatism and snakebite.

Rare cases of liver injury have been reported; people with liver disease should not use it. The TİTCK monograph advises against use in pregnancy and breastfeeding, and that people with breast cancer should use it only under medical supervision.

Plants recorded as “bringing on a period”, and the deaths reported

In folk medicine records some plants appear as means of bringing on a period or of ending a pregnancy. These records are real and belong in an ethnobotanical catalogue; but being recorded is not the same as being usable. The two highest safety risks in this review are in this group.

pennyroyal, pennyroyal (Mentha pulegium), carries a high proportion of pulegone in its volatile oil. Pulegone is toxic to the liver and kidneys, and deaths have been reported from ingestion of the oil [2]. It has been used throughout history in Europe and Latin America to bring on a period and to end a pregnancy. The decisive point here is this: the toxicity arrives before, or together with, any intended effect. It is not a plant that works but carries risk; it is a poisoning that can end in liver failure. Nor should it be drunk as a tea heavily or over long periods.

rue, rue (Ruta graveolens), is known in folk medicine as a means of bringing on a period, and in Andean markets it is the central plant of limpia cleansing rituals under the name ruda. It can cause miscarriage in pregnancy, and at high doses it is toxic to the liver and kidneys. Touching the fresh plant and then going into sunlight causes severe skin burns (phytophotodermatitis) — its furanocoumarins make skin hypersensitive to sunlight. There is no clinical evidence for the plant itself [2].

For someone seeking to end a pregnancy, these plants are not a route. In Türkiye the termination of pregnancy is governed by Article 5 of Law no. 2827 on Population Planning; it is a procedure that may be carried out within ten weeks of the beginning of the pregnancy, with the person’s consent and without their having to give a reason [4]. The place to go is not a plant but an obstetrician and gynaecologist.

The difference between monograph types

The phrases “well-established use” and “traditional use” above are not synonyms. A herbal product reaches the market in the European Union by different regulatory routes, two of which diverge at the level of the monograph [1].

On the well-established use route (Directive 2001/83/EC, Article 10a), scientific literature must establish that the active substances have been in well-established medicinal use within the European Union for at least ten years, with recognised efficacy and an acceptable level of safety [1].

On traditional use registration (Article 16a(1)), the plant must have been in use for at least thirty years, of which at least fifteen within the European Union. No clinical trials are required; sufficient safety data and plausible efficacy are enough [1].

Two records in this review sit in the upper category, and neither is for period pain: chaste tree for premenstrual syndrome, black cohosh for menopausal symptoms. The plants recorded for period pain itself remain at the level of traditional use.

When to see a doctor

Period pain is common, but not all pain is the same and some of it has a cause that needs investigating. Herbal use should not delay that investigation.

The following should not be deferred by a herbal product: pain severe enough to prevent daily activity and unresponsive to painkillers, pain that worsens markedly over time, pain during intercourse or persisting outside the menstrual period, bleeding that requires changing a pad or tampon every one to two hours, bleeding lasting more than seven days, bleeding between periods, any bleeding after menopause, absence of periods for more than three months (pregnancy aside), and pelvic pain with fever.

For people taking oral contraceptives, hormone therapy, blood thinners or antidepressants, some of the plants in this review carry interactions and restrictions. This holds particularly for chaste tree, which has a hormonal effect. The interaction information is given on each plant’s own record page.
