What did they eat at Göbekli Tepe? Anatolia’s plants before farming

Twelve thousand years ago, on a hill northeast of Şanlıurfa, people raised stone pillars weighing tonnes. Those who did it were not yet farmers. The plant remains from the excavation show this odd situation directly: every cereal found at Göbekli Tepe is wild. And yet they left more than seven thousand grinding tools at the same place. They processed grain on a large scale without sowing it.

What came out of the flotation tank

The only published archaeobotanical study of Göbekli Tepe is Reinder Neef’s preliminary report covering the 1997–2003 seasons. One thousand nine hundred and fifty litres of soil were washed, and this is what was left: wild einkorn grains and spikelet forks, wild barley, lentil, pistachio, almond, legumes, and a few seeds of the beard-grass and hyacinth types [1].

The names on that list need care, because none of them is the form sold in a shop today. The pistachio in the remains is not pistachio but its wild relative, terebinth. The lentil is half of a single seed: 2.1 millimetres across, that is, within the range of wild lentil [1]. The barley grains are flat and broadly furrowed — wild barley.

The charcoal list tells the same story: mostly terebinth and almond wood, a little oak, some hawthorn or pear type, and water-loving trees such as ash, poplar and willow [1]. So the hill stood in steppe with scattered trees, and along the nearby streams there were moisture-loving trees.

The most important sentence in the report is this: there is no evidence of domestication in the plant material found [1]. None of the cereals is domesticated. The break scars on the spikelet forks are smooth — the mark of a wild plant whose ear shatters by itself.

One more detail: during the excavation Neef also recorded the hill’s present-day vegetation. The slopes of the Göbekli Tepe plateau are still covered by a grass steppe dominated by wild barley [1]. The plant those people gathered eleven and a half thousand years ago still stands on the same slope.

Processing without sowing

Few plant remains, many tools. More than seven thousand grinding and pounding tools have been recorded at Göbekli Tepe [2]. For a long time nobody could explain why so many tools sat alongside so few seeds.

The answer came from phytoliths. A phytolith is a microscopic silica cast formed in plant tissue that survives after the plant decays. In the Göbekli Tepe samples, phytoliths from the upper parts of the cereal — the ear and the upper stem — were markedly more common than those from the lower stems [3]. That suggests the ears were cut a few centimetres down the stalk and carried away.

So these people were not sowing grain, but they were gathering, carrying, cleaning and grinding it. A pre-agricultural society’s relationship with cereals does not begin with the verb “to sow”.

Where was einkorn domesticated?

Northeast of Göbekli Tepe, in the same landscape, stands a volcanic mountain: Karacadağ. A genetic study published in Science in 1997 showed that the wild lines closest to domesticated einkorn wheat were samples collected from this region, and proposed that domestication may have happened here [4].

That finding was not overturned in later years, but it was refined. A broad survey in 2007 did not find the genetic bottleneck expected from domestication, and raised the possibility of several domestications from scattered populations rather than a single event in a single place [5]. A 2018 study looking at the genes that prevent the ear from shattering moved the closest wild lines a little further west, between Kahramanmaraş and Gaziantep [6].

An einkorn genome study published in Nature in 2023 pointed back to the area around Karacadağ and supported the view that domestication happened from a small, limited wild population [7]. The same study showed something else: the story of domesticated einkorn does not end at Karacadağ. On average about seven per cent of its genome came later from wild relatives in central and northwestern Anatolia [7].

In short: Göbekli Tepe was still standing while wheat was being domesticated, but no domesticated wheat has been found on the hill. They are two separate moments in the same landscape.

The beer argument, and how it ended

Large limestone vats holding up to a hundred and sixty litres were found at Göbekli Tepe. A paper published in 2012 argued, on the strength of oxalate residue on their walls, that a fermented cereal drink — beer — may have been prepared here; the paper’s own phrasing was “almost certain” [8]. The claim spread fast: “the world’s first brewery”.

Eight years later the same excavation team tested its own claim. A 2020 study combining chemical analysis, experimental archaeology and tool-use traces found traces of heated cereal in the vats but no firm evidence of fermentation. Oxalate, it noted, can also form through regular contact with water or from other plants, and so is not diagnostic [9].

The team’s 2021 monograph is blunter: the analyses clearly show that large quantities of crushed cereal were processed and prepared by heating — not fermented [2]. The primary interpretation today is not beer but porridge.

This is less the refutation of a claim than an example of how science works. The people who made the claim are the same people who tested it and withdrew it.

Are there plants on the stones?

The iconography of Göbekli Tepe is overwhelmingly animal: snake, fox, wild boar, crane, scorpion, vulture. Depictions of plants are almost absent.

The one carving that might be read as a plant is the geometric frieze on the head of Pillar 43. It has been suggested that the motifs, which look at first like opposed triangles, are in fact two rows of tassel shapes, that one row resembles a sheaf, and that they evoke grass anatomy — a short ear on a long thin stalk [2].

But this reading is not settled. The researcher who proposed it calls it explicitly “hypothetical” and adds this: the real interest of the Göbekli Tepe artists was not landscape or plants but animals [2].

What is not true

Most of the plant-related claims circulating about Göbekli Tepe have no source. Four are worth separating out.

“Farming was practised here.” No. There is no evidence of domestication in the plant remains from the excavation; every cereal is wild [1].

“The world’s first brewery.” Not supported. The excavation team’s own analyses found no firm evidence of fermentation [9].

“Psychoactive plants were used here.” There is neither such a claim nor any evidence in the peer-reviewed literature. No psychoactive plant is among the taxa identified at the site [1][2].

“Traces of a lost civilisation, the Garden of Eden, visitors from elsewhere.” This is pseudoscience; the archaeological literature names these claims as such [10]. The claim that figures on the pillars record a comet impact has also been answered in detail by the excavation team: the pillars were moved and reused, the date proposed is centuries earlier than the structure’s earliest radiocarbon date, and the reading lifts a single stone out of its context [11].

A note on Karahantepe as well: no archaeobotanical data have been published in a peer-reviewed form to date. The dietary details that appear in the press rest on studies not yet published. This page will be updated when they are.
