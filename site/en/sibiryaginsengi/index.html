BT-227 · Araliaceae (sarmaşıkgiller)

Siberian ginseng

Eleutherococcus senticosus (Rupr. et Maxim.) Maxim.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

Its name is the residue of a marketing decision. The plant is neither in the ginseng genus nor a traditional substitute for ginseng; after Soviet research presented it as an "adaptogen" in the mid-twentieth century, it began to be sold in Western markets as the cheap counterpart to expensive Panax ginseng, under the name "Siberian ginseng". The name caught so thoroughly that it now appears as the Turkish name on an official list. Yet the two plants have separate monographs, separate compositions and separate evidence — carrying one’s name is not inheriting the other’s data.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the names "Sibirya ginsengi, dikenli ginseng"; Acanthopanax senticosus (Rupr. & Maxim.) Harms and Hedera senticosa Rupr. & Maxim. are synonyms. In English: Siberian ginseng, acanthopanax, devil’s shrub, eleuthero, wild pepper. ⚠️ Despite the "ginseng" in its name it is not true ginseng (Panax ginseng): same family, different genus.

In short

What is Siberian ginseng?

Siberian ginseng (Eleutherococcus senticosus (Rupr. et Maxim.) Maxim.) is a plant of the Araliaceae (sarmaşıkgiller) family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia. Part used: the root.

What is Siberian ginseng used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": symptoms of asthenia such as fatigue and weakness. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no single-ingredient clinical trial of Siberian ginseng in the monograph indication — fatigue and weakness — using the root, which is the monograph drug.

Is Siberian ginseng safe?

Safety label: caution. Insomnia, headache, palpitations and tachycardia — the Turkish monograph writes these not as effects that may occur but as effects the plant causes, and the EU monograph lists the same four among adverse reactions of unknown frequency. Anyone with palpitations or an arrhythmia should not use this plant without asking their doctor. Not to be taken for more than two months — a firm time limit written into both monographs. People with diabetes should use it under medical supervision. The Turkish monograph also asks those who consume bitter or spicy substances to be careful. Not recommended under 12, in pregnancy or in lactation. Tinctures and extracts may contain ethanol.

Traditional use

Europe — one indication

The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": symptoms of asthenia such as fatigue and weakness. The time limit the monograph sets is notable, and rare in this catalog: not to be taken for more than two months.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph lists the root, gives the same two-month limit, and states the adverse effects outright: it causes insomnia, headache, palpitations and tachycardia. People with diabetes are to use it under medical supervision. The monograph also carries an unusual caution: people who consume bitter or spicy substances should use it carefully. Not recommended under 12.

Northeast Asia

The plant grows in southern Siberia, northern China, Korea and northern Japan. In the middle of the twentieth century Soviet research brought it forward as an "adaptogen", and in the West it was introduced as a plant that could stand in for expensive Panax ginseng — which is exactly where the name "Siberian ginseng" comes from. It does not grow in Turkey and arrives as a product.

In nature

Where it grows
The mixed forests of southern Siberia, northern China, Korea and northern Japan; a thorny shrub. It does not grow in Turkey.
Harvest season
The roots are lifted in autumn, after the plant has drawn back its aerial parts.
Local names
Sibirya ginsengi, dikenli ginseng — both appear in the Turkish monograph.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ It is confused with true ginseng (Panax ginseng); same family, different genus, different monograph. Other plants also carry "ginseng" in their names (Brazilian ginseng, Indian ginseng) and none of them is a Panax. Check the Latin name when buying a product.

Preparation

The EU monograph gives many forms as a daily dose: herbal tea, 0.5–4 g of the comminuted drug in 150 ml of boiling water (divided into one to three doses); powdered drug 0.75–3 g; liquid extract (DER 1:1) 2–3 ml; dry extracts at doses corresponding to 0.5–4 g of dried root; dry aqueous extract 90–180 mg; tincture 10–15 ml. If symptoms persist longer than two weeks, consult a doctor. Not to be taken for more than two months. Not recommended under 12.

What science says

There is no single-ingredient clinical trial of Siberian ginseng in the monograph indication — fatigue and weakness — using the root, which is the monograph drug. Two pieces of human work exist and both measure something else. A double-blind randomised trial published in 2024 in Frontiers in Cardiovascular Medicine gave a supplement twice daily for four weeks to 28 community-dwelling elderly people and examined cardiac autonomic function (heart-rate variability measures), reporting improvement in those measures — a physiological index in 28 people, not whether fatigue lifted. A placebo-controlled study published in 2020 in Nutrients measured cognitive function, but used the leaf of the plant together with another plant (rhizome of Drynaria fortunei): neither the monograph’s drug nor a single ingredient. The EU monograph rests the indication on long use. The evidence level is Traditional. A note: "adaptogen" is not a level of evidence but a classification proposed in the mid-twentieth century; calling a plant an adaptogen does not show that it relieves fatigue.

Safety

Caution

Insomnia, headache, palpitations and tachycardia — the Turkish monograph writes these not as effects that may occur but as effects the plant causes, and the EU monograph lists the same four among adverse reactions of unknown frequency. Anyone with palpitations or an arrhythmia should not use this plant without asking their doctor. Not to be taken for more than two months — a firm time limit written into both monographs. People with diabetes should use it under medical supervision. The Turkish monograph also asks those who consume bitter or spicy substances to be careful. Not recommended under 12, in pregnancy or in lactation. Tinctures and extracts may contain ethanol.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Cardiac medicines — digoxin and the likeThe Turkish monograph reports interaction with digoxin and similar cardiac medicines. Digoxin has a narrow safety margin; anything that might affect its blood level should be mentioned to the doctor.
Antihypertensives, antidiabetics, anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugsGroups on the Turkish monograph’s list. Blood pressure, blood glucose and clotting are all measurable values targeted by medication, which is why a reported interaction in these groups has practical consequences.
Antipsychotics, lithium and central nervous system depressantsOn the Turkish monograph’s list. Lithium has a narrow safety margin. The insomnia and restlessness the monograph reports also make use alongside this group problematic in its own right.
Medicines metabolised by CYP1A2 and CYP2C9, and hormone medicinesThe last items on the Turkish list. Those two enzymes are involved in processing a great many medicines, so the warning is broad in scope; asking a pharmacist who knows the medication list is the most practical route. The EU monograph, for its part, lists nothing in its interaction section.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Siberian ginseng.
