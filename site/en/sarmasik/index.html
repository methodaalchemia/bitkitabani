Common ivy

Hedera helix L.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

The medicinal part is the leaf, while the plant’s reputation for toxicity comes from its berries. What enters the monograph is the leaf only, and even then not the leaf itself but a standardised extract of it. It is also a good example of where "natural means gentle" breaks down: what rules it out for children under two is not the plant’s toxicity but the expectorant effect itself.

In short

What is Common ivy?

Common ivy (Hedera helix L.) is a plant of the Araliaceae (ivy family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the leaf.

What is Common ivy used for?

Traditional use: Ivy leaf extract is one of the best-selling over-the-counter herbal preparations for productive cough in Europe, sold as syrup, drops and effervescent tablets. Scientific evidence: limited. Here the regulatory decision and the independent literature diverge, and that divergence is the important part.

Is Common ivy safe?

Safety label: caution. Contraindicated in children under 2 years of age: the EMA monograph gives this as a contraindication, on the grounds that secretolytic medicines can aggravate respiratory symptoms. Persistent or recurrent cough in children aged 2–4 requires a medical diagnosis before treatment. Not for anyone hypersensitive to the active substance or to plants of the Araliaceae family. Fresh leaves can cause allergic contact dermatitis. Reported undesirable effects are gastrointestinal (nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea) and allergic (urticaria, skin rash, dyspnoea, anaphylactic reaction); the frequency of both is unknown. Caution in gastritis or gastric ulcer. Seek medical advice if dyspnoea, fever or purulent sputum occurs. Safety in pregnancy and lactation has not been established, so use is not recommended; no fertility data are available.

Traditional use

Europe — expectorant

Ivy leaf extract is one of the best-selling over-the-counter herbal preparations for productive cough in Europe, sold as syrup, drops and effervescent tablets. The European Medicines Agency adopted a monograph in 2009 and placed the indication under "well-established use", meaning the agency accepts that the use rests on clinical evidence rather than on a history of use alone. The monograph indication is one sentence: an expectorant in case of productive cough.

Turkey — official list

The leaf is listed in the Turkish Medicines and Medical Devices Agency’s medicinal plant inventory, with its own safety warnings. Cough syrups containing ivy leaf extract are available in Turkish pharmacies.

Anatolia

Ivy grows in moist woodland and gardens across Turkey, clinging to walls and tree trunks, evergreen all year. Its medicinal use comes from European phytotherapy rather than from Anatolian folk medicine.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to Europe, western Asia and North Africa. Found in moist woodland, on garden walls and tree trunks throughout Turkey. Extremely shade-tolerant; climbs 20–30 m using rootlike holdfasts.
Harvest season
Evergreen. Flowers greenish-yellow from September to November, one of the last nectar sources of the European autumn. Berries ripen in late winter and spring.
Local names
Usually sarmaşık in Turkish; the regulatory monograph gives "sarmaşık, duvar sarmaşığı". Note that sarmaşık is used in Turkish for many climbing plants; the medicinal species is Hedera helix.

Mix-up risk

Because the Turkish name is applied to many climbers, the risk of confusion is high — in particular with Virginia creeper (Parthenocissus) and trumpet vine (Campsis). In pharmacy products the part used is a standardised extract, so the Latin name on the box is what settles it.

Preparation

The EMA monograph describes standardised extracts rather than the leaf itself, so there is no home preparation. It lists five separate extract types for adolescents, adults and the elderly, each with its own dose; for dry extracts a single dose of 15–65 mg and a daily dose of 45–105 mg, with a maximum daily dose of 67 mg for ethanol-containing finished products. These doses belong to the extract, not the plant, and one extract type is not interchangeable with another. Leaf tea is not in the monograph.

What science says

Here the regulatory decision and the independent literature diverge, and that divergence is the important part. The EMA places the indication under "well-established use". An updated systematic review in 2021 (Eur J Clin Pharmacol) assessed six randomised trials, one controlled trial and four observational studies and concluded: all studies found ivy leaf extract effective and safe, three randomised trials reported a more rapid reduction in cough severity or frequency, but the clinical significance of these effects appears to be minimal, the overall quality of reporting was low and the risk of bias was high. No serious adverse effects were reported. What is disputed is not whether there is an effect but how large it is and how good the evidence behind it is. The evidence level on this site is therefore labelled Limited rather than Clinical: regulatory approval on its own does not amount to strong independent evidence.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Antitussives (codeine, dextromethorphan)The Turkish regulatory monograph notes that ivy may interact with antitussive medicines such as codeine or dextromethorphan. The reasoning: ivy is an expectorant, thinning mucus so that it can be coughed up, while an antitussive suppresses the very reflex that clears it. Taken together, thinned secretions may accumulate in the airways. Combined use calls for a doctor’s or pharmacist’s assessment.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe interaction section of the EMA monograph says "none reported". That is a statement about what has been studied, not evidence that no interaction exists; the Turkish warning rests on pharmacological reasoning.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Common ivy.
