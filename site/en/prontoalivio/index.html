What is Pronto alivio?

Pronto alivio (Lippia alba (Mill.) N.E.Br. ex Britton & P.Wilson) is a plant of the Verbenaceae (verbena family) family. Traditions that use it: Amazon, North America. Part used: leaves and flowering stems.

What is Pronto alivio used for?

Traditional use: A review in the Journal of Ethnopharmacology records the species being used mainly for digestive and respiratory ailments, and as a sedative and antihypertensive remedy. Scientific evidence: limited. The review in the Journal of Ethnopharmacology states that partial, positive results have been obtained for sedative and anxiolytic effects, but that well conducted biological studies are still needed for several of the species’ indications.

Is Pronto alivio safe?

Safety label: caution. The two clinical studies relied on here reported no adverse effects, but they were small and uncontrolled, which does not mean safety was demonstrated. The plant’s traditional use is sedative and blood-pressure-lowering, so care is needed for people taking sleep or anxiety medicines and blood-pressure medicines. Because of chemotype variability the composition of a plant in hand is unknown. The sources relied on here contain no separate assessment for pregnancy, lactation or children, and as there is no monograph there is no official list of contraindications.

Traditional use

Tropical America — digestion, breathing and as a sedative

A review in the Journal of Ethnopharmacology records the species being used mainly for digestive and respiratory ailments, and as a sedative and antihypertensive remedy. The review notes that some of these uses may be explained by the plant’s anti-infective and analgesic properties.

The chemotype problem — seven different plants under one name

The same review states that seven separate chemotypes exist in the species’ essential oil. This means two plants carrying the same Latin name can differ markedly in scent and in active compounds; the review stresses that this variability matters for the validity of research results.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew’s records its range covers 56 regions: open, humid places in tropical and subtropical America. It has also naturalised on other continents.
Harvest season
It does not grow in Turkey; the sources relied on here give no collection calendar for Turkey.
Local names
No Turkish regional name. Local names: pronto alivio, erva cidreira, falsa melissa.

Mix-up risk

It can be confused with lemon balm (Melissa officinalis) and lemongrass (Cymbopogon); all three smell of lemon and all three are separate plants. Since chemotypes of this one species also smell different from each other, scent alone is not an identifying criterion.

Preparation

No official monograph sets an accepted dose. What the clinical studies relied on here used was a hydroalcoholic extract of the leaves, and they were carried out with one particular chemotype (geranial-carvenone); that is not the same thing as a tea brewed at home. Because the review states that essential oil composition varies by chemotype, a plant in hand cannot be assumed to match the one studied.

What science says

The review in the Journal of Ethnopharmacology states that partial, positive results have been obtained for sedative and anxiolytic effects, but that well conducted biological studies are still needed for several of the species’ indications. Two studies in Phytomedicine tested the hydroalcoholic extract in migraine: the first is a phase 2, non-controlled cohort study reporting that more than 80% of patients had at least a halving of pain intensity and frequency; the second, with 21 women, is again non-controlled and reports falls in MIDAS and HIT-6 scores with more than 70% of patients having at least a halving of pain frequency. Neither had a placebo arm, so these results were not compared against placebo, and in a fluctuating complaint such as migraine uncontrolled studies can read far too optimistically. Both studies used a single chemotype.

Safety

Caution

The two clinical studies relied on here reported no adverse effects, but they were small and uncontrolled, which does not mean safety was demonstrated. Because of chemotype variability the composition of a plant in hand is unknown. The sources relied on here contain no separate assessment for pregnancy, lactation or children, and as there is no monograph there is no official list of contraindications.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Sedatives, sleep and anxiety medicinesGiven its traditional use and the partial sedative findings in the review, it may increase drowsiness.
Blood-pressure medicinesAs it is used in folk medicine to lower blood pressure, monitoring blood pressure is sensible when the two are combined.
Migraine medicinesThe clinical studies tested the plant alone in migraine; it does not replace prescribed migraine treatment and no study assesses the combination.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Pronto alivio.
