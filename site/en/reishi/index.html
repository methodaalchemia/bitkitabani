In short

What is Reishi mushroom?

Reishi mushroom (Ganoderma lucidum) is a plant of the Ganodermataceae family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia. Part used: fruiting body and spores.

What is Reishi mushroom used for?

Traditional use: For two thousand years, as lingzhi, it has been the “mushroom of immortality”, a symbol of long life in imperial art and temples. Scientific evidence: limited. A Cochrane review (2016) found no evidence to support reishi as a cancer treatment on its own; low-quality data suggest it may slightly improve tumour response and immune markers when combined with chemotherapy or radiotherapy.

Is Reishi mushroom safe?

Safety label: caution. It can cause dry mouth, stomach upset and rash. Serious liver injury has been reported with powdered products. Not for people on blood thinners, with bleeding disorders or before surgery. People being treated for cancer should not use it without asking their doctor.

Traditional use

China

For two thousand years, as lingzhi, it has been the “mushroom of immortality”, a symbol of long life in imperial art and temples. Taoist texts call it the food of the immortals.

Japan and Korea

Known as reishi in Japan and yeongji in Korea; the tea and powder are taken for fatigue and immunity.

In nature

Where it grows
Temperate forests of Asia, at the base of broadleaf trees such as oak; related Ganoderma species grow in Türkiye.
Harvest season
In the wild it appears in summer and autumn on dead or weakened broadleaf trunks.
Local names
reishi, lingzhi

Mix-up risk

European Ganoderma lucidum and Asian lingzhi (G. lingzhi) are genetically different species; product labels are not always accurate.

Preparation

Hard and woody, so it is sliced and simmered for an hour or more; today it is mostly taken as powder or extract capsules.

What science says

A Cochrane review (2016) found no evidence to support reishi as a cancer treatment on its own; low-quality data suggest it may slightly improve tumour response and immune markers when combined with chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Small studies on cholesterol, blood sugar and blood pressure have shown no clear effect.

Safety

Caution

It can cause dry mouth, stomach upset and rash. Serious liver injury has been reported with powdered products. Not for people on blood thinners, with bleeding disorders or before surgery. People being treated for cancer should not use it without asking their doctor.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Blood thinners and antiplateletsMay slow clotting; bleeding risk may rise.
Blood pressure and diabetes medicinesMay lower blood pressure and blood sugar; effects can add up.
ImmunosuppressantsIts immune-stimulating effect may weaken the drug.
