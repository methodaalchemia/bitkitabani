Dragon’s blood (Socotra dragon tree)

Dracaena cinnabari Balf.f.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

Pliny wrote in AD 77 that dragon’s blood was what ran from a dragon crushed beneath a dying elephant, the blood of both animals mixed. He also complained that because the poisonous mineral minium shared its name, physicians were giving patients poison instead of the resin.

What is Dragon’s blood (Socotra dragon tree)?

Dragon’s blood (Socotra dragon tree) (Dracaena cinnabari Balf.f.) is a plant of the Asparagaceae (asparagus family) family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia, Anatolia and the Mediterranean. Part used: dark red resin that bleeds from the trunk.

What is Dragon’s blood (Socotra dragon tree) used for?

Traditional use: The tree grows wild only on Socotra, an island belonging to Yemen. Scientific evidence: traditional. The resin contains homoisoflavonoids, flavonoids, biflavonoids and chalcones; its species markers are loureirin A and B and 7,4′-dihydroxyflavylium.

Is Dragon’s blood (Socotra dragon tree) safe?

Safety label: caution. Not to be used in pregnancy: folk use as an abortifacient in the first trimester is on record. No data for breastfeeding. In rats no toxic findings appeared up to a single 2000 mg/kg dose or 1500 mg/kg daily for 28 days. The real risk is adulteration: resin sold in Yemen’s own markets has been found partly or wholly substituted, and the pharmacopoeial source of “dragon’s blood” is not this tree but the palm Daemonorops draco.

Traditional use

Socotra

The tree grows wild only on Socotra, an island belonging to Yemen. The resin is tapped from the trunk and dried; islanders use it for skin, dental, eye and digestive complaints. Centuries of tapping have left the trees covered in scars.

Arab and Islamic medicine

Classical Arabic sources call the resin dam al-akhawayn, “blood of the two brothers”. Al-Asma‘i names it around 800, Ibn al-Baytar in 1248 as “dragon’s blood” and “serpent’s blood”. It appears in Avicenna’s Canon and al-Razi’s al-Hawi, used for wounds, fractures, ulcers, diarrhoea and to stop bleeding.

Ancient Mediterranean

The earliest mention is the Periplus of about AD 60, which calls it “Indian cinnabar”. Dioscorides records it as kinnabari, a substance “brought from Africa”.

Pigment and lacquer

Dragon’s blood has been identified by gas chromatography in gold lacquers, transparent glazes and reverse glass painting from the 15th to the 19th century — though it is named far more often in historical sources than it is actually found in surviving works.

In nature

Where it grows
Wild only on Socotra. Listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List — an assessment made in 2004 and not revised since. Overgrazing, climate change and unsustainable tapping all press on it.
Harvest season
Not seasonal; the resin is collected once the liquid that seeps from a cut in the trunk has dried.
Local names
Dragon tree, blood of the two brothers

Mix-up risk

“Dragon’s blood” names red resins from at least four different plant families: Dracaena cinnabari (Socotra), Daemonorops draco (a Southeast Asian palm), Croton lechleri and C. gossypifolium (South America) and Pterocarpus draco. They can only be told apart by chromatography or Raman spectroscopy; even museum collections carry mislabelled samples.

Preparation

There is no official preparation: neither a European Medicines Agency monograph nor an NCCIH page exists. In traditional use the dried resin is powdered onto a wound or mixed into an ointment.

What science says

The resin contains homoisoflavonoids, flavonoids, biflavonoids and chalcones; its species markers are loureirin A and B and 7,4′-dihydroxyflavylium. Antifungal, antioxidant and cell-level antitumour effects have been reported. No controlled human study exists; all evidence is from cells and animals. The one randomised trial of a “dragon’s blood cream” used a different plant, the Amazonian Croton lechleri.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Blood thinners and antiplatelet medicinesNo interaction has been studied. Some compounds in the resin inhibit platelet aggregation in cell studies, so a theoretical concern exists; anyone on these medicines should ask their doctor first.
