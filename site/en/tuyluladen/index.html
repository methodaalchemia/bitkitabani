BT-203 · Cistaceae (rock-rose family)

Cretan rock rose

Cistus creticus L.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

Rock rose’s cultural weight comes not from its medicine but from its resin. In summer heat its leaves exude a sticky, heavy-scented resin called ladanum, used around the Mediterranean for thousands of years in incense and perfume. The way it is gathered is still odd enough to be worth describing: in Crete the resin is collected by drawing a rake-like tool hung with leather straps (the ladanisterion) across the bushes. The indication the EU monograph gives is none of this — only cough with a cold.

Other names

In short

What is Cretan rock rose?

Cretan rock rose (Cistus creticus L.) is a plant of the Cistaceae (rock-rose family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the aerial parts, the essential oil and the resin.

What is Cretan rock rose used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": relief of cough associated with a cold. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no randomised trial of Cretan rock rose under its own name; there is one in a close relative, and the two need separating.

Is Cretan rock rose safe?

Safety label: caution. Most of the warnings are specific to the essential oil and need separating from the herbal tea. The essential oil: not to be used by people with epilepsy; to be used with care by people with high blood pressure; oxidised oil must not be used — essential oils oxidise over time with air and light, and oxidised oil irritates the skin more, so old or poorly stored bottles should be discarded; not to be applied neat or above the recommended dose to the skin; contact with mucous membranes and eyes to be avoided; not taken orally undiluted. Hypersensitivity to the active substance can occur. When used for cough, see a doctor if dyspnoea, fever or purulent sputum occur — these can mean the cough belongs to something more serious. Use under 18 has not been established; not recommended in pregnancy or lactation.

Traditional use

Europe — cough with a cold

The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": relief of cough associated with a cold. The forms are herbal tea, infusion, decoction or macerate.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used broadly: "the aerial parts, fresh leafy branch tips, the essential oil and the resin". Most of its warnings are specific to the essential oil: not to be used by people with epilepsy, to be used with care by people with high blood pressure, oxidised oil must not be used, not to be applied neat or above the recommended dose to the skin, contact with mucous membranes and eyes to be avoided, and not to be taken orally in undiluted form.

Anatolia — resin and incense

Rock rose is a familiar shrub of the Aegean and Mediterranean maquis in Turkey; in summer heat its leaves exude a sticky, fragrant resin (ladanum). That resin has been gathered around the Mediterranean for thousands of years as incense and a perfume material. The monograph’s indication is cough, but the plant’s cultural weight comes from its resin.

In nature

Where it grows
Maquis and stony slopes of the eastern Mediterranean. Common along the Aegean and Mediterranean coasts of Turkey.
Harvest season
Pink flowers with crumpled petals from April to June, each lasting a day. The resin is gathered in summer heat.
Local names
Tüylü laden, laden, and names such as "pamuk otu" and "karakız"; the Turkish monograph gives "tüylü laden".

Mix-up risk

⚠️ The Turkish monograph sets a species warning: Cistus ladanifer is a different species and must not be confused with it. C. ladanifer is a plant of the western Mediterranean (Spain, Portugal, Morocco), with white flowers usually carrying dark blotches at the petal bases; Cretan rock rose has pink flowers. Several Cistus species also grow together in Turkey and all are called laden.

Preparation

The EU monograph describes tea, infusion, decoction and macerate of the aerial parts. The essential oil is a different thing, and the Turkish monograph’s warnings are specific to it: not taken orally undiluted, not applied neat to the skin, oxidised oil not used. Use under 18 has not been established.

What science says

There is no randomised trial of Cretan rock rose under its own name; there is one in a close relative, and the two need separating. A prospective, randomised, placebo-controlled study published in 2010 in Antiviral Research compared a Cistus incanus extract (CYSTUS052) with placebo in 160 patients with upper respiratory tract infection. The result: the subjective symptom score fell significantly over the course of treatment with Cistus, while the fall with placebo was less distinct; of the inflammatory markers examined, C-reactive protein was the most affected and fell significantly in the treatment group. The caveat here is taxonomic: the trial used a C. incanus extract, while the monographs are about C. creticus. The two names are entangled in botany — C. creticus is often treated as a subspecies of C. incanus — but "a close relative, perhaps even the same species" is not the same as "the monograph’s drug", and the result of a standardised extract (CYSTUS052) cannot be carried over to any rock rose tea. The EU monograph rests its indication on long use. The evidence level is Traditional.

Safety

Caution

Most of the warnings are specific to the essential oil and need separating from the herbal tea. The essential oil: not to be used by people with epilepsy; to be used with care by people with high blood pressure; oxidised oil must not be used — essential oils oxidise over time with air and light, and oxidised oil irritates the skin more, so old or poorly stored bottles should be discarded; not to be applied neat or above the recommended dose to the skin; contact with mucous membranes and eyes to be avoided; not taken orally undiluted. Hypersensitivity to the active substance can occur. When used for cough, see a doctor if dyspnoea, fever or purulent sputum occur — these can mean the cough belongs to something more serious. Use under 18 has not been established; not recommended in pregnancy or lactation.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No drug interactions reported in either monographBoth the EU and the Turkish monograph leave the interaction section empty. "None reported" is not evidence of no interaction; it means not studied.
Blood pressure medicines (essential oil)Not a reported interaction, but the Turkish monograph asks people with high blood pressure to use the essential oil with care. Anyone on blood pressure medication would do well to ask their doctor before using it.
Epilepsy medicines (essential oil)Again not a reported interaction but a contraindication: the Turkish monograph states the essential oil is not to be used by people with epilepsy. This is a general caution about the constituents of certain essential oils.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Cretan rock rose.
