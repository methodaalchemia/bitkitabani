The Turkish-name cell for this plant on the Turkish list is empty: the table reads "-". Because it is absent from the flora of Turkey, never entered folk medicine there, and was added to the official list directly by way of the European monograph, no Turkish name for it ever formed. The title "Java zerdeçalı" in this catalog is therefore a translation, not a vernacular name — and saying so is more accurate than inventing one. An empty cell on an official list is data too: it says the plant has no history with that country.

Other names
⚠️ The Turkish monograph gives no Turkish name for this plant — the table reads "-". The English names it gives are curcuma, curcuma javanis and Java turmeric; the title in this catalog is the Turkish rendering of that last name, not a vernacular Turkish name. In short

What is Java turmeric?

Java turmeric (Curcuma xanthorrhiza Roxb.) is a plant of the Zingiberaceae (ginger family) family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia. Part used: the rhizome.

What is Java turmeric used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": symptomatic treatment of digestive disturbances such as feelings of fullness, slow digestion and flatulence. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no clinical trial of Java turmeric in humans.

Is Java turmeric safe?

Safety label: caution. Not for people with gastric ulcer or excess stomach acid (Turkish monograph). Not for use in bile-duct obstruction, cholangitis, gallstones, liver disease or other biliary disease — the EU monograph states the reason plainly: the plant may stimulate bile secretion. Increased bowel movement and stomach discomfort have been reported rarely; the EU monograph lists dry mouth, flatulence and gastric irritation among effects of unknown frequency. After topical application, excessive sun exposure should be avoided; it may cause slight hair loss — this warning appears only in the Turkish monograph, and the EU monograph has no topical use at all. Not recommended under 18 or in pregnancy and lactation.

Traditional use

Europe — one indication

The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": symptomatic treatment of digestive disturbances such as feelings of fullness, slow digestion and flatulence. The monograph’s reasoning runs through bile secretion, and its contraindications follow from that.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph lists the rhizome and sets two limits: people with gastric ulcer or excess stomach acid must not use it, and people with bile-duct obstruction, gallstones or liver disease must not use it. It carries one further warning absent from the EU monograph: after topical application, excessive sun exposure should be avoided; it may cause slight hair loss. Use under 18 is not recommended.

Indonesia

The plant is native to Java and, as temulawak, one of the common ingredients of the Indonesian jamu tradition. Its rhizome is larger and more orange than turmeric’s; locally it is used as a drink and as a powder. It does not grow in Turkey and arrives as a product.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to Java; cultivated in Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand. It does not grow in Turkey.
Harvest season
It grows year-round in a tropical climate; the rhizomes are lifted about nine months after planting.
Local names
It has no Turkish name — the Turkish monograph prints "-". In Indonesia, temulawak.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ It is confused with turmeric (Curcuma longa), and that confusion is one of the most consequential in this catalog: the clinical trials done on turmeric are not evidence for this plant. The English names do not help either; the Turkish monograph gives "curcuma" as one of this plant’s own names. Checking the Latin name is the only safe route.

Preparation

For adults the EU monograph gives three forms: as a herbal tea, a single dose of 1 g of the comminuted drug infused in 100 ml of boiling water, three times daily; dry extract (DER 20–50:1, ethanol), single dose 8–13 mg, three times daily; dry extract (DER 9–12:1, acetone), single dose 50–100 mg, twice daily. If symptoms persist longer than two weeks, consult a doctor. Use under 18 is not recommended.

What science says

There is no clinical trial of Java turmeric in humans. Recent publications are at laboratory level: activity against UV damage in cells, an inhibitory effect on virus-like particles, analyses of its constituents. None of these shows whether it relieves a feeling of fullness or flatulence in a patient. The EU monograph rests the indication on long use. The trap for a reader here is obvious: there are hundreds of clinical trials on turmeric (Curcuma longa) and on curcumin, and this plant is not one of them. The two species share a genus, their rhizomes look alike, and even their English names run into one another — but a study on curcumin is a study on Curcuma longa and cannot be counted as evidence for Java turmeric. The evidence level is Traditional.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
The interaction list the Turkish monograph reportsThe monograph states it interacts with anticoagulants, antidepressants, cardiovascular medicines, antibiotics, chemotherapeutic agents and antihistamines. The list is broad and general, and the monograph’s sources include a consumer medicines database. Anyone on an anticoagulant or undergoing chemotherapy should not use this plant without asking their doctor.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing in its interaction section. That one agency reports six drug groups for the same plant while the other reports none shows the lists are compiled from different sources.
Confusion with turmeric studiesNot a drug interaction, but just as important: the studies on curcumin’s interaction with anticoagulants are studies on Curcuma longa. The two species do not share the same composition, and data on one cannot be carried across to the other.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Java turmeric.
