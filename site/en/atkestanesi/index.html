In short

What is Horse chestnut?

Horse chestnut (Aesculus hippocastanum) is a plant of the Sapindaceae family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean. Part used: seed (standardised extract), bark.

What is Horse chestnut used for?

Traditional use: Its name is said to come from the Ottoman practice of feeding the seeds to horses with coughs and breathing trouble. Scientific evidence: clinical. A Cochrane review (2012) found that standardised horse chestnut seed extract reduced leg pain, swelling and itching in chronic venous insufficiency compared with placebo in short-term use; in one trial its effect was close to compression stockings.

Is Horse chestnut safe?

Safety label: caution. Raw seeds, bark, leaves and flowers are poisonous; mistaking the seeds for sweet chestnuts causes vomiting, diarrhoea and dizziness. Only purified standardised extract is used. It can cause nausea and itching. People on blood thinners, with kidney or liver disease, and pregnant women should ask a doctor first.

Traditional use

Ottoman Istanbul

Its name is said to come from the Ottoman practice of feeding the seeds to horses with coughs and breathing trouble. It was planted in Istanbul’s parks, palace gardens and waterside mansions.

Europe

In the 20th century the seed extract became a herbal medicine for chronic venous insufficiency in the legs (swelling, heaviness, cramps); it is widely used in Germany and central Europe.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to Balkan mountain forests; a park and street tree in Istanbul and many Turkish cities.
Harvest season
Flowers April–May; seeds drop September–October.
Local names
at kestanesi

Mix-up risk

The shiny brown seed looks like a sweet chestnut (Castanea sativa). Sweet chestnut husks are densely covered in fine spines and the nut has a pointed tip; horse chestnut husks have a few thick spines and a round seed with no tip.

Preparation

Not prepared at home. The form used is a standardised seed extract with the toxic esculin removed and a set amount of aescin.

What science says

A Cochrane review (2012) found that standardised horse chestnut seed extract reduced leg pain, swelling and itching in chronic venous insufficiency compared with placebo in short-term use; in one trial its effect was close to compression stockings. The EMA recognises the standardised extract for this use. Larger studies are needed on long-term effect and safety.

Safety

Caution

Raw seeds, bark, leaves and flowers are poisonous; mistaking the seeds for sweet chestnuts causes vomiting, diarrhoea and dizziness. Only purified standardised extract is used. It can cause nausea and itching. People on blood thinners, with kidney or liver disease, and pregnant women should ask a doctor first.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Blood thinners (warfarin, aspirin, clopidogrel)Thought to increase bleeding risk; use together only under medical supervision.
