Wild pansy

Viola tricolor L.; V. arvensis Murray; V. vulgaris Koch
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

The plant given to infants for eczema in central Europe for centuries is not recommended orally under 18 in the EU monograph. That is not a contradiction but how regulatory language works: when the monograph says "insufficient data", an age limit is set however old the tradition. And the one serious case the monograph reports is also a child: haemolysis in a child with G6PD deficiency. Wild pansy is a record where the tradition of paediatric phytotherapy and paediatric safety data come face to face.

In short

What is Wild pansy?

Wild pansy (Viola tricolor L.; V. arvensis Murray; V. vulgaris Koch) is a plant of the Violaceae (violet family) family. Traditions that use it: Europe, Anatolia and the Mediterranean. Part used: the flowering aerial parts.

What is Wild pansy used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": symptomatic treatment of mild seborrhoeic skin conditions — orally as tea, cutaneously as a wet dressing or bath additive. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no clinical trial of wild pansy in humans; no randomised study appears on PubMed, and the publications a "clinical trial" search returns are reviews on other subjects.

Is Wild pansy safe?

Safety label: caution. Not for anyone hypersensitive to salicylates (aspirin and the like) — the plant carries salicylic acid derivatives; someone with aspirin allergy should not drink this tea. Not on open wounds or large areas of damaged skin. Hot baths must not be used in febrile or infectious illness, heart insufficiency or hypertension. The monograph reports one serious case: haemolysis in a child with G6PD deficiency after oral use — that enzyme deficiency is not rare in the Mediterranean basin, Turkey included, and with a known G6PD deficiency the plant should not be taken orally. Not orally under 18, not cutaneously under 12; not recommended in pregnancy or lactation.

Traditional use

Europe — one indication

The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": symptomatic treatment of mild seborrhoeic skin conditions — orally as tea, cutaneously as a wet dressing or bath additive. The contraindication is notable: hypersensitivity to salicylates — the plant carries salicylic acid derivatives.

Turkey

There is no monograph for wild pansy on the Turkish list. Its wild species grow on field margins in Turkey; its tea is known in Anatolian folk medicine for childhood eczema, but this catalog does not extend that folk use without a source.

Europe — the children’s skin plant

Wild pansy has been used for centuries in central Europe for the infant eczema called "milk crust"; it is a classic drug of German paediatric phytotherapy. Yet the monograph does not recommend oral use under 18 or cutaneous use under 12 — a point where tradition and regulatory language part ways.

In nature

Where it grows
Europe and western Asia; field margins, meadows, sandy soils. Wild species occur in Turkey.
Harvest season
Three-coloured (purple-yellow-white) flowers from spring to autumn; the flowering aerial parts are gathered through summer.
Local names
Hercai menekşe, yabani hercai menekşe; no unsourced regional names given.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ The large-flowered pansy sold in garden centres (Viola × wittrockiana) is an ornamental hybrid, not the monograph’s drug. Sweet violet (Viola odorata) is a separate species with a separate tradition of use.

Preparation

Orally: 3 g of comminuted drug as an infusion 1–3 times daily. Cutaneously: 5–20 g/l as an infusion, applied as a wet dressing 2–3 times daily. Bath additive: 5–10 g/l as an infusion, 1 litre added to the bath. See a doctor if symptoms last longer than four weeks. Not orally under 18, not cutaneously under 12.

What science says

There is no clinical trial of wild pansy in humans; no randomised study appears on PubMed, and the publications a "clinical trial" search returns are reviews on other subjects. The EU monograph rests the indication on long use. The known chemistry partly explains the seborrhoeic use: salicylic acid derivatives (keratolytic), flavonoids and mucilage. The same chemistry is the reason for the monograph’s salicylate contraindication. The evidence level is Traditional.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Salicylates and anticoagulants (theoretical)The monograph reports no interaction; but the plant’s salicylate content means a theoretical added load for anyone on aspirin or a blood thinner. The amount in a tea is small; even so, with regular use it is right to tell the doctor.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing; there is no Turkish monograph.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Wild pansy.
