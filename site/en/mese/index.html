Oak

Quercus robur, Q. petraea ve diğer türler
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

Other names
Pedunculate or English oak (Q. robur), sessile oak (Q. petraea), downy oak (Q. pubescens)
Part used
Bark of young branches
Region
Anatolia and the Mediterranean
Europe
Use
Healing, Ritual and shamanic
In short

What is Oak?

Oak (Quercus robur) is a plant of the Fagaceae (kayıngiller) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: bark of young branches.

What is Oak used for?

Traditional use: According to the Roman writer Pliny, the Druids of Gaul held nothing more sacred than mistletoe and the oak it grows on, and performed no rite without oak branches. Scientific evidence: traditional. The bark contains a highly variable 8–20% of tannins, which tighten tissue.

Is Oak safe?

Safety label: caution. The EMA does not recommend it under 18, in pregnancy or while breastfeeding. Take other medicines at least 1 hour apart, as it may delay their absorption. Not for baths on open wounds, large skin injuries or infected skin; avoid hot baths with fever, heart failure or high blood pressure. Allergic reactions have been reported.

Traditional use

Celts

According to the Roman writer Pliny, the Druids of Gaul held nothing more sacred than mistletoe and the oak it grows on, and performed no rite without oak branches. Mistletoe was cut on the sixth day of the moon by a priest in white with a golden sickle and caught in a white cloak.

Galatia (central Anatolia)

Strabo writes that the council of 300 of the Celtic Galatians, who settled in central Anatolia, met at a place called Drynemetum. Frazer reads the name as “sacred oak grove”.

Northern and eastern Europe

In Frazer’s compilation the oak was the tree of the thunder gods: Donar (Thor) among the Germans, Perun among the Slavs and Perkūnas among the Lithuanians. At Novgorod an oak-wood fire burned day and night for Perun.

Anatolia

In Doğanşehir, Malatya, large oaks are called “ziyaret ağacı” (pilgrimage tree) or “ulu ağaç” (great tree): people tie rags to them when making wishes, eat beneath them and do them no harm. In Sorgun, Yozgat, a centuries-old grove of Vallonea oak has survived thanks to the beliefs around it.

European folk medicine

The bark is used as an astringent for diarrhoea, inflammation of the mouth and skin, and haemorrhoids; the European Medicines Agency (EMA) recognises these as traditional uses.

In nature

Where it grows
Pedunculate oak grows in Thrace, the Black Sea region and eastern Anatolia; Turkey is the southern limit of its range (Şahin and Şahin 2023).
Harvest season
Bark is taken from young branches in spring; oaks flower in spring and their acorns ripen in autumn.
Local names
Saplı meşe, sapsız meşe; large sacred oaks are called ziyaret ağacı or ulu ağaç

Mix-up risk

Many oak species grow in Turkey; the EMA monograph covers only the bark of pedunculate, sessile and downy oak.

Preparation

According to the EMA monograph, a decoction of the bark is drunk for diarrhoea or used externally as a gargle, compress or sitz bath. Not for more than 3 days for diarrhoea, or more than 1 week for other uses.

What science says

The bark contains a highly variable 8–20% of tannins, which tighten tissue. According to the EMA assessment report there are no controlled clinical studies, so its use is regarded as traditional.

Safety

Caution

The EMA does not recommend it under 18, in pregnancy or while breastfeeding. Take other medicines at least 1 hour apart, as it may delay their absorption. Not for baths on open wounds, large skin injuries or infected skin; avoid hot baths with fever, heart failure or high blood pressure. Allergic reactions have been reported.
