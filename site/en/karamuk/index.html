Barberry

Berberis vulgaris L.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

One plant with two lives: the WHO wrote up its bark, science studied its fruit. While the 2009 monograph said "no clinical trial of the bark; what we know is berberine", the following decade saw randomised trials of the sour fruit on blood lipids accumulate in Iran. This catalog’s rule is to keep parts apart: the fruit’s meta-analysis is not the bark’s evidence, and the bark’s berberine is not the fruit’s safety. The red grain on the rice in Anatolia and the pharmacist’s yellow bark come from the same shrub but are not the same thing.

Other names
Turkish karamuk, kadıntuzluğu, amberbaris; in English barberry, European barberry; Persian zereshk. The drug of the WHO monograph is the stem bark (Cortex Berberidis); the part eaten and studied in Turkey and Iran is the fruit. There is no Turkish or EU monograph; the record rests on the WHO monograph (Vol. 4, 2009) and PubMed.

In short

What is Barberry?

Barberry (Berberis vulgaris L.) is a plant of the Berberidaceae family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe, Asia and Siberia. Part used: the dried stem bark (who); the fruit (food and clinical trials).

What is Barberry used for?

Traditional use: The World Health Organization monograph (Vol. Scientific evidence: limited. Barberry has evidence, but not in the indication the WHO approves nor with the part that is the WHO drug.

Is Barberry safe?

Safety label: caution. Use with caution in high blood pressure, diabetes, glaucoma or a history of cardiovascular disease — the WHO monograph’s general precaution; berberine’s effects on blood pressure and glucose can collide with medication. Avoid sunlight and UVA after applying topical preparations containing berberine. Not for anyone allergic to the plant. Not recommended under 12, in pregnancy or lactation for lack of safety data. No serious adverse events were reported in the fruit trials; the bark and root are rich in alkaloids and do not count as food the way the fruit does.

Traditional use

WHO monograph — the bark

The World Health Organization monograph (Vol. 4, 2009) assesses the stem bark under three headings. Uses supported by clinical data: none. Uses described in pharmacopoeias: digestive complaints such as dyspepsia, diarrhoea, gastritis, fullness and flatulence — orally. In traditional medicine: cystitis, dysmenorrhoea, eczema, fever, haemorrhoids, nasal congestion, rheumatism; as cholagogue, diuretic and tonic. The monograph says so plainly: no clinical trial of the bark exists; the known active principle is berberine, and berberine itself has been tested in bacterial diarrhoea, trachoma and cutaneous leishmaniasis.

Turkey

There is no monograph for barberry on the Turkish list. The plant is a common thorny shrub of Anatolia; its sour red fruit is dried and eaten and added to rice (the zereshk of Persian cooking is the same fruit). Its yellow root and bark dye wool; "kadıntuzluğu" is a widespread folk name.

Iran — clinical trials of the fruit

Nearly all the randomised trials of the last twenty years come from Iran and were done not with the bark that is the WHO drug but with the fruit or its extract: blood lipids, blood glucose, blood pressure. The monograph’s plant and the clinical literature’s plant are the same species, but a different part.

In nature

Where it grows
Europe and northern Asia; forest edges and scrub of northern and central Anatolia.
Harvest season
Yellow flower clusters in May; sour red berries in September–October. The bark is stripped in spring or autumn.
Local names
Karamuk, kadıntuzluğu, amberbaris — common Turkish names; Persian zereshk.

Mix-up risk

Other Berberis species grow in Turkey (B. crataegina among them) and their fruits are gathered together; the WHO monograph is for B. vulgaris only. Goldenseal (Hydrastis) and Coptis also carry berberine; "berberine" supplements are usually made from other sources, not this plant.

Preparation

The WHO monograph gives a daily dose for the bark: 0.5–1.0 g of drug three times daily, as a decoction; liquid extract (1:1, 60% ethanol) 0.3–1.0 ml three times daily; tincture (1:10) 2–4 ml three times daily. The doses in the clinical trials of the fruit are separate (mostly 1–3 g of dried fruit or extract daily). Not recommended under 12, in pregnancy or lactation.

What science says

Barberry has evidence, but not in the indication the WHO approves nor with the part that is the WHO drug. A meta-analysis published in 2019 in Complementary Therapies in Medicine pooled 5 randomised trials (339 people): barberry supplementation lowered total cholesterol by 23.6 mg/dL, triglycerides by 29.2 mg/dL and LDL by 13.8 mg/dL; no significant change in HDL. A second meta-analysis published in 2020 in the same journal (7 trials, 452 people) is more cautious on blood glucose: insulin fell, but no significant change in fasting glucose, HbA1c or HOMA-IR, with very high heterogeneity. A third meta-analysis in 2021 examined blood pressure. All three call for larger and longer trials; nearly all the trials are small and from one country. Evidence level Limited — and note: this evidence belongs to the fruit, not to the bark of the WHO monograph. For the bark itself the monograph’s verdict stands: no clinical trial.

Safety

Caution

Use with caution in high blood pressure, diabetes, glaucoma or a history of cardiovascular disease — the WHO monograph’s general precaution; berberine’s effects on blood pressure and glucose can collide with medication. Avoid sunlight and UVA after applying topical preparations containing berberine. Not for anyone allergic to the plant. Not recommended under 12, in pregnancy or lactation for lack of safety data. No serious adverse events were reported in the fruit trials; the bark and root are rich in alkaloids and do not count as food the way the fruit does.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
CiclosporinThe WHO monograph cites a study in kidney-transplant patients in which berberine hydrochloride raised ciclosporin blood levels by 75%; toxicity did not increase, but a rise in level matters for a narrow-margin drug. Transplant patients should not use this plant.
Chemotherapy drugs (paclitaxel)The monograph states that berberine upregulates the multidrug resistance transporter (P-gp) and may reduce the retention of chemotherapeutic agents. Anyone on chemotherapy should not use it without asking their doctor.
Antidiabetics and antihypertensives (theoretical)The counterpart of the monograph’s "use with caution": a plant affecting blood glucose and pressure can stack on a drug acting the same way.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Barberry.
