BT-312 · Solanaceae (nightshade family)

Potato

Solanum tuberosum L.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

Bitter potato species — the ones high in glycoalkaloids — are not discarded in the Andes. Because they are unsuited to fresh eating, they go almost exclusively into chuño, and the long soaking step of white chuño is reported to leach the glycoalkaloids of bitter species partly away; no measurement of that in *S. tuberosum* could be found for this record. The logic of the process is still here: Andean farming turned the plant’s own chemical defence into something that could be washed out.

Other names
Known in Turkish as patates, and regionally as kumpir or yer elması; potato in English, papa in Quechua. Neither the European Medicines Agency nor Türkiye’s TİTCK has a monograph for this species. EMA’s only *Solanum* monograph covers Solanum dulcamara stem — a separate record on this site — and does not apply to the potato.

In short

What is Potato?

Potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) is a plant of the Solanaceae (nightshade family) family. Traditions that use it: Andes. Part used: tuber.

What is Potato used for?

Traditional use: A 2010 field study in Economic Botany describes how farmers in the high Andes of central and southern Peru and Bolivia freeze-dry potatoes into chuño. Scientific evidence: traditional. The scientific file on the potato is a safety file rather than an efficacy one.

Is Potato safe?

Safety label: caution. Green, sprouted or bitter-tasting potatoes are not eaten. Glycoalkaloid poisoning brings nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea; more severe cases may be accompanied by drowsiness, restlessness, shaking, confusion, weakness and disturbed vision. In 1979, 78 children at a boarding school fell ill after eating potatoes at lunch and 17 were admitted to hospital; the source was a sack held in storage. Deaths attributed to potato glycoalkaloids have been reported, but they are few and the records are old (the most cited is a five-year-old boy in Glasgow in 1917). One detail weakens even that single attribution and belongs here: EFSA 2020 records that the post-mortem examination in that case gave bowel strangulation as the cause of death, read as a consequence of extreme retching and vomiting. The estimated lethal dose in humans is 3–6 mg/kg body weight, while the rodent LD50 is at least 300-fold higher — humans are considerably more sensitive than rodents. In a case reported in England in 1959, four members of a family fell ill after eating potatoes baked in their skins; a fifth person who ate only the flesh developed no symptoms at all — that is the difference between peel and flesh (the potatoes measured 500 mg/kg). Small children should be given peeled potatoes only and no potato-peel snacks. The reason is exposure rather than susceptibility: EFSA states plainly that the available human data do not allow a conclusion as to whether children or other subgroups are more susceptible to potato glycoalkaloids; what it did identify is the higher intake per kilogram in the younger age groups. The link proposed in 1972 between sprouted potatoes and neural tube defects was not supported by two British prospective studies; two Chinese case-control studies did find an association, but glycoalkaloid levels were not measured and EFSA treats the hypothesis as unconfirmed. The potato part of the claim that "nightshades cause inflammation" rests on cell culture and mouse studies; there is no such study in humans, and EFSA found no link between long-term intake and health problems in people. Safety level "moderate".

Traditional use

The Andes — chuño, the freeze-dried potato

A 2010 field study in Economic Botany describes how farmers in the high Andes of central and southern Peru and Bolivia freeze-dry potatoes into chuño. The work is done between 3,600 and 4,300 m, where sunny days and freezing nights break down the cell walls; the authors write that chuño "has made human life in the Andes above 3,800 m possible". The same study notes that bitter potato species, high in glycoalkaloids, are unsuited to fresh eating and are used almost only this way. That the long soaking step in white chuño (tunta) partly leaches glycoalkaloids into the water is often repeated, but it could not be confirmed as a measurement for this record. The 2026 chuño study in Foods measures no glycoalkaloids at all: what it measures is starch (amylose, viscoamylography, SEM, FT-IR, XRD), and the words "solanine" and "chaconine" do not appear in its text. It mentions the leaching in a single sentence of its Introduction, cites de Haan and colleagues for it, and ties it to *bitter and semi-domesticated* species (*S. ajanhuiri*, *S. juzepczukii*, *S. curtilobum*) — whereas this is a record for *S. tuberosum*. What de Haan and colleagues themselves measured was mineral content. So neither document carries a glycoalkaloid measurement of leaching in this species.

Worldwide — a staple food

FAO’s International Year of the Potato report states that a medium potato supplies a high amount of potassium and roughly half an adult’s daily vitamin C requirement. The same report lists chuño among the staples of the Inca state storehouses.

In nature

Where it grows
Kew records 34 regions in all, but only 10 of them are native: north-western and southern Argentina, Bolivia, three regions of Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru and Venezuela — that is, the Andean belt and Chile. The remaining 24 are introduced. Türkiye is not in the native range — cultivation is a separate matter and the potato is widely grown there. Chuño processing is carried out between 3,600 and 4,300 m.
Harvest season
In Türkiye planting is in spring and harvest runs from summer into autumn. In the Andes chuño making depends on freezing nights and is done in the winter months.
Local names
patates, kumpir, yer elması

Mix-up risk

A greened or sprouted tuber should not be treated as an intact one: it is the same part of the same plant but carries a different glycoalkaloid load. Potato leaves and green fruits are not eaten. The Turkish name "yer elması" is also used for Jerusalem artichoke (Helianthus tuberosus), a different species in a different family.

Preparation

The potato is a food; no official monograph sets an accepted "therapeutic dose" and this page gives no measure of use. The consumer advice of Germany’s Federal Institute for Risk Assessment (BfR) is: generously cut away green parts and the "eyes", do not eat a dish that tastes bitter, store potatoes in a cool, dark and dry place, do not reuse the water potatoes were boiled in (some of the alkaloids pass into it), and give small children peeled potatoes only. Green or heavily sprouted tubers and potato-peel snacks are not eaten.

What science says

The scientific file on the potato is a safety file rather than an efficacy one. In 2020 the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) published a formal risk assessment of potato glycoalkaloids (α-solanine and α-chaconine). The acute effects in humans are nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea, and for these EFSA took 1 mg total glycoalkaloids per kg body weight per day as the lowest observed adverse effect level. For EFSA, no health-based guidance value could be set; that does not hold for every body — BfR, on which this record relies, derived its own NOAEL of 0.5 mg per kg body weight per day and tied its 100 mg/kg recommendation for table potatoes to not exceeding that NOAEL, while noting that in view of the incomplete data these recommendations "should be regarded as provisional". No evidence was found linking repeated or long-term intake in humans to health problems. EFSA did identify a health concern for the younger age groups, particularly at the higher exposure percentiles. The widely accepted safety limit is 200 mg/kg in unpeeled, uncooked potatoes, but that ceiling is itself contested: BfR, which this record relies on, recommends that the glycoalkaloid content of potatoes intended for consumption lie below 100 mg/kg, and Hungary has set a legal maximum of 100 mg/kg for all raw unpeeled potatoes; the Nordic guideline and Denmark reserve 100 mg/kg for new varieties. There is no legal maximum at EU level. A normal tuber holds around 20–100 mg/kg, while the peel carries a multiple of the flesh: across 60 cultivars, 4–957 mg/kg in the flesh against 150–8,133 mg/kg in the peel (dry weight). Sprouts are far higher. Light drives glycoalkaloid synthesis; the increase also happens in the dark, but at roughly a fifth of the rate. Green colour is not the glycoalkaloid itself but its indicator — chlorophyll and glycoalkaloid formation are triggered together by light yet are genetically independent. On cooking, the accurate statement is this: glycoalkaloids are stable to decomposition below 150 °C, so a green potato cannot be made safe by cooking it; peeling, however, reduces the content by 25–75%, and in peeled potatoes boiling reduces it by 5–65% and frying by 20–90%. In an ascending dose study, 14 volunteers were split across six arms (2–3 per arm); in the arms given mashed potato containing 200 mg/kg, no acute systemic effects appeared at 0.95–1.25 mg/kg; one person at the highest dose became nauseous and vomited. The same study notes that clearance takes more than 24 h, so the compounds may accumulate with daily consumption. Taste is a warning: below 100 mg/kg it is not detectable, above that a burning sensation develops on the tongue and back of the mouth within 15–30 seconds, and above 200 mg/kg the burning is immediate. Evidence level "traditional" — this is a food record and carries no therapeutic claim.
