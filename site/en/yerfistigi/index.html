In short

What is Peanut?

Peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.) is a plant of the Fabaceae (legume family) family. Traditions that use it: Amazon. Part used: seed.

What is Peanut used for?

Traditional use: The peanut is a crop of South American origin, now grown through the warm zones of the world for oil, protein and snacks. Scientific evidence: clinical. A review in Molecular Immunology states that 16 proteins in peanut are officially recognised as allergens and that they trigger specific IgE production in predisposed people.

Is Peanut safe?

Safety label: generally safe. The peanut is a common food and causes no trouble for most people. The issue is allergy: according to the review in Molecular Immunology there are 16 officially recognised allergens in peanut, and the same review states that symptoms can be triggered by tiny amounts of allergen and even manifest as severe anaphylaxis. For an allergic person no safe amount can be worked out alone. Oral immunotherapy is conducted only under medical supervision, and most participants in the trials reported adverse events. No source assesses use above food amounts in pregnancy or lactation. The World Health Organization’s "Mycotoxins" fact sheet states that nuts including peanuts are regularly contaminated with aflatoxins, advises inspecting them for evidence of mould and discarding any that look mouldy, discoloured or shrivelled, and notes that mould can penetrate deep into food rather than only growing on the surface.

Traditional use

South America — the origin of the cultivated peanut

The peanut is a crop of South American origin, now grown through the warm zones of the world for oil, protein and snacks. The sources relied on here treat the plant not as a medicinal plant but as a food and an allergen.

Modern medicine — the allergen itself as the medicine

Almost every human study found for this species examines not a benefit of the plant but the treatment of peanut allergy. What is used in oral immunotherapy is measured amounts of peanut protein itself. According to the European Medicines Agency’s product page, Palforzia (defatted powder of Arachis hypogaea L., semen) was authorised in the European Union on 21 December 2020; it is used to treat peanut allergy in children aged 1 to 17, is available on prescription only, and the first phase of treatment is carried out in a clinic equipped to treat severe allergic reactions. Here the peanut is not a healing herb but the substance used to accustom an allergic person’s immune system.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew’s records its range covers 103 regions; it needs warmth and sandy soil. Its origin is South America. After the flower is fertilised its stalk bends down and the fruit ripens in the soil.
Harvest season
It is grown on Turkey’s Mediterranean and Aegean coasts, with harvest in autumn. The sources relied on here give no collection calendar for medicinal use.
Local names
yer fıstığı, Amerikan fıstığı, çerez fıstığı.

Mix-up risk

The pistachio (Pistacia vera) and the cashew are quite different plants, with different allergies. According to the cross-reactivity review in Current Allergy and Asthma Reports, clinically relevant sensitisation to other legumes such as soybean, lupin, lentil or pea occurs in peanut-allergic people: of 39 peanut-sensitised patients, 82% were also sensitised to lupin, 55% to pea and 87% to soybean, yet on double-blind placebo-controlled food challenge only 29–35% had symptoms to those beans. For sesame, the same review notes shared allergen families (oleosins) but states that their clinical relevance and IgE cross-reactivity are unknown.

Preparation

No official monograph sets an accepted dose; the peanut is a food. Oral immunotherapy is not something to do at home: in the studies relied on here, doses were given under medical supervision, in settings prepared for anaphylaxis, and raised step by step over weeks. Almost all participants in those studies had adverse events.

What science says

A review in Molecular Immunology states that 16 proteins in peanut are officially recognised as allergens and that they trigger specific IgE production in predisposed people. The ARC004 study, published in the Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology: In Practice, is the open-label follow-on to the randomised controlled PALISADE trial: it continued with 358 participants aged 4–17, desensitisation rates appeared higher in the daily-dosing cohorts than in the non-daily ones, and 83% of participants had mild or moderate adverse events. In the RAMSES trial in Annals of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology, among 506 children the adverse-event rate during initial dose escalation was 55% on treatment against 33.9% on placebo, and 98.8% against 94% during updosing — both figures belong to the dose-raising period, not to maintenance; the commonest were gastrointestinal. None of these studies shows the peanut curing a disease; what they measure is an allergic person’s threshold of tolerance to peanut.
