In short

What is White horehound?

White horehound (Marrubium vulgare L.) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the flowering aerial parts.

What is White horehound used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places all three indications under "traditional use": (1) as an expectorant in cough associated with a cold; (2) symptomatic treatment of mild dyspeptic complaints such as bloating and flatulence; (3) temporary loss of appetite. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no randomised clinical trial of white horehound on its own.

Is White horehound safe?

Safety label: caution. Not to be used in bile duct obstruction, cholangitis, liver disease or bowel obstruction — note the last: ileus is an emergency and not something to approach with a herbal product. Not for anyone hypersensitive to the active substance or to other plants of the Lamiaceae family; that family is large and includes thyme, mint, lemon balm and sage. People with an active peptic ulcer, gallstones or other biliary disorders should consult a doctor before use. Use should be stopped two weeks before surgery. Not recommended under 12; not to be used in pregnancy or lactation. If symptoms worsen, consult a doctor or pharmacist.

Traditional use

Europe — three indications

The EMA monograph places all three indications under "traditional use": (1) as an expectorant in cough associated with a cold; (2) symptomatic treatment of mild dyspeptic complaints such as bloating and flatulence; (3) temporary loss of appetite. It is one of the few drugs used on both the respiratory and the digestive side.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the flowering aerial parts". The contraindications concern the bile and the bowel: not to be used in bile duct obstruction, cholangitis, liver disease or bowel obstruction (ileus). Use should be stopped two weeks before surgery. People with an active peptic ulcer, gallstones or other biliary disorders should consult a doctor before use.

Anatolia

White horehound is very common along field margins, roadsides and pastures in Turkey, a whitish, hairy member of the mint family. Its bitterness is marked and underlies both the cough and the digestive use. A tea is made from it in folk practice.

In nature

Where it grows
Field margins, roadsides, pastures and waste ground of Europe, North Africa and western Asia. Very common in Turkey.
Harvest season
Whorls of white flowers in the leaf axils from May to September; the flowering aerial parts are gathered then.
Local names
Bozotu, karaderme, itsineği, kukasotu — all four appear in the Turkish monograph.

Mix-up risk

Many Marrubium species grow in Turkey and several are called bozotu; the EU monograph covers M. vulgare only. Ballota species of the same family also look similar and the local names overlap between the two — one of the names the Turkish monograph itself gives is "karaderme", which is also used for Ballota.

Preparation

The monograph describes oral preparations of the flowering aerial parts. Extracts containing ethanol must carry the appropriate labelling. If symptoms worsen, consult a doctor or pharmacist. Not recommended under 12. Use should be stopped two weeks before surgery.

What science says

There is no randomised clinical trial of white horehound on its own. There are studies of multi-ingredient cough products containing it, and those measure the mixture, not any single plant. The rest of the recent literature is at laboratory level: chemical profiling, antioxidant and cytotoxicity measurements, computational modelling. These show what is in the plant; they do not show whether it relieves cough or bloating in a patient. The EU monograph rests all three indications on long use. The known mechanism comes from the bitterness and ties all three indications together: bitter drugs stimulate digestive secretion and so open the appetite, and the same bitter compounds are associated with an expectorant effect in the airways. That mechanism also explains the Turkish monograph’s peptic-ulcer warning. The evidence level is Traditional.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Diabetes medicinesThe Turkish monograph lists this group drug by drug: glimepiride, glyburide, insulin, pioglitazone, rosiglitazone, chlorpropamide, glipizide, tolbutamide and others. Most monographs say "antidiabetics" and move on; naming them makes visible how wide the warning reaches. Anyone on diabetes medication should tell their doctor.
Blood pressure medicinesThe Turkish monograph names this group too: captopril, enalapril, losartan, valsartan, diltiazem, amlodipine, hydrochlorothiazide, furosemide and others. The list covers all the main classes of blood pressure medicine.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing; the Turkish one names two large drug groups drug by drug. The gap between them shows why "none reported" should not be taken as reassurance on its own.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying White horehound.
