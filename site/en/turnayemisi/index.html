In short

What is Cranberry?

Cranberry (Vaccinium macrocarpon) is a plant of the Ericaceae family. Traditions that use it: North America. Part used: fruit.

What is Cranberry used for?

Traditional use: Indigenous peoples such as the Wampanoag mixed the berries with dried meat and fat to make pemmican, used them to dye cloth and placed them on wounds. Scientific evidence: clinical. A Cochrane review (2023) found that cranberry products reduce the risk of urinary tract infection in women with recurrent infections, in children, and after some medical procedures.

Is Cranberry safe?

Safety label: generally safe. Generally safe. Large amounts of juice can upset the stomach and cause diarrhoea; sweetened juices raise blood sugar. People with a history of kidney stones should avoid high doses. Interaction with warfarin has been reported, though newer studies show no serious effect; INR should still be monitored.

Traditional use

North America

Indigenous peoples such as the Wampanoag mixed the berries with dried meat and fat to make pemmican, used them to dye cloth and placed them on wounds. European settlers learned the fruit from them.

Today

Juice and capsules are used to prevent recurrent urinary tract infections; in the US it becomes the sauce of the Thanksgiving table.

In nature

Where it grows
A creeping dwarf shrub of acid bogs and marshes in the north-eastern US and Canada.
Harvest season
Berries ripen September–November.
Local names
turna yemişi

Mix-up risk

The plant called kızılcık in Türkiye is Cornus mas, a completely different plant; cranberry products are sometimes wrongly labelled kızılcık. The European cranberry (V. oxycoccos) has smaller fruit.

Preparation

Used as unsweetened juice or standardised capsules; most dried cranberries are sweetened.

What science says

A Cochrane review (2023) found that cranberry products reduce the risk of urinary tract infection in women with recurrent infections, in children, and after some medical procedures. No benefit was seen in older people in care homes, pregnant women or people with bladder-emptying problems. Cranberry does not treat infection and is no substitute for antibiotics.

Safety

Generally safe

Generally safe. Large amounts of juice can upset the stomach and cause diarrhoea; sweetened juices raise blood sugar. People with a history of kidney stones should avoid high doses. Interaction with warfarin has been reported, though newer studies show no serious effect; INR should still be monitored.
