Lemon myrtle

Backhousia citriodora F.Muell.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

The 2021 review calls the species "an unrivalled source of citral": among the countless sources of the compounds that carry a lemon note, the leaf of this Australian tree is held to be superior. So the purest commercial source of the scent of lemon is not a lemon but a tree in the myrtle family.

Other names
English lemon myrtle, lemon ironwood, sweet verbena tree. It has no established Turkish name; "limon mersini" is a descriptive rendering of the English one. In Australia the leaf is grown as a spice and a tea, and the essential oil is sold to the flavour industry. Neither the European Medicines Agency nor the Turkish agency has a monograph for this species.
Part used
Leaf (fresh or dried); the essential oil distilled from the leaf
Region
Oceania

In short

What is Lemon myrtle?

Lemon myrtle (Backhousia citriodora F.Muell.) is a plant of the Myrtaceae (myrtle family) family. Traditions that use it: Oceania. Part used: leaf (fresh or dried); the essential oil distilled from the leaf.

What is Lemon myrtle used for?

Traditional use: A comprehensive review published in Foods in 2021 examines the species’ history, natural occurrence, cultivation, taxonomy, chemistry, biological activity, toxicology, standardisation and commercialisation. Scientific evidence: traditional. The only study in humans is the thirty-one-child molluscum contagiosum trial; it is vehicle-controlled and randomised, so it is genuine evidence, but it is small and unrepeated.

Is Lemon myrtle safe?

Safety label: caution. Using the leaf as a spice and a tea is ordinary. The concentrated essential oil is a separate matter, and here there is a measured hazard: a study published in Food and Chemical Toxicology in 2002 reports that lemon myrtle oil and its major component citral were very toxic to human cell lines (HepG2, a skin-derived fibroblast line and primary cultures of human skin fibroblasts); IC50 values ranged from 0.008–0.014% at four hours to 0.003–0.012% at twenty-four hours. The same study calculates a no-observed-adverse-effect level of 0.5 mg/l and a reference dose of 0.01 mg/l, and states that a product containing 1% oil was found low in toxicity. The 2023 study likewise shows the oil triggering apoptosis in HepG2 cells. These are in vitro measurements, but they all point the same way: the oil is not applied undiluted to the skin and is not taken internally. A study published in Antioxidants in 2024 found a 96-hour LC50 of 270 mg/L in zebrafish larvae — an animal model. Citral is a fragrant aldehyde and can sensitise the skin. The sources relied on here contain no separate assessment for pregnancy, breastfeeding or drug interactions.

Traditional use

Spice and tea — a cultivated crop in Australia

A comprehensive review published in Foods in 2021 examines the species’ history, natural occurrence, cultivation, taxonomy, chemistry, biological activity, toxicology, standardisation and commercialisation. The review writes that the lemon note in the flavour and fragrance industry comes from citronellal and citral, that citral is preferred, and that among the many known sources of citral this Australian tree is considered superior.

For molluscum contagiosum — a randomised trial in children

A study published in Biomedicine & Pharmacotherapy in 2004 treated thirty-one children (mean age 4.6) diagnosed with molluscum contagiosum with a once-daily 10% solution of the oil or vehicle (olive oil). At twenty-one days, nine of the sixteen children in the oil group showed a greater than 90% reduction in lesion count, while no child in the vehicle group met the same criterion (P < 0.05). No adverse events were reported. It is a small trial and has not been repeated in a larger one since. The systematic review with network meta-analysis published in Paediatric Drugs in 2026 — the first pediatric-specific one — still mentions the option and places it plainly: single-study treatments including "Backhousia citriodora oil" achieved the highest rankings but require validation. The treatments the review puts forward on robust multi-study evidence are salicylic acid + lactic acid, cantharidin and potassium hydroxide.

Food preservative and antimicrobial work

A study published in Foods in 2018 tested the species’ solvent extracts against two bacteria and six weak-acid-resistant yeasts, reporting broad-spectrum antifungal activity. Fluorescence microscopy attributes the mode of action to damage of the yeast cell membrane. A study published in Molecules in 2022 measured the leaf oil’s composition and its antioxidant, antibacterial and antibiofilm activity. All of these are in vitro measurements.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew’s records its natural range is a single region: Queensland. It is native to the rainforest belt of north-eastern Australia, and is cultivated today for its leaf and oil.
Harvest season
The sources relied on here give no collection calendar for Turkey; the plant does not grow there naturally.
Local names
It has no established Turkish local name.

Mix-up risk

It is in the same family as myrtle (*Myrtus communis*) but a different genus; the "myrtle" in its name indicates a family resemblance, not kinship, and what is written for myrtle does not apply to this species. Anise myrtle (*Syzygium anisatum*) is another tree of the same family, sold alongside this one in the Australian spice trade; their scents are entirely different.

Preparation

No dose is accepted in an official monograph. The leaf is a food: in Australia it is dried and ground as a spice and infused as a tea, and the sources relied on here give no upper limit for that use. The essential oil is an entirely different matter: in the single clinical trial the solution applied to the skin was 10% and its vehicle was olive oil, while the 2002 toxicology study writes that a product containing 1% oil was found low in toxicity and could be used in topical antimicrobial products. The oil is not taken internally and is not applied undiluted to the skin.

What science says

The only study in humans is the thirty-one-child molluscum contagiosum trial; it is vehicle-controlled and randomised, so it is genuine evidence, but it is small and unrepeated. All the remaining efficacy data are in vitro: inhibition of yeasts and bacteria, antioxidant measurements, biofilm measurements. A study published in the Journal of Ethnopharmacology in 2023 reports that the oil arrests the cell cycle at S phase and triggers apoptosis in HepG2 cells — that is a cell line measurement and does not mean a cancer treatment; as the safety note below shows, the same measurements are also an indication that the concentrated oil is harmful to human cells.
