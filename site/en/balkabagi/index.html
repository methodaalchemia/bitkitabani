Bitterness sometimes comes not from the plant itself but from the root it was grafted onto: in a 2019 study every melon plant grafted onto *Cucurbita maxima* rootstock produced bitter fruit, while ungrafted plants did not. A bitter melon may be carrying the chemistry of the rootstock rather than of the melon.

Other names
Known in Turkish as bal kabağı or kestane kabağı; winter squash or Hubbard squash in English. Neither the European Medicines Agency nor Türkiye’s TİTCK has a monograph for this species. EMA’s `Cucurbitae semen` (pumpkin seed) monograph belongs to *Cucurbita pepo* — a separate record on this site — and does not apply to *C. maxima*.

In short

What is Winter squash?

Winter squash (Cucurbita maxima Duchesne) is a plant of the Cucurbitaceae (gourd family) family. Traditions that use it: Andes. Part used: fruit flesh and seeds.

What is Winter squash used for?

Traditional use: This is one of the three major cultivated species of the gourd family; even EMA’s *C. Scientific evidence: traditional. The scientific file here splits in two, and the distinction between species is critical.

Is Winter squash safe?

Safety label: caution. A squash that tastes bitter is not eaten — that is the single most important rule. Cucurbitacin poisoning brings diarrhoea, vomiting and abdominal pain, with the dehydration, low blood pressure, raised heart rate, headache and dizziness that follow. In the French series of 353 patients no deaths and no severe cases were reported, though 14 patients were of moderate severity. Hair loss after cucurbitacin poisoning is reported in two publications. Honesty is required here: most of these human cases give no Latin name, and the only group that does is bottle gourd; no human data ties these cases to *C. maxima*. The capacity of *C. maxima* to carry cucurbitacins has, however, been shown at the enzyme and grafting level, so the rule holds regardless of species. Risk rises with squashes grown from saved seed, with planting beside ornamental gourds and cross-pollination, and in hot seasons. Poison centres state that cucurbitacin is not destroyed by cooking; no peer-reviewed measurement of its heat stability under boiling or baking conditions could be found (measurements on drying temperature do exist). The indirect evidence is this: cucurbitacin E has once been detected in canned squash — in canned zucchini in California and yellow squash in Alabama, both *C. pepo* — meaning it survived commercial heat processing. Safety level "moderate".

Traditional use

South America and worldwide — a winter vegetable

This is one of the three major cultivated species of the gourd family; even EMA’s *C. pepo* assessment report notes that "the most popular in Central and Northern Europe are Cucurbita pepo L., Cucurbita moschata Duch. and Cucurbita maxima Duch." The flesh is eaten cooked and the seeds roasted.

Nutrition — measured at species level

A 2007 comparison in the Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry showed that the carotenoid profiles of the three squash species differ: β- and α-carotene dominate in *C. moschata*, while lutein and β-carotene lead in *C. maxima* and *C. pepo*, and violaxanthin predominated in one *C. maxima* cultivar examined. A 2012 study in Nutrition Research and Practice found significantly more carbohydrate, protein, fat and fibre in the flesh of *C. maxima* than in the other two species, and the highest β-carotene content in its seeds.

In nature

Where it grows
Kew records a range of 70 regions, a figure that includes naturalisation. The species originates in South America; Türkiye is not in the native range, cultivation being a separate and widespread matter.
Harvest season
In Türkiye the harvest is in autumn; the fruit keeps through winter, which is why it is called a winter squash.
Local names
bal kabağı, kestane kabağı

Mix-up risk

The most important confusion is with ornamental gourds, which carry high cucurbitacin levels and are not grown to be eaten. In squashes grown from saved seed, backcrossing can bring the bitterness back. The species with a pumpkin seed monograph is *Cucurbita pepo*, a separate record; there is no monograph dose for the seeds of this species. Bitter bottle gourd (*Lagenaria siceraria*) is a different species whose juice has caused fatal poisonings.

Preparation

Winter squash is a food; no official monograph sets an accepted dose and this page gives no measure of use. It is eaten cooked. The only rule is a safety rule: a squash that tastes bitter is not eaten. The recommendation of the French poison centre series of 353 patients is, verbatim, "we recommend disposing of any squash with a bitter taste". Taste a very small piece of the raw squash, spit it out if it is bitter, and do not try to cook a bitter squash.

What science says

The scientific file here splits in two, and the distinction between species is critical. The bitter triterpenoids called cucurbitacins are the source of poisoning in this family: in a French poison centre series of 353 patients covering 2012–2016 the picture was mostly diarrhoea, vomiting and abdominal pain, with no deaths and no severe cases, and 14 patients of moderate severity. Hair loss following cucurbitacin poisoning is reported in two publications: a 2018 short report in JAMA Dermatology (by a French author) and a 2023 French conference abstract. Care is needed over which species these belong to: most of these human cases give no Latin name — the series of 353 patients says "non-edible squash", the hair-loss reports say "bitter-tasting pumpkin soup" and "spaghetti squash", and the 2020 German case says "bitter zucchini". That these are *C. pepo* is an inference, not something the sources state. The only group of cases giving a Latin binomial is bottle gourd (*Lagenaria siceraria*) poisoning — a separate record on this site. An independent search found no human cucurbitacin poisoning case naming *C. maxima*. The capacity of *C. maxima* to carry cucurbitacins has been shown at species level, though not with human data: the enzyme cucurbitacin B Δ23-reductase was isolated directly from *C. maxima* (Green Hubbard) fruit, and a 2019 study in PLoS ONE found that all melon plants grafted onto *C. maxima* rootstocks produced bitter fruit while ungrafted plants did not. Cultivated squashes have been bred to be free of cucurbitacins; EMA’s own assessment report states that "backmutations occur randomly which may lead to plants with toxic and bitter fruits". Evidence level "traditional".
