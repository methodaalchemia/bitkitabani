Caraway

Carum carvi L.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

In Turkish the word "kimyon" points to two different plants, both in the carrot family: cumin (Cuminum cyminum) and caraway (Carum carvi). Separate genera, separate flavours — caraway closer to anise, sweetish and sharp — and separate monographs on the Turkish list. English has the same trouble: caraway and cumin are frequently translated for one another. When reading a monograph, checking which Latin name it carries is safer than checking which common name it carries.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the name "frenk kimyonu" and lists no synonyms. In English: caraway. ⚠️ It is not the same plant as cumin (Cuminum cyminum): both are in the carrot family, but they are separate genera with separate flavours and separate monographs.

In short

What is Caraway?

Caraway (Carum carvi L.) is a plant of the Apiaceae (carrot family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe, Asia and Siberia. Part used: the fruit (comminuted) and the essential oil obtained from it by steam distillation.

What is Caraway used for?

Traditional use: The European Medicines Agency has published two monographs for caraway — the fruit and the essential oil — and the indication is the same in both, both under "traditional use": symptomatic relief of digestive disorders such as bloating and flatulence. Scientific evidence: limited. Caraway’s strongest evidence exists — but not for caraway alone: it is for caraway with peppermint oil.

Is Caraway safe?

Safety label: caution. The essential oil must not be taken orally undiluted — the Turkish monograph’s explicit provision; essential oils are used diluted and in measured doses. It must not be applied to mucous membranes, around the eyes or to broken skin. Oral use is not recommended in liver disease, cholangitis, achlorhydria (absence of stomach acid), gallstones and other biliary disorders. People with diabetes and people with haemochromatosis should use it with caution, and use should be stopped two weeks before surgery — the source the monograph gives for these three warnings is a consumer medicines database, not a clinical study; they are reproduced here as they stand because they are on the official list. Hypersensitivity can occur in anyone allergic to the carrot family, to mugwort or to birch. The tea is not recommended under 12 and the oil not orally under 18; it must not be used in pregnancy or lactation.

Traditional use

Europe — two drugs, one indication

The European Medicines Agency has published two monographs for caraway — the fruit and the essential oil — and the indication is the same in both, both under "traditional use": symptomatic relief of digestive disorders such as bloating and flatulence. The oil is also used as a thin layer applied to the abdomen.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph lists both drugs. The scope of the contraindication is notable: hypersensitivity can occur in people sensitive to fennel, anise, celery, coriander and dill of the same family, and also to mugwort and birch. Oral use is not recommended in liver disease, cholangitis, achlorhydria, gallstones and other biliary disorders. The essential oil must not be taken orally undiluted, and must not be applied to mucous membranes, around the eyes, or to broken skin.

Anatolia and the kitchen

Caraway grows in Turkey and its seed is used as a spice in bread, pickles, cheese and vegetable dishes. The belief that it eases digestion is woven into the cooking: the habit of adding caraway to cauliflower, cabbage and pulses is precisely the folk form of the monograph’s indication.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to Europe, western Asia and North Africa; it grows and is cultivated in Turkey.
Harvest season
A biennial; it flowers and fruits in its second year. The fruit ripens in early summer and is dried.
Local names
Frenk kimyonu — the Turkish monograph gives one Turkish name. Regional names exist in common speech, but this catalog does not print a name it has no source for.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ It is confused with cumin (Cuminum cyminum); the two are separate genera with separate monographs. Fennel, anise, coriander and dill are in the same family and their fruits can be mixed up when seen together — and because that family also contains lethal species such as hemlock, the fruit of an umbellifer should not be gathered from the wild.

Preparation

The fruit: 0.5–2 g of the comminuted drug infused in 150 ml of boiling water, 1–3 times daily. The essential oil orally: 0.15–0.3 ml daily, divided into 1–3 doses. Cutaneous: 2% semi-solid preparations applied once daily as a thin layer on the abdomen — this form may be used in infants as well. If symptoms persist longer than two weeks, consult a doctor or pharmacist. The tea is not recommended under 12, and the oil is not recommended orally under 18.

What science says

Caraway’s strongest evidence exists — but not for caraway alone: it is for caraway with peppermint oil. A Cochrane review published in 2023 examined non-Chinese herbal medicines in functional dyspepsia (41 trials, 4,477 participants, 27 herbal medicines) and found this for peppermint and caraway oil: compared with placebo at four weeks, it probably results in a large improvement in global dyspepsia symptoms (SMD −0.87; 95% CI −1.15 to −0.58; 2 studies, 210 people) and increases the improvement rate (RR 1.53; 95% CI 1.30–1.81; 3 studies, 305 people), both at moderate certainty. Quality of life also improved. That is firmer evidence than many plants in this catalog carry — but what was measured is a fixed two-oil combination, and caraway’s own share of the effect has not been separated out. There is no trial of caraway alone in the monograph indication, which is why the EU and Turkey both keep it under traditional use. We record the evidence level as Limited: there is moderate-certainty evidence, but for the combination.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
The interaction list the Turkish monograph reportsThe monograph states it may interact with: antidiabetic medicines, lithium, drugs metabolised by CYP1A1, sedatives (central nervous system depressants), diuretics, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, iron preparations and rifampin. The monograph gives a consumer medicines database as the source for that list — these are compiled cautions, not interactions demonstrated in clinical studies. For anyone taking a narrow-margin drug such as lithium or isoniazid, telling the doctor is still the right move.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographsNeither European monograph lists anything in its interaction section. That two agencies give different interaction lists for the same plant shows the lists are compiled from different sources.
Use together with peppermint oilNot an interaction but the place the evidence comes from: the product measured in dyspepsia is a fixed combination of peppermint and caraway oil. It has not been shown that the two oils taken separately give the same effect.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Caraway.
