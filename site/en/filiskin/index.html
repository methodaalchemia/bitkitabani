Slender ziziphora

Evidence: LimitedCaution

A study published in Scientific Reports in 2025 describes the species as an endangered medicinal plant in Iran. The same study showed that at room temperature the essential oil loses most of its pulegone in three months: the content fell from 42.84 per cent to 7.86.

Other names
In Türkiye the genus Ziziphora is known by the names nane ruhu, kır nanesi, dağ reyhanı, firüskül and karınağrısı otu; these names are shared by several species of the genus. The name “filiskin” could not be confirmed for this species in the sources examined. Persian kakuti (“Cacotti” in publications). Its synonyms include Ziziphora acutifolia Montbret & Aucher ex Benth. and Ziziphora parviflora (Trautv.) Des.-Shost. (Kew). It has no monograph on the EMA or TİTCK lists.

In short

What is Slender ziziphora?

Slender ziziphora (Ziziphora tenuior L.) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Asia and Siberia. Part used: flowering aerial parts; the essential oil.

What is Slender ziziphora used for?

Traditional use: A 2020 field study on the ethnobotany of the Ziziphora taxa of Türkiye compiled local names and uses through face-to-face interviews in seven geographical regions. Scientific evidence: limited. There is no clinical trial in humans on the species; all of the studies reported in the 2025 review are preclinical.

Is Slender ziziphora safe?

Safety label: caution. ⚠️ In most samples of the species the main constituent of the essential oil is pulegone (reported contents 26.65–67.34 per cent). According to the EMA’s public statement on pulegone and menthofuran (2016), the main target organ of these substances is the liver; drinking about 10 ml of pennyroyal oil, which is high in pulegone, has caused moderate to severe poisoning, and more than 15 ml has caused death. The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies pulegone in Group 2B (possibly carcinogenic to humans). The EMA recommends 0.75 mg/kg body weight per day as the acceptable limit for the sum of pulegone and menthofuran in herbal medicines; this comes to 37.5 mg a day in life-long use for a 50 kg adult and is scaled down with body weight in children. Among the people the plant is known as an emmenagogue; it should not be used in pregnancy, its medicinal use should be avoided while breastfeeding and in children, and the essential oil should not be drunk. There are no human safety data for the species. People with liver disease should keep away from strong teas and extracts. If the essential oil has been swallowed, call 114 (the National Poison Information Centre of Türkiye) or 112.

Traditional use

Türkiye — spice, pleasure tea and folk remedy

A 2020 field study on the ethnobotany of the Ziziphora taxa of Türkiye compiled local names and uses through face-to-face interviews in seven geographical regions. According to the study Ziziphora tenuior is one of the species used in different regions, and the genus is in general valued as a food spice, a pleasure tea and a medicinal plant. A 2025 review reports that the infusions are drunk for colds, coughs and digestive complaints.

Türkiye and Iran — recorded traditional uses

The same review records that for Türkiye and Iran the plant is used internally as an infusion and externally as a decoction. It is reported to be used as an analgesic, antipyretic, expectorant, stomachic, carminative and emmenagogue; and in colds, flu, gastritis, constipation, dysentery, wounds and burns. These are records of folk use, not proven indications.

Jordan — cough and abdominal pain

According to a 2016 paper in the Journal of Ethnopharmacology, in Jordan the plant goes into antimicrobial, expectorant and wound-healing folk preparations; it is used for cough, stomach ache, dysentery, fever, uterus infection, gut inflammation and painful menstruation.

Uzbekistan — a desert plant used since old times

A field study conducted between 2012 and 2022 in the arid regions of Uzbekistan identified 529 medicinal species; Ziziphora tenuior is counted among the species that are spread over a large area and have been used regularly by local people since old times.

In nature

Where it grows
According to the Kew world plant checklist its native range extends from eastern Ukraine to Xinjiang and the Arabian Peninsula: Türkiye, the East Aegean islands, Crimea, the Caucasus, Iran, Iraq, Lebanon-Syria, Palestine, Sinai, the Gulf states, Oman, Central Asia, Afghanistan and the western Himalaya. In Türkiye, samples collected from Muğla have been studied. In Uzbekistan it is common in saline and degraded arid areas.
Harvest season
It is an annual herb; the 2025 review notes that the only perennial species of the genus is Ziziphora clinopodioides. In a study in Iran the pulegone content was measured at 67.34 per cent at the flowering stage and 59.61 per cent before flowering.
Local names
Nane ruhu, kır nanesi, dağ reyhanı, firüskül, karınağrısı otu (names shared across the genus Ziziphora).

Mix-up risk

The same Turkish names are also used for the other species of the genus; the name alone does not identify the species. It differs from the perennial dağ reyhanı (Ziziphora clinopodioides) in being annual. Pulegone is also the main constituent in the oil of pennyroyal (Mentha pulegium) (EMA); a similar scent does not change the fact that it is a separate species.

Preparation

The aerial parts are infused to make tea or added to food as a spice. Because there is no monograph for this species on the EMA or TİTCK lists, there is no accepted dose. In most samples the main constituent of the essential oil is pulegone; for pulegone and menthofuran the EMA recommends that total daily intake should not exceed 0.75 mg per kilogram of body weight. This page gives no recipe for oral use of the essential oil. The essential oil degrades quickly at room temperature: in one study the pulegone content fell from 42.84 per cent to 7.86 per cent in three months, while the sample stored in the freezer kept its composition.

What science says

There is no clinical trial in humans on the species; all of the studies reported in the 2025 review are preclinical. The pulegone content of the essential oil varies from study to study: 55.1–67.34 per cent in Iranian samples, 46.8 per cent in the southern Jordan sample, 26.65 per cent in another Iranian sample; in one study, on the other hand, the main constituents were found to be α-terpineol (16.2 per cent) and thymol (10.4 per cent). The Jordanian oil inhibited the fungus Cryptococcus neoformans at a concentration of 0.16 µl/ml and showed an anti-inflammatory effect in vitro at concentrations that did not harm mammalian cells (0.16 and 0.32 µl/ml). The oil of the sample collected from Muğla was also active in vitro against bacteria and Candida albicans. In rats, 40, 80 and 160 mg/kg of extract for 8 weeks reduced the markers of inflammation and cell death caused in the liver and lung by an insecticide (chlorpyrifos). In vitro the essential oil increased cell death in colon cancer cells (HT-29); the highest effect was seen at a concentration of 200 µg/ml. These findings have not been tested in humans.

Safety

Caution

⚠️ In most samples of the species the main constituent of the essential oil is pulegone (reported contents 26.65–67.34 per cent). The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies pulegone in Group 2B (possibly carcinogenic to humans). Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Drug interactionsNo drug interaction study has been carried out for this species. The EMA statement notes that pulegone targets the liver; for this reason people with liver disease and those taking medicines that can affect the liver should not use pulegone-containing oil and concentrated extracts without consulting a doctor.
