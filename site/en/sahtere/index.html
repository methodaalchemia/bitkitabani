Common fumitory

Fumaria officinalis L.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

The plant’s name gives the same image in three languages, and the image is a surprising one: Fumaria in Latin (from fumus, smoke), "earth smoke" in English. The comparison comes from the plant’s fine, blue-grey, much-divided leaves, which seen in a mass look like smoke rising off the ground. The Turkish "şahtere", by contrast, comes from the Persian *shāh-terre*, "king of the earth" — so the same plant is the earth’s smoke in one language and the earth’s king in another.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the names "şahtere, sedef otu"; Fumaria angustifolia and F. cirrhata are synonyms. In English: fumitory, earth smoke — the Latin name comes from the same place, fumus terrae, "smoke of the earth". ⚠️ "Sedef otu" is also used in Turkish for Ruta graveolens, a different and riskier plant.

What is Common fumitory?

Common fumitory (Fumaria officinalis L.) is a plant of the Papaveraceae (gelincikgiller) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the aerial parts.

What is Common fumitory used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": relief of digestive disturbances such as feelings of fullness, slow digestion and flatulence. Scientific evidence: traditional. Fumitory has a randomised trial in humans, and the result is negative.

Is Common fumitory safe?

Safety label: caution. Used in large amounts it causes severe diarrhoea, muscle cramps and shortness of breath — that sentence in the Turkish monograph is a more concrete dose warning than most plants in this catalog carry, and it explains why the amount on the label should not be exceeded. Contraindicated in bile duct obstruction, cholangitis, gallstones, other biliary disease and hepatitis; the reason is written in the EU monograph: the plant may stimulate bile secretion, and stimulating it in these conditions makes matters worse. Hypersensitivity to the active substance can occur. Not used under 18, nor in pregnancy or lactation. If symptoms do not resolve over two weeks, or if adverse effects appear, consult a doctor. Tinctures and extracts may contain ethanol. Digestive complaints should not be managed with a herbal product for long without finding out their cause.

Traditional use

Europe — digestive complaints

The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": relief of digestive disturbances such as feelings of fullness, slow digestion and flatulence. The monograph also states the mechanism: the plant may stimulate bile secretion.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the aerial parts" and states two things plainly. First a dose warning: used in large amounts it causes severe diarrhoea, muscle cramps and shortness of breath. Second the contraindications: not used in bile duct obstruction, cholangitis, gallstones, other biliary disease or hepatitis.

Anatolia

Fumitory is common along field margins and in gardens in Turkey, flowering pink-purple in spring. A tea is made from it as a "blood cleanser" in folk use — that is not the monograph’s indication, and has no counterpart in it.

In nature

Where it grows
Field margins, vineyards, gardens and waste ground of Europe, North Africa and western Asia. A common arable weed in Turkey.
Harvest season
Pink-purple flowers with dark tips, in spikes, from spring into early summer; the aerial parts are gathered in flower.
Local names
Şahtere, sedef otu — both appear in the Turkish monograph.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ Two confusions. The name: "sedef otu" is also used in Turkish for Ruta graveolens, a different and riskier plant — the Latin name on the label settles it. The species: many Fumaria species grow in Turkey and all are called şahtere; the EU monograph covers F. officinalis only.

Preparation

The monograph describes oral preparations of the aerial parts. Dose matters: the Turkish monograph states that large amounts cause severe diarrhoea, muscle cramps and shortness of breath, so the amount on the label should not be exceeded. If symptoms do not resolve over two weeks, consult a doctor. Tinctures and extracts containing ethanol must carry the appropriate labelling. Not used under 18.

What science says

Fumitory has a randomised trial in humans, and the result is negative. In a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial published in 2005 in the Scandinavian Journal of Gastroenterology, 106 patients with irritable bowel syndrome were assigned to three groups: 1,500 mg of fumitory daily (24), 60 mg of Curcuma xanthorrhiza daily (24) and placebo (58). Treatment ran for 18 weeks, and the main outcome was patients’ own rating of pain and distension on a visual analogue scale. The result: no significant difference between the three groups in pain, distension, global assessment of symptoms or psychosocial stress from the condition. The authors’ conclusion is plain: neither fumitory nor Curcuma xanthorrhiza showed any therapeutic benefit over placebo, and the use of these herbs for irritable bowel syndrome cannot be recommended. One distinction has to be kept: that trial tested irritable bowel syndrome, whereas the EU monograph’s indication is narrower — fullness, slow digestion and flatulence. So the negative result does not test the monograph’s exact indication; but it is the most serious clinical test of the plant in digestive complaints, and it came out negative. The monograph rests its indication on long use. The evidence level is Traditional.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No drug interactions reported in either monographThe Turkish monograph says "no interaction is known" and the EU monograph lists nothing. The two agreeing is not common in this catalog; even so, "none known" does not mean studied and not found.
Medicines acting on the bile ductsThere is no interaction study; the issue comes from the mechanism. The plant is used to stimulate bile secretion, so anyone on medication for gallstones or biliary disease should ask their doctor — the monograph already contraindicates the plant in those conditions.
Antidiarrhoeal and bowel medicinesAgain from the mechanism: in large amounts fumitory can cause severe diarrhoea. Anyone taking an antidiarrhoeal or a medicine that affects bowel motility should take that into account.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Common fumitory.
