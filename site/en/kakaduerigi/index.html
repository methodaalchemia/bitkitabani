BT-328 · Combretaceae

Kakadu plum

Terminalia ferdinandiana Exell
Evidence: TraditionalGenerally safe

Its vitamin C content is unusually high: in the 2023 measurement in Meat Science more than ten per cent of the fruit powder’s dry weight — 12.05 grams per 100 grams — was L-ascorbic acid. Because the sources relied on here contain no measurement ranking that figure against other fruits, no claim of being "the richest" is written here. Because the fruit is largely wild-harvested, measuring this tree-to-tree variation on the spot has been a problem; in 2021 researchers tried scanning the fruit where it was gathered with a handheld infrared instrument, and reported that the device was not adequate for analytical quantification but might serve to separate high- from low-vitamin-C batches.

Other names
English Kakadu plum, billygoat plum, salty plum. (The name "gubinge", said to be used in north-western Australia, does not appear in the sources relied on here — the Kew record and the six citations given — and so is not written in.) It has no established Turkish name; "kakadu eriği" is a descriptive rendering of the English one — it is not related to the plum (*Prunus*). Neither the European Medicines Agency nor the Turkish agency has a monograph for this species.

In short

What is Kakadu plum?

Kakadu plum (Terminalia ferdinandiana Exell) is a plant of the Combretaceae family. Traditions that use it: Oceania. Part used: fruit (fresh, frozen or as a powder).

What is Kakadu plum used for?

Traditional use: A study published in Meat Science in 2023 measured the composition of the fruit powder: total phenolic content was 13.9 grams gallic acid equivalent per 100 grams dry weight, and vitamin C 12.05 grams as L-ascorbic acid plus 0.5 grams as dehydroascorbic acid. Scientific evidence: traditional. In the sources available to us there is no study in humans at all.

Is Kakadu plum safe?

Safety label: generally safe. The fruit is a food and is traditionally eaten in northern Australia; today it is harvested and sold commercially. That said, there is no safety assessment in humans for this species, and the sources relied on here contain no separate assessment for pregnancy, breastfeeding, children or drug interactions. The fruit powder is a concentrated product: in the 2023 study cytotoxicity was measured for food safety purposes and blended products were reported to be less cytotoxic than the pure powder, so the amount of powder is not immaterial. Because the vitamin C content is very high, taking the powder in large amounts over time resembles a supplement rather than a food; the sources relied on here give no upper limit for such use. What is written for haritaki (*Terminalia chebula*) does not belong to this species — it is a separate species in the same genus and its use is quite different.

Traditional use

As a food — a fruit rich in vitamin C

A study published in Meat Science in 2023 measured the composition of the fruit powder: total phenolic content was 13.9 grams gallic acid equivalent per 100 grams dry weight, and vitamin C 12.05 grams as L-ascorbic acid plus 0.5 grams as dehydroascorbic acid. A study published in Molecules the same year assesses the fruit powder as a functional food ingredient and writes that the species is endemic to Australia and is drawing interest for its high vitamin C levels and strong antioxidant properties.

As a natural preservative — a trial measured in meat

The same 2023 study added fruit powder at 0.2–0.8% to raw beef patties and stored them under modified atmosphere packaging at 4 °C for twenty days. In the treated samples lipid oxidation was significantly delayed compared with both the untreated samples and those treated with sodium metabisulphite; 0.2% and 0.4% slowed the microbial growth rate compared with the untreated samples, though sodium metabisulphite had higher antimicrobial activity. This is a food technology trial, not a health claim.

Laboratory measurements and compound isolation

A study published in Molecules in 2023 measured the fruit powder and five products blended with it for vitamin C, total phenolic and ellagic acid content and in antioxidant, antidiabetic and antimicrobial assays; cytotoxicity was also tested for food safety. The blended products were found both less cytotoxic and less active than the pure fruit powder, but in most measurements still above the commercial blueberry powder used as a benchmark. A study published in Plants in 2024 examined the species’ methanolic extract by bioassay-guided fractionation, and for this species the result was negative: the fractions obtained from the flesh extract showed no cytotoxicity except against the HT29 cell line, and only Fraction 2 exhibited some antibacterial activity; the authors attribute this to a potential loss of synergy as the compounds are separated, and write that they did not pursue further fractionation for this species. These are in vitro measurements.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew’s records its natural range covers three regions: the Northern Territory, Queensland and Western Australia. It is a small, open-crowned tree of the tropical savanna belt of northern Australia; the fruit is largely wild-harvested, and the samples in the 2021 study came from the Kimberley region of Western Australia.
Harvest season
The sources relied on here give no collection calendar for Turkey; the plant does not grow there naturally.
Local names
It has no established Turkish local name.

Mix-up risk

It is in the same genus as haritaki (*Terminalia chebula*) but a separate species; one species’ monograph and restrictions do not belong to its relative, and their uses are not the same either — what is used in haritaki is the dried fruit, and it belongs to the Ayurvedic tradition. Although its name says "plum" it is not related to the plum (*Prunus*); they are in separate families.

Preparation

No dose is accepted in an official monograph. The fruit is a food; it is used fresh, frozen or dried as a powder, and the sources relied on here give no measure or upper limit for use in humans. The proportions tested as a preservative in meat (0.2–0.8%) belong to food technology and are not a dietary recommendation. The fruit powder is a concentrated product: in the sources relied on here its cytotoxicity was tested for food safety purposes, and blended products were reported to be less cytotoxic than the pure powder.

What science says

In the sources available to us there is no study in humans at all. The soundest data we have are composition measurements, and they are genuinely striking: the 2023 measurement in Meat Science found 12.05 grams of L-ascorbic acid per 100 grams dry weight of the fruit powder — more than ten per cent of dry weight. The sources relied on here contain no measurement comparing that figure against fruits in general: the only comparison made, in the 2023 study in Molecules, is against a commercial blueberry powder used as a benchmark. So no ranking of the kind "among the richest known fruits" is written into this record; what is written is the measured figure. All the antioxidant, antidiabetic and antimicrobial results are in vitro; they show no effect in any disease. A review published in the Journal of Ethnopharmacology in 2021 mentions the species among selected Australian plants for its antioxidant and possible therapeutic value; it is a review. A study published in Sensors in 2021 reports that a handheld near-infrared instrument could predict vitamin C and ellagic acid in wild-harvested fruit, but that the values obtained were not adequate for analytical quantification and the method can only be used for rapid screening.

Safety

Generally safe

The fruit is a food and is traditionally eaten in northern Australia; today it is harvested and sold commercially. That said, there is no safety assessment in humans for this species, and the sources relied on here contain no separate assessment for pregnancy, breastfeeding, children or drug interactions.
