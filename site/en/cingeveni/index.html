Chinese milkvetch root

In Turkish "geven" names the thorny cushions of the steppe; Anatolia has hundreds of species and the most famous is the source of gum tragacanth. The huángqí in a Chinese pharmacy is a milkvetch too — but two species of northern China. Under one Turkish name stand Anatolia’s gum plant and China’s immune root, and neither can inherit the other’s evidence. Other names
Turkish calls this one geven as well — the Turkish monograph gives its Turkish name as "geven". Chinese huángqí, English astragalus root, milkvetch. The WHO monograph defines the drug as "Radix Astragali", the dried root. ⚠️ The milkvetches of Anatolia are not this plant: Turkey’s four hundred-odd Astragalus species (including A. gummifer, the source of gum tragacanth) fall under neither the WHO nor the Turkish monograph; they have their own record. In short

What is Chinese milkvetch root?

Chinese milkvetch root (Astragalus membranaceus (Fisch.) Bunge; A. mongholicus Bunge) is a plant of the Fabaceae (legume family) family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia. Part used: the dried root.

What is Chinese milkvetch root used for?

Traditional use: "Uses supported by clinical data": none. Scientific evidence: limited. Astragalus’s current evidence lies not where the WHO said "none" in 1999 but in oncology.

Is Chinese milkvetch root safe?

Safety label: caution. The WHO monograph writes "no information available" for contraindications, warnings and adverse reactions — which means not that it is safe but that data were lacking in 1999. Not recommended in pregnancy or lactation; no data in children. As a plant credited with immune effects, people with autoimmune disease and those on immunosuppressants should not use it without asking a doctor — a theoretical but established caution. The roots of Turkey’s wild milkvetches should not be gathered and used: telling the species apart is expert work, and some Astragalus species are poisonous to livestock.

Traditional use

WHO monograph (1999)

"Uses supported by clinical data": none. In pharmacopoeias and traditional systems: adjunctive therapy in colds and influenza; to enhance the immune system and increase stamina; chronic diarrhoea, oedema, abnormal uterine bleeding, diabetes; as a cardiotonic. 9–30 g of root daily. Contraindications, warnings and adverse reactions: "no information available"; not recommended in pregnancy or lactation for lack of data.

Turkey — same name, other species

In Anatolia "geven" means the thorny cushion plants of the steppe; Turkey has more than 400 Astragalus species, and gum tragacanth oozes from the stem of one of them. The huángqí root in a Chinese pharmacy belongs to two northern Chinese species. One Turkish name, quite different plants and uses — and no Turkish monograph for either.

China — a qi tonic

Huángqí is among the most used "qi-strengthening" roots of Chinese medicine, an ingredient of hundreds of formulas and today the raw material of many licensed products in China, injections included. On the Western supplement market it is sold as an "immune" herb.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to northern China, Mongolia and eastern Siberia; widely cultivated in China. It does not grow in Turkey.
Harvest season
Yellowish pea flowers in summer; the root is lifted from 4–5-year-old plants in spring or autumn.
Local names
Geven; Chinese huángqí, English astragalus.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ Anatolia’s milkvetches have their own record — native species such as the tragacanth milkvetch (A. gummifer), A. longifolius and A. muschianus are different plants, outside this monograph, and the part used is not the root but the gum that seeps from the stem. Because the Turkish list gives both the same Turkish name, "geven", the confusion has reached official documents. Root adulteration (other Astragalus and Hedysarum species) is also known on the Chinese market.

Preparation

The WHO monograph gives 9–30 g of dried root daily, orally (decoction). Not recommended in pregnancy or lactation; no data in children. The roots of Turkey’s wild milkvetches are not this monograph’s drug.

What science says

Astragalus’s current evidence lies not where the WHO said "none" in 1999 but in oncology. A systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2025 in Integrative Cancer Therapies pooled 8 randomised trials of astragalus added to treatment in cancer-related fatigue: a large difference in fatigue score (SMD −1.63; 95% CI −1.90 to −1.36) and improved quality of life — but the authors write that the trials were small and of low quality and the result must be read with caution. The other meta-analyses (membranous nephropathy, left-ventricular remodelling in heart failure, polysaccharides with chemoradiotherapy) share the pattern: all in China, all added to standard therapy, most as multi-herb formulas or injections. That evidence cannot be transferred directly to the "immune astragalus" sold as capsules in the West. Evidence level Limited.

Safety

Caution

The WHO monograph writes "no information available" for contraindications, warnings and adverse reactions — which means not that it is safe but that data were lacking in 1999. Not recommended in pregnancy or lactation; no data in children. As a plant credited with immune effects, people with autoimmune disease and those on immunosuppressants should not use it without asking a doctor — a theoretical but established caution. The roots of Turkey’s wild milkvetches should not be gathered and used: telling the species apart is expert work, and some Astragalus species are poisonous to livestock.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No interactions reported in the WHO monographThe monograph gives no interaction information. Theoretical notes: possible opposing action with immunosuppressants (cyclophosphamide, ciclosporin); level changes with lithium and diuretics through a diuretic effect. None shown in a clinical study.
