Chilli pepper

Capsicum annuum L. var. minimum (Miller) Heiser
Evidence: ClinicalCaution

In Turkish, "kırmızı biber" covers both sweet paprika and hot chilli, but what enters the monograph is the small-fruited, hot variety. The distinction is capsaicin — the compound that provides both the heat and the medicinal effect. The heat is not an incidental property here but the active substance itself: a pepper that is not hot has no place in a pain plaster.

Other names
The Turkish regulatory monograph gives the Turkish name as "kırmızı biber" (red pepper) and Capsicum annuum var. annuum as a synonym. The EU monograph covers small-fruited varieties of Capsicum frutescens under the same heading. Called chilli or cayenne in English.

In short

What is Chilli pepper?

Chilli pepper (Capsicum annuum L. var. minimum (Miller) Heiser) is a plant of the Solanaceae (patlıcangiller) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, North America. Part used: the dried ripe fruit.

What is Chilli pepper used for?

Traditional use: The European Medicines Agency adopted a monograph in 2011 and placed the indication under "well-established use": relief of muscle pain such as low back pain. Scientific evidence: clinical. This is the plant with the firmest independent evidence in this batch.

Is Chilli pepper safe?

Safety label: caution. Not for anyone hypersensitive to the active substance or to other sources of capsaicinoids (pepper plants, chilli). Not to be applied to broken skin, wounds or eczema. Safety and efficacy under 18 have not been established. Do not apply near the eyes or to mucous membranes, and do not scratch the application site. The plaster increases local blood flow and causes marked reddening and a sensation of warmth; this is the expected pharmacological action and subsides shortly after removal. Burning, stinging or itching may occur, especially in the first days. Rarely (~1/10,000 to <1/1,000) skin hypersensitivity and allergic reactions (urticaria, blisters at the application site) occur, and treatment must then be stopped immediately. The Turkish monograph additionally asks for caution in ulcer, gastric inflammation and chronic intestinal inflammation, says the powder and extract must not be inhaled, and does not recommend use in people allergic to glove latex because of cross-reactivity. Chronic gastritis, kidney and liver disorders and neurotoxic effects have been reported with prolonged high-dose use. No data in pregnancy; capsaicin crosses the placenta and may pass into breast milk, so use only after a careful risk-benefit assessment. The culinary use of chilli is not the subject of these warnings.

Traditional use

Europe — topical use for muscle pain

The European Medicines Agency adopted a monograph in 2011 and placed the indication under "well-established use": relief of muscle pain such as low back pain. The forms described are medicated plasters and semi-solid preparations — applied to the painful area, not taken by mouth. The active substance is capsaicin, the same compound that makes the fruit hot.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "dried ripe fruits" and adds warnings of its own: avoid in ulcer, gastric inflammation and chronic intestinal inflammation; do not inhale the powder or extract; not recommended for people allergic to glove latex.

Anatolia — in cooking

Chilli is a staple of Anatolian cooking: pul biber flakes, isot, pepper paste. That food use is not the same as the medicinal use above, which involves a standardised preparation applied to the skin.

In nature

Where it grows
Of Central American origin; reached Turkey in the Ottoman period and is now grown across most of the country, above all in the southeast. Wants heat, sun and free-draining soil.
Harvest season
A summer crop; fruits ripen and redden in late summer and autumn, then are dried for storage.
Local names
Pul biber, isot (Şanlıurfa), acı biber, arnavut biberi. The regulatory monograph uses "kırmızı biber".

Mix-up risk

The main risk of confusion is within the species: sweet red pepper and hot chilli are varieties of the same plant, but only the small-fruited hot one enters the medicinal monograph. Pharmacy products state their capsaicin content on the label; kitchen pepper flakes do not make a pain plaster.

Preparation

There is no home preparation for pain; the monograph describes standardised products only. A plaster is adjusted to deliver 22–35 micrograms of capsaicinoids (as capsaicin) per square centimetre; at most one plaster a day, worn for at least 4 and up to 12 hours, with at least 12 hours before a new plaster is applied to the same site. The Turkish monograph sets a tighter frame: avoid applications longer than two days and leave 14 days before a new one; if pain persists, treatment may continue up to three weeks under medical supervision, followed by a break of at least two weeks. Keep away from sun, hot water and heating pads during use — heat intensifies the effect unpredictably, and so does physical activity that causes sweating.

What science says

This is the plant with the firmest independent evidence in this batch. The 2014 Cochrane review of herbal medicine for low-back pain (14 randomised trials, 2,050 participants) reports that Capsicum frutescens cream or plaster "probably produces more favourable results than placebo" in chronic low back pain: three trials, 755 participants, moderate quality evidence. The review’s own conclusion states it plainly: "C. frutescens (cayenne) reduces pain more than placebo." For acute low back pain the picture is unclear — one trial, 40 participants, low quality evidence. So the effect has been shown in chronic pain, not in acute pain. Few other plants in the same review fare as well: low to moderate quality for devil’s claw and willow bark, low for comfrey. Because the regulatory category and the independent review agree here — which is uncommon — the evidence level is Clinical.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Other topical products on the same siteThe monograph states that the plaster or semi-solid form is not intended to be applied at the same site at the same time as other topical products — particularly rubefacients, which increase perfusion, and pain-relieving gels. A detail worth noting: the interaction can persist for up to 12 hours after the plaster has been removed, so removing it is not enough on its own.
Sources of heatNot an interaction in the technical sense, but the same in practice: sun, infrared, heating pads, hot water and sweat-inducing physical activity all intensify the effect of capsaicin. The monograph asks that these be avoided during treatment.
No interaction studies performedThe interaction section of the monograph says so explicitly. The warnings above do not come from research but from how the product works.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Chilli pepper.
