Almond

Prunus dulcis (Mill.) D.A.Webb
Evidence: ClinicalCaution

The sweet almond we eat today is the product of a single broken gene: wild almond species accumulate the bitter, toxic compound amygdalin, and a mutation in one bHLH transcription factor made the kernel sweet — and almond domestication possible.

Other names
Also written with the names Amygdalus communis L. and Prunus amygdalus Batsch; in Turkish ethnobotanical literature Amygdalus communis is the usual one. Kurdish behiv, bıiv, beivf; payam in Mardin.

In short

What is Almond?

Almond (Prunus dulcis (Mill.) D.A.Webb) is a plant of the Rosaceae (rose family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean. Part used: kernel (inside of the seed); leaf and resin locally.

What is Almond used for?

Traditional use: In layer III of Göbekli Tepe, among the remains obtained by floating 1,950 litres of soil, there are shell fragments belonging to the genus Prunus: 158 fragments, corresponding to an estimated 13.5 whole fruits. Scientific evidence: clinical. Almond is among the most trialled nuts for cardiometabolic endpoints.

Is Almond safe?

Safety label: caution. Almond is a tree nut and tree-nut allergy can cause severe, life-threatening reactions. Its main allergen is amandin (prunin); the Pru du 6 protein is used as a marker for almond allergy, and cross-reactivity with other Rosaceae fruits is on record. BITTER ALMOND is a separate matter: wild almond species accumulate the cyanogenic compound amygdalin, which the body converts to cyanide. A case of cyanide poisoning with severe lactic acidosis after eating bitter almonds has been published. Bitter almond and amygdalin (laetrile) should not be used as food or medicine. For sweet almond there is no verified data on drug interactions or on pregnancy and breastfeeding.

Traditional use

Göbekli Tepe

In layer III of Göbekli Tepe, among the remains obtained by floating 1,950 litres of soil, there are shell fragments belonging to the genus Prunus: 158 fragments, corresponding to an estimated 13.5 whole fruits. Part of the charcoal has also been identified as Prunus cf. amygdalus. So what was found is not the domesticated almond but its wild relative; the excavation’s archaeobotanist Reinder Neef writes that there is no evidence of domestication in any of the material. About nine tenths of the charcoal consists of wild pistachio and wild almond; this shows that the vegetation surrounding the hill was an “almond-pistachio steppe forest”.

Şanlıurfa — Suruç

In the same province as Göbekli Tepe, in the villages of Suruç, almond fruit is eaten for diabetes; its local name is “beivf”.

Şırnak — İdil

Fresh almond fruit is used for mouth sores, for eczema and to thicken the hair; its local names are “badem” and “behiv”.

Mardin — non-medicinal use

The resin that seeps from the trunk of almond and eastern almond (Amygdalus orientalis) is used as glue: book pages that have come loose are stuck together with it. The trunk is also valued as firewood.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to western Asia; grown across Turkey, concentrated in the Aegean, Mediterranean and southeast. Drought-hardy but sensitive to late frost.
Harvest season
Flowers in February and March, before the leaves — one of the first trees to bloom in Anatolia. Fruit ripens in August and September; eaten green as “çağla” in May and June.
Local names
Behiv, bıiv, beivf, payam

Mix-up risk

Bitter and sweet almond are two forms of the same species and cannot be told apart by eye; the difference is taste and amygdalin content. Eastern almond (Amygdalus orientalis) and wild almond species grow naturally in Anatolia and may share the same local name. The Göbekli Tepe remains are recorded as “Prunus sp.” and “Prunus cf. amygdalus”: wild, and not settled at species level.

Preparation

There is no official preparation: neither a European Medicines Agency monograph nor an NCCIH page exists. Clinical trials use roughly 30–60 g a day of raw or roasted sweet almonds — a food amount, not a drug dose.

What science says

Almond is among the most trialled nuts for cardiometabolic endpoints. Meta-analyses of randomised trials find that almonds lower LDL and total cholesterol, barely affect triglycerides and leave HDL unchanged. Human trials also report that almonds eaten before a meal lower post-meal blood glucose in prediabetes, and that they measurably affect gut transit and the gut microbiota. All of this is evidence for almonds as a food; there is no approved medical indication.

Safety

Caution

Almond is a tree nut and tree-nut allergy can cause severe, life-threatening reactions. Its main allergen is amandin (prunin); the Pru du 6 protein is used as a marker for almond allergy, and cross-reactivity with other Rosaceae fruits is on record. BITTER ALMOND is a separate matter: wild almond species accumulate the cyanogenic compound amygdalin, which the body converts to cyanide. A case of cyanide poisoning with severe lactic acidosis after eating bitter almonds has been published. Bitter almond and amygdalin (laetrile) should not be used as food or medicine. For sweet almond there is no verified data on drug interactions or on pregnancy and breastfeeding.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No known drug interactionNo verified interaction study exists for sweet almond, and none has been reported at food amounts. Bitter almond and amygdalin (laetrile) products are a different matter: they carry a risk of cyanide poisoning and should not be taken at all.
