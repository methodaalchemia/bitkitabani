Catnip

Nepeta cataria L.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

A paper published in the journal Science in 1964 proposed that nepetalactone repels insects and that its real function in the plant is not to attract cats but to protect it from plant-eating insects. According to a 2020 genome study, iridoid production was lost in the ancestors of this plant and evolved again in the Nepeta lineage.

Other names
English catnip, catmint. The volatile compound that attracts cats is nepetalactone. According to Kew’s plant list its native range runs from southern and eastern Europe through Türkiye, the Caucasus, Iran and Central Asia to the Himalaya, China, Korea and Japan; it was later carried to North America and to central and northern Europe. There is no EMA, WHO or TİTCK monograph.

In short

What is Catnip?

Catnip (Nepeta cataria L.) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: Europe, Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Asia and Siberia. Part used: leaves and flowering tops; the essential oil.

What is Catnip used for?

Traditional use: A 1995 pharmacognosy study (Sarkar et al.) writes that the leaves and flowering tops are regarded as carminative, diaphoretic, tonic and emmenagogue, and are used in infantile colic and nervous complaints. Scientific evidence: limited. There are no clinical studies of catnip in humans; a 2018 review (Süntar et al.) notes that the findings in the genus Nepeta remain at the preclinical level and that clinical studies are needed.

Is Catnip safe?

Safety label: caution. Its safety in humans has not been studied systematically. ⚠️ It should not be given to infants or small children: in the 1997 case report a toddler who had eaten a large quantity of catnip showed obtundation and central nervous system depression that could not be explained by any other cause. Sarkar and colleagues write that the plant is regarded as an emmenagogue; it should not be used in pregnancy, and there are no data for breastfeeding. In mouse studies it increased susceptibility to seizures and altered barbiturate sleep; people with epilepsy and those taking sedatives or sleeping medicines should not use it without consulting their doctor. The lethal dose (LD50) of the essential oil in mice was 1300 mg/kg; the essential oil is not taken by mouth. If drowsiness or a change in consciousness follows swallowing a large amount, call 114 (the National Poison Information Centre) or 112.

Traditional use

Europe and south-west Asia — a herb for colic and cough

A 1995 pharmacognosy study (Sarkar et al.) writes that the leaves and flowering tops are regarded as carminative, diaphoretic, tonic and emmenagogue, and are used in infantile colic and nervous complaints. A 2009 study (Gilani et al.) notes that the plant is traditionally used for colic, diarrhoea, cough and asthma. These are traditional uses; use in infants is not recommended today.

The Himalaya, Iran and Türkiye — the genus in folk medicine

A 2021 review (Sharma et al.) reports that Nepeta species are widely used in the Himalayan parts of India, Pakistan and Nepal, in China and in the mountainous regions of Türkiye and Iran for colds, cough, asthma, stomach disorders and febrile illnesses. This information is for the genus as a whole; not every use belongs to catnip.

Cats and insects — the plant’s real fame

Catnip is best known for the response it produces in cats: they rub their faces and heads against the plant and roll on the ground. A 2021 study (Uenoyama et al.) showed that this behaviour transfers the plant’s iridoids onto the cat’s fur and protects the cat from mosquito bites. The plant is widely used in pet toys. A 2023 review lists catnip oil among the insect-repellent ingredients registered with the US Environmental Protection Agency.

⚠️ The claim of recreational use

In 1969 the medical journal JAMA published a paper suggesting that catnip alters consciousness. A 1997 case report (Osterhoudt et al.) writes that this claim is controversial and that it remains uncertain whether the plant affects human consciousness. The same paper reported central nervous system depression in a toddler who had eaten a large quantity of catnip.

In nature

Where it grows
A perennial herb; its stem is hollow and its leaves are deltoid-oval and hairy. Its native range is southern and eastern Europe, Türkiye, the Caucasus, Iran, Central Asia and the Himalaya; it is widely naturalised in North America.
Harvest season
Flowers in summer; the leaves and flowering tops are gathered during flowering.
Local names
No established local name in Turkish.

Mix-up risk

Most of the ornamental plants sold in gardens as “catmint” are other Nepeta species and hybrids. Silver vine (Actinidia polygama), which produces a similar response in cats, is a separate plant from a different family.

Preparation

The traditional form is an infusion (tea) of the dried leaves and flowering tops. Because there is no EMA, WHO or TİTCK monograph, there is no accepted dose, and no dose-finding study has been carried out in humans. The essential oil is not taken by mouth. This page deliberately gives no recipe for smoking the plant like tobacco or using it recreationally; the effect of such use is unproven and its safety is unknown.

What science says

There are no clinical studies of catnip in humans; a 2018 review (Süntar et al.) notes that the findings in the genus Nepeta remain at the preclinical level and that clinical studies are needed. In the 2009 study by Gilani and colleagues the essential oil relaxed contractions in rabbit intestine and guinea-pig trachea tissues; the authors attributed this to calcium-channel and phosphodiesterase inhibition. The animal behaviour studies do not agree with one another: in a 1978 study the essential oil (500 mg/kg) prolonged barbiturate sleep in mice, whereas in a 1995 study the plant, added to the feed at 10%, increased activity in mice, shortened barbiturate sleep and raised susceptibility to seizures. In a 2009 laboratory study carried out in Türkiye the main constituent of the oil was nepetalactone at 70.4%, and the oil was active in the test tube against eleven bacteria. The composition of the essential oil varies greatly with the source: in Gilani’s sample the main constituent was 1,8-cineole at 21.00%. Evidence rating "limited": laboratory and animal data only.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Sedatives, sleeping medicines and alcoholIn the 1978 mouse study the essential oil prolonged barbiturate sleep; an increase in sedative effect is possible. There is no interaction study in humans.
Epilepsy medicinesIn the 1995 mouse study the plant increased susceptibility to seizures induced by picrotoxin and strychnine. People with a history of seizures should not use it.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Catnip.
