BT-193 · Gentianaceae (gentian family)

Common centaury

Centaurium erythraea Rafn
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

In Turkish, "kantaron" names two entirely different plants that are not even related. Yellow-flowered St John’s wort (Hypericum perforatum) is used for depression and interacts seriously with many medicines; common centaury (Centaurium erythraea) is a bitter used for the stomach, with no interactions reported in its monograph at all. Two plants sharing a name and differing this much in safety profile is one of the clearest arguments for why the Latin name is what settles things.

Other names
The TİTCK monograph gives the Latin name in the broad sense: Centaurium erythraea Rafn s. l., including C. majus and C. suffruticosum; its synonyms are Erythraea centaurium, C. umbellatum, C. minus. In English centaury. ⚠️ It is not even related to kantaron (Hypericum perforatum) in our catalog: that one is from the Hypericaceae family, this one from the Gentianaceae.

What is Common centaury?

Common centaury (Centaurium erythraea Rafn) is a plant of the Gentianaceae (gentian family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the aerial parts.

What is Common centaury used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": mild dyspeptic and gastrointestinal complaints and/or temporary loss of appetite. Scientific evidence: traditional. The most honest sentence in this record is this: there is no clinical trial of common centaury in humans.

Is Common centaury safe?

Safety label: caution. Contraindicated in people with peptic ulcer — both the EU and the Turkish monograph say so, and the reason is direct: the plant works by stimulating gastric secretion through its bitterness, which is an intervention in the wrong direction on an open ulcer. Not for anyone hypersensitive to the active substance. Not to be used under 18 — a higher age limit than usual for this kind of plant. If symptoms do not resolve over two weeks, or if adverse effects appear, consult a doctor. Not recommended in pregnancy or lactation for lack of data. Tinctures and extracts may contain ethanol, so read the label. The Turkish monograph requires supervision by a doctor or pharmacist trained in herbal treatment.

Traditional use

Europe — the bitter principle

The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": mild dyspeptic and gastrointestinal complaints and/or temporary loss of appetite. This is the classic use of the European pharmacopoeia’s class of bitter drugs (amara): the bitter taste stimulates digestive secretion and so opens the appetite.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the aerial parts" and states the key limit plainly: contraindicated in people with peptic ulcer. Not used under 18. If symptoms do not resolve over two weeks, consult a doctor.

Anatolia

Common centaury grows widely along field margins and in meadows in Turkey, recognisable by its pink-red star-shaped flowers. A tea is made from it for the stomach in folk use, matching the monograph’s indication.

In nature

Where it grows
Meadows, field margins and clearings of Europe, North Africa and western Asia. Common in Turkey.
Harvest season
Between June and August it opens pink-red, star-shaped flowers; the aerial part is gathered during flowering.
Local names
Kırmızı kantaron — the Turkish monograph gives one Turkish name.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ It is not the same thing as kantaron (Hypericum perforatum) in our catalog, and not even related to it. Yellow St John’s wort has yellow flowers, and its leaves look dotted when held up to the light; red centaury’s flower is pink-red and star-shaped. Their uses and interaction profiles are entirely different.

Preparation

The monograph describes oral preparations of the aerial parts; as is usual for bitter drugs, they are taken shortly before meals. Tinctures and extracts containing ethanol must carry the appropriate labelling. If symptoms do not resolve over two weeks, consult a doctor. Not used under 18.

What science says

The most honest sentence in this record is this: there is no clinical trial of common centaury in humans. Every recent publication on the plant in PubMed is laboratory work — chemical profiling, antioxidant and antibacterial measurements in cell culture, animal models, trials of ointment formulations. These show what is in the plant and what it does under laboratory conditions; they do not show whether it works in a patient. The distance between laboratory activity and clinical benefit is the one most often skipped in phytotherapy, and it is not skipped here. The EU monograph rests the indication on long use in any case; in its own words, "exclusively based upon long-standing use and experience". The one known mechanism is plausible and old: the plant’s bitter compounds (secoiridoid glycosides) stimulate digestive secretion through taste, which is where the appetite use of bitter drugs comes from. That mechanism also explains the Turkish monograph’s peptic-ulcer contraindication: something that increases gastric secretion is no good for an open ulcer. The evidence level is Traditional.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No drug interactions reported in either monographBoth the EU and the Turkish monograph leave the interaction section empty. "None reported" is not evidence of no interaction; it means not studied — and given that no clinical trial of this plant in humans exists, that is hardly surprising.
Medicines that act on stomach acidThere is no interaction study; the issue here comes from the mechanism. The plant is used to stimulate gastric secretion, while acid-suppressing medicines do the opposite. Anyone using both would do well to mention it to their doctor.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Common centaury.
