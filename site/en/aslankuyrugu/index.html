Motherwort

Leonurus cardiaca L.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

The plant’s two names carry two different traditional uses. The Latin species name *cardiaca* points to the heart, and the EU monograph’s second indication is still there: nervous palpitations. The English name "motherwort" comes from an old use around childbirth and the lying-in period — and the modern monograph puts its sharpest warning exactly there: contraindicated in pregnancy. One of the plant’s names still points to a valid indication; the other now points to a prohibition.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the name "aslan kuyruğu" (lion’s tail); the synonyms are Cardiaca crispa, Lamium cardiaca and Leonurus aconitifolius. Both the Latin species name (*cardiaca*) and the English name carry a traditional use: the heart, and childbirth.

In short

What is Motherwort?

Motherwort (Leonurus cardiaca L.) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (ballıbabagiller) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the aerial parts.

What is Motherwort used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places both indications under "traditional use": (1) relief of symptoms of nervous tension; (2) relief of symptoms of nervous cardiac complaints such as palpitations, after serious conditions have been excluded by a medical doctor. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no randomised clinical trial of motherwort on its own.

Is Motherwort safe?

Safety label: caution. Contraindicated in pregnancy. This is an important distinction in the catalog: for most plants the EU monograph says "not recommended for lack of data", while for this one it says outright "not to be used in pregnancy". Safety during lactation has not been established. Driving and operating machinery: the EU monograph states the plant may impair that ability and that affected patients should not drive or operate machinery — a warning that is also uncommon in this catalog. Hypersensitivity to the active substance can occur. Not recommended under 18. Consult a doctor if symptoms have not resolved after four weeks. For the palpitations indication, do not forget the EU monograph’s condition: after serious conditions have been excluded by a medical doctor. Managing palpitations with a herbal product can delay a heart problem underneath being found.

Traditional use

Europe — two indications

The EMA monograph places both indications under "traditional use": (1) relief of symptoms of nervous tension; (2) relief of symptoms of nervous cardiac complaints such as palpitations, after serious conditions have been excluded by a medical doctor. That condition matters especially here: palpitations can also belong to a serious heart problem.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the aerial parts". Not recommended under 18, nor in pregnancy or lactation; consult a doctor if symptoms have not resolved after four weeks.

Anatolia

Motherwort grows along roadsides, under hedges and on waste ground in northern and central Turkey, recognisable by its deeply cut leaves and the whorls of pinkish flowers in the leaf axils.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to Europe and central Asia; roadsides, hedge bottoms and waste ground. Found in northern and central Turkey.
Harvest season
Whorls of pinkish flowers in the leaf axils from June to September; the aerial parts are gathered in flower.
Local names
Aslan kuyruğu — the Turkish monograph gives one Turkish name.

Mix-up risk

Confused with deadnettles (Lamium) of the same family — indeed one of the plant’s old names is Lamium cardiaca. The distinguishing feature is the leaf: motherwort’s are deeply cut with pointed lobes, deadnettle’s heart-shaped and toothed. The Turkish name "aslan kuyruğu" is also used for Leonotis leonurus, grown as an ornamental, which is a different genus.

Preparation

The monograph describes oral preparations of the aerial parts. Tinctures and liquid extracts must carry the appropriate ethanol labelling. Consult a doctor if symptoms have not resolved after four weeks or if they worsen. Not recommended under 18. Not used in pregnancy.

What science says

There is no randomised clinical trial of motherwort on its own. All the recent literature is at laboratory level: the structure of phenolic and iridoid glycosides, effects on subtypes of the GABA-A receptor, nanoparticles made from its polysaccharides, antioxidant and genoprotective measurements. One of these offers an interesting hint — that some of the plant’s compounds act on GABA-A receptor subtypes would fit the nervous-tension indication — but that is a cell-level finding and does not show that it reduces tension in a patient. The EU monograph rests both indications on long use. The evidence level is Traditional. The real information in this record is on the safety side: the EU monograph contraindicates the plant outright in pregnancy and states that it may impair the ability to drive.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Cardiac glycosides (the digitalis group)The Turkish monograph states it may interact with cardiac glycosides. That group — digoxin and the like — works within a very narrow range of blood levels, where a small change can have serious consequences. And because the plant’s indication concerns the heart, the two meeting in the same person is not a remote possibility: anyone on heart medication must tell their doctor.
Blood-thinning medicinesThe Turkish monograph states it may interact with anticoagulants.
Sedatives and alcohol (not an interaction but a caution)Not a reported interaction, but the EU monograph states the plant may impair the ability to drive and operate machinery. Adding a medicine or alcohol that acts the same way can deepen that effect.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing; the Turkish one reports two drug groups.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Motherwort.
