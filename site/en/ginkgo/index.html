Ginkgo

Ginkgo biloba
Evidence: LimitedCautionRare in the wild

Ginkgo is the only living member of a 200-million-year-old lineage; trees from the age of the dinosaurs are called “living fossils”.

Other names
Maidenhair tree, yinxing
Part used
Leaves, seeds
Region
Asia and Siberia
Use
Healing, Food, Ritual and shamanic
In short

What is Ginkgo?

Ginkgo (Ginkgo biloba) is a plant of the Ginkgoaceae family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia. Part used: leaves, seeds.

What is Ginkgo used for?

Traditional use: A sacred tree planted in temple gardens. Scientific evidence: limited. The EMA recognises standardised ginkgo leaf extract for mild age-related cognitive decline.

Is Ginkgo safe?

Safety label: caution. Can increase bleeding risk; stop before surgery. Raw or large amounts of seeds are poisonous and can cause seizures. People with seizures should be careful.

Traditional use

China

A sacred tree planted in temple gardens. The roasted seed is eaten for coughs and asthma; leaf use is more recent.

Japan

Ginkgo trees that survived the atomic bomb in Hiroshima are symbols of peace and resilience.

In nature

Where it grows
Grown as an ornamental in parks and streets in Türkiye; not found wild.
Harvest season
Leaves are picked in late summer, before they yellow.
Local names
ginkgo, mabet ağacı, yelpaze ağacı

Mix-up risk

The fruit-like seed coats of female trees smell bad and irritate the skin.

Preparation

The form used is a standardised leaf extract (such as EGb 761). Home tea from the leaves is not recommended.

What science says

The EMA recognises standardised ginkgo leaf extract for mild age-related cognitive decline. But a large US trial (GEM) showed ginkgo did not prevent dementia in older people. Evidence for tinnitus and circulation claims is weak.

Safety

Caution

Can increase bleeding risk; stop before surgery. Raw or large amounts of seeds are poisonous and can cause seizures. People with seizures should be careful.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Blood thinners and antiplatelet drugs (warfarin, aspirin, clopidogrel)May increase bleeding risk.
Seizure medicinesGinkgotoxin in the seeds may lower the seizure threshold.
Efavirenz and some HIV drugsReported to lower drug levels.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Ginkgo.

Conservation

IUCN
Endangered (EN), wild populations
Note
Wild ginkgo survives only in a few places in China; almost every ginkgo in the world was planted by people.
Alternative
Commercial leaves come from plantations.
