Thyme or sage? For cough and sore throat

Thyme and sage are the two herbs most often brewed for colds in Anatolia. Both are in the Lamiaceae family and contain strongly scented essential oils.

Key differences

  • The European Medicines Agency recognises thyme for productive cough and sage for mouth and throat inflammation, both as traditional uses.
  • Thyme’s thymol and carvacrol show strong antimicrobial effects in the lab.
  • Because sage contains thujone, it should not be drunk for long periods or in large amounts. Pure thyme oil burns; it must not be applied to the skin undiluted.

Which one, when?

For a productive cough, thyme tea; for a sore throat, a sage gargle.

What the science says

Thyme and oregano: Contains thymol and carvacrol, whose strong antimicrobial effect has been shown in the lab. The EMA recognises thyme (Thymus vulgaris) as a traditional herbal medicine for productive cough.

Greek sage: The EMA recognises sage (Salvia officinalis) for mouth and throat inflammation and excessive sweating as traditional uses. The Anatolian species is rich in cineole and usually contains less thujone.

Safety

Thyme and oregano: Safe in culinary amounts. Pure thyme oil is caustic; it should not be applied undiluted or swallowed.

Greek sage: Because it contains thujone, it should not be used long-term or in high doses. Not recommended in pregnancy, breastfeeding or epilepsy.

Drug interactions

Greek sage: Seizure medicines (antiepileptics) (Thujone can lower the seizure threshold and weaken the drug at high doses); Diabetes medicines (Can lower blood sugar; risk of hypoglycaemia with the drug); Sedatives and sleeping pills (May increase the effect).
