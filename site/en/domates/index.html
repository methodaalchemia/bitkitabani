In short

What is Tomato?

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) is a plant of the Solanaceae (nightshade family) family. Traditions that use it: Andes, North America. Part used: ripe fruit.

What is Tomato used for?

Traditional use: A 2015 study in BMC Genomics examined 1,008 tomato accessions and found that fruit weight was strongly selected in the semi-domesticated form in the Andean region of Ecuador and northern Peru before domestication in Mesoamerica. Scientific evidence: limited. The tomato has two separate scientific files, and they do not carry equal weight.

Is Tomato safe?

Safety label: generally safe. The ripe tomato is a common and well-tolerated food. Its leaves and unripe green fruit carry α-tomatine and are not the subject of this page; the leaves are not eaten. EFSA reports no accounts of acute toxicity in humans from eating green tomatoes, but could not characterise the risk of tomato glycoalkaloids for lack of data — that is a statement of a gap, not a declaration of safety. The claim that "nightshades aggravate rheumatic inflammation" has no randomised evidence in humans: only the protocol of the first randomised trial was published in 2024 (40 participants, 8 weeks) and no results exist yet. On the interventional side, a 2017 meta-analysis had found that tomato supplementation lowered IL-6, but that result was later superseded: a 2022 meta-analysis pooling seven randomised trials found no significant effect on IL-6, and a 2023 review of 34 studies found no significant effect on inflammatory markers including IL-6. But that same 2022 meta-analysis carries one further result, and it runs against the claim: across the same seven trials tomato intake significantly reduced TNF-α (Hedges’ g = −0.45; 95% CI −0.76 to −0.13; p = 0.005), while there was no significant effect on CRP. The interventional data, in other words, points not for but — on one marker — against the claim that "nightshades cause inflammation". Tomato allergy, mostly via profilin and lipid transfer protein, is real and is a separate matter. Safety level "low".

Traditional use

The Andean coast — the wild ancestor and the first selection

A 2015 study in BMC Genomics examined 1,008 tomato accessions and found that fruit weight was strongly selected in the semi-domesticated form in the Andean region of Ecuador and northern Peru before domestication in Mesoamerica. The plant’s first reshaping by human hands happened on the Andean coastal belt.

Mesoamerica — full domestication

A 2020 study in Molecular Biology and Evolution describes the process in two steps: wild *S. pimpinellifolium* → semi-domesticated *var. cerasiforme* in South America → the fully domesticated tomato in Mesoamerica. The authors also suggest that many domestication traits arose in South America, were lost as these forms spread north, and were then reselected in Mesoamerica.

In nature

Where it grows
Kew records a range of 150 regions, a figure that includes naturalisation, since the species is grown worldwide. Its wild ancestor *S. pimpinellifolium* belongs to the Andean coastal belt of Ecuador and northern Peru.
Harvest season
In Türkiye, open-field harvest runs from summer into autumn; under cover, all year.
Local names
domates, firenk, banadura

Mix-up risk

Leaves and unripe green fruit are not treated as the ripe fruit. The red berries of ornamental and wild nightshades should not be taken for tomatoes; black nightshade and woody nightshade belong to the same family but are different species.

Preparation

The tomato is a food; no official monograph sets an accepted dose and this page gives no measure of use. What has been measured in humans is lycopene uptake: heat processing increases absorption. The separate contribution of fat has not been isolated in the underlying studies. Serum lycopene did not rise on unprocessed tomato juice, but did on juice boiled for an hour with corn oil. Tomato paste gave 2.5-fold higher peak concentrations and 3.8-fold higher area under the curve than fresh tomatoes — and since both arms received the same amount of oil, that difference is one of matrix and heat processing, not fat. Unripe green fruit is not the subject of this page.

What science says

The tomato has two separate scientific files, and they do not carry equal weight. The lycopene side is weak: a 2011 Cochrane review found only three randomised trials (154 participants in total) for prostate cancer prevention, judged two of them at high risk of bias, found no significant difference in PSA, and concluded there was insufficient evidence either to support or to refute its use. A 2021 meta-analysis of six trials likewise found no significant effect on circulating PSA, with a reduction only in the subgroup of patients with higher baseline PSA. The absorption side is sounder, but the class of evidence has to be stated as it is: the effect of heat processing has been measured in humans, yet the underlying studies are small single-dose bioavailability studies — the 1992 study is not even indexed in PubMed as a randomised trial, and the 1997 one is indexed as a "clinical trial", not as a randomised controlled trial. The contribution of fat has not been isolated in any of the underlying studies: in the 1997 study both arms received the same amount of oil, while in the 1992 study oil was added only to the processed arm, so heat and fat are confounded. So we cannot conclude that tomatoes cure a disease; we can say "we absorb lycopene better when it is cooked". The α-tomatine of unripe green fruit falls as the fruit ripens; EFSA’s 2020 assessment reports no accounts of acute toxicity in humans from eating green tomatoes — but the same body could not carry out a risk characterisation for tomato glycoalkaloids for lack of data, which means "not assessed", not "safe". Evidence level "limited".
