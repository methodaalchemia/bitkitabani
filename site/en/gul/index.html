Damask rose

Rosa damascena
Evidence: LimitedGenerally safe

It takes tonnes of petals to make a kilogram of rose oil, which is why it is among the most expensive essential oils. Isparta and Bulgaria’s Kazanlak region are the world’s main producers of rose oil.

What is Damask rose?

Damask rose (Rosa damascena) is a plant of the Rosaceae (gülgiller) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Asia and Siberia. Part used: petals (flower).

Traditional use

Anatolia

In Isparta and Burdur, flowers picked early in the morning in May–June are distilled into rose oil and rose water. Rose water flavours sweets such as güllaç and Turkish delight; rose jam is made at home.

Iran and the Middle East

Rose-water distillation (golabgiri) is an old tradition in Iran; rose water is used in religious ceremonies, in cooking and for eye and skin care. In Islamic culture and Ottoman poetry the rose is the symbol of love and beauty.

In nature

Where it grows
Grown in gardens around Isparta and Burdur in the Turkish Lakes Region; a drought-tolerant shrub.
Harvest season
Flowers are picked from late May into June, before the sun is high; the harvest lasts only three or four weeks.
Local names
yağ gülü, Isparta gülü

Mix-up risk

Most garden roses come from other species and hybrids; their scent is weak and they are not used for oil or rose water. Rose hips come from the dog rose (Rosa canina).

Preparation

A dessertspoon of dried petals is steeped in a cup of hot water for 5–10 minutes and used as a gargle for mouth and throat or as a compress on the skin.

What science says

The EMA recognises rose petals (Rosa gallica, R. centifolia and R. damascena) for traditional use to relieve mild inflammation of the mouth, throat and skin. A systematic review (2017) of 12 clinical trials found damask rose promising for pain relief but said it needs confirming with standardised products. A Cochrane review (2016), based on a single trial, found no consistent difference between damask rose and painkillers for period pain and rated the evidence low quality.

Safety

Generally safe

Rose water, jam and tea are safe in food amounts. Rose oil is highly concentrated: do not apply it undiluted or swallow it. The EMA does not recommend use under 12 because of lack of data; safety in pregnancy and breastfeeding is unknown. See a doctor if symptoms last more than a week.
