Lemon verbena

Aloysia citrodora Paláu (syn. Lippia citriodora Kunth)
Evidence: LimitedHigh risk

In Turkish the name "limon otu" has been pinned on two different plants: lemon balm (Melissa officinalis, mint family) and lemon verbena (Aloysia citrodora, verbena family). Both smell of lemon, both have EU monographs for sleep and digestion — but they are two plants from separate families and separate continents. A 90-day trial tests the monograph’s "aid sleep" indication directly; seeing a single-ingredient RCT in the exact indication of a traditional-use plant is rare in this catalog.

Other names
Turkish limon verbena, limon otu (a name also used for lemon balm, so open to confusion); synonyms Aloysia triphylla (L’Hér.) Kuntze, Verbena triphylla L’Hér., Lippia citriodora Kunth. In English: lemon verbena. There is no Turkish monograph; the record rests on the EU monograph and PubMed.

In short

What is Lemon verbena?

Lemon verbena (Aloysia citrodora Paláu (syn. Lippia citriodora Kunth)) is a plant of the Verbenaceae (verbena family) family. Traditions that use it: Andes. Part used: the leaves.

What is Lemon verbena used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places both under "traditional use": (1) relief of mild symptoms of mental stress and to aid sleep; (2) mild gastrointestinal complaints including bloating and flatulence. Scientific evidence: limited. Lemon verbena has a single-ingredient, placebo-controlled randomised trial in the monograph indication — aiding sleep.

Is Lemon verbena safe?

Safety label: high risk. It may impair driving and the use of machines — the EU monograph says affected patients should not drive or operate machinery; an expected warning for a sleep aid. Not for anyone hypersensitive to the verbena family. Use in children not established; not recommended in pregnancy or lactation. No adverse effects known; none reported in the 90-day trial either. A safe tea plant at kitchen doses; insomnia lasting longer than two weeks deserves to have its cause investigated.

Traditional use

Europe — two indications

The EMA monograph places both under "traditional use": (1) relief of mild symptoms of mental stress and to aid sleep; (2) mild gastrointestinal complaints including bloating and flatulence. For sleep, 1–2 g of leaf half an hour before bedtime. The monograph states it may impair driving and the use of machines.

Turkey

There is no monograph for lemon verbena on the Turkish list. It is grown in Turkey as a garden and pot plant and its leaf drunk as tea. Because it carries the same scent as lemon balm, the name "limon otu" is pinned on both.

The Andes — South America

The plant is native to Argentina, Chile, Peru and Uruguay; the Spanish brought it to Europe in the 17th century. In South America and Spain (hierba luisa) it is drunk as an after-dinner digestive tea; the monograph’s second indication corresponds to that tradition.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to the Andean belt of South America; a garden plant in Mediterranean countries and Turkey, frost-tender.
Harvest season
Pale purple flower sprays in summer; the leaves are gathered before and during flowering and keep their scent when dried.
Local names
Limon verbena, limon otu; Spanish hierba luisa, cedrón.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ The name "limon otu" also covers lemon balm; and lemongrass (Cymbopogon) is an entirely different grass. All three smell of lemon; the monograph is for Aloysia citrodora only.

Preparation

For tension: 5 g of comminuted leaf decocted in 100 ml of boiling water, three times daily. For sleep: 1–2 g of leaf infused in 200 ml of boiling water, half an hour before bedtime. For digestion: 2–3 g in 200 ml 1–3 times daily, or 1 g up to five times daily. See a doctor if symptoms last longer than two weeks. Use in children not established.

What science says

Lemon verbena has a single-ingredient, placebo-controlled randomised trial in the monograph indication — aiding sleep. A double-blind study published in 2024 in Nutrients gave 71 healthy adults with sleep problems a lemon verbena extract (33) or placebo (38) for 90 days: differences favoured the extract on a visual sleep-quality scale (6.5 vs 5.5), on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (total score, sleep latency, sleep efficiency 84.5% vs 79.8%) and on actigraphy; nocturnal melatonin was also higher in the extract group. That is a rare study matching the monograph indication exactly. But one trial, 71 people, an extract (not a tea) and an industry-linked team — the evidence level is Limited. The other human studies concern a polyphenol extract combined with hibiscus (appetite, weight); that mixture’s data cannot be attributed to this plant alone.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing; there is no Turkish monograph. As with any plant acting in the same direction as sedatives and alcohol, the possibility of added drowsiness should be kept in mind — not in the monograph, a theoretical note.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Lemon verbena.
