Neem

Azadirachta indica
Evidence: LimitedCaution

A European patent on the antifungal use of neem oil was revoked in 2000 because it rested on knowledge known in India for centuries; the case became a symbol of the “biopiracy” debate.

Other names
Margosa, Indian lilac, nim
Part used
Leaf, twig, seed oil
Region
Asia and Siberia
Use
Healing, Ritual and shamanic
In short

What is Neem?

Neem (Azadirachta indica) is a plant of the Meliaceae family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia. Part used: leaf, twig, seed oil.

What is Neem used for?

Traditional use: Twigs are chewed as toothbrushes and the leaves are used for skin diseases and against lice; in villages the tree’s shade is thought to be healing. Scientific evidence: limited. Neem leaf and oil show antibacterial, antifungal and insect-repellent effects in the lab; azadirachtin from the seeds is one of the world’s most used natural pesticides.

Is Neem safe?

Safety label: caution. Neem oil must not be swallowed: in babies and young children even a few teaspoons have caused vomiting, seizures, coma and death. Long-term use of leaf extract may harm the liver and kidneys. Not for pregnant women or those trying to conceive; neem has traditionally been used as a contraceptive.

Traditional use

India

Twigs are chewed as toothbrushes and the leaves are used for skin diseases and against lice; in villages the tree’s shade is thought to be healing.

Festival in south India

At the Ugadi new-year festival bitter neem flowers are eaten mixed with jaggery, a sign of accepting the bitter and the sweet of life together.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to the dry tropics of the Indian subcontinent; spread to Africa and the Middle East. Does not grow wild in Türkiye.
Harvest season
Evergreen; flowers in spring, fruit ripens in midsummer.
Local names
neem, nim

Mix-up risk

The chinaberry (Melia azedarach) is in the same family and is a park tree in Türkiye; its fruits are poisonous and must not be taken for neem.

Preparation

A fresh twig tip is chewed like a toothbrush. Leaves are boiled and the water applied to skin and hair. The seed oil is for external use only.

What science says

Neem leaf and oil show antibacterial, antifungal and insect-repellent effects in the lab; azadirachtin from the seeds is one of the world’s most used natural pesticides. Small human studies on gum inflammation and lice are promising. Claims for diabetes and cancer are unproven.

Safety

Caution

Neem oil must not be swallowed: in babies and young children even a few teaspoons have caused vomiting, seizures, coma and death. Long-term use of leaf extract may harm the liver and kidneys. Not for pregnant women or those trying to conceive; neem has traditionally been used as a contraceptive.
