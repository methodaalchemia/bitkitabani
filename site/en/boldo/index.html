Boldo

Peumus boldus Molina
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

Boldo is one of the plants in this catalog that shows a distinction most clearly: two preparations of the same plant are not the same thing where safety is concerned. According to the Turkish monograph, the toxic compound ascaridole is not present in aqueous preparations but can pass into ethanolic products. So boldo tea and boldo tincture are different things, even with the same Latin name on the label. A good illustration of how little the word "herbal" says on its own: same plant, different solvent, different risk.

In short

What is Boldo?

Boldo (Peumus boldus Molina) is a plant of the Monimiaceae family. Traditions that use it: Andes. Part used: the leaf.

What is Boldo used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": symptomatic relief of dyspepsia and mild spasmodic disorders of the gastrointestinal tract. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no randomised clinical trial of boldo on its own.

Is Boldo safe?

Safety label: caution. The warning list here is among the longest in this catalog, and all of it comes from the monographs. Not to be used in cholangitis, serious liver disease, bile duct obstruction, gallstones or other biliary disorders requiring medical supervision. Hypersensitivity (anaphylaxis) has been reported; the frequency is unknown — a report of anaphylaxis is uncommon in herbal monographs and should be taken seriously. Very high doses can cause vomiting and spasms. Not to be used longer than four weeks in mild liver and biliary disorders. Use should be stopped at least two weeks before surgery. Ascaridole: the plant contains this toxic compound and the Turkish monograph requires its level in the product to be determined; according to the monograph ascaridole is not present in aqueous preparations but can pass into ethanolic ones — so a tea and an alcoholic extract are not the same thing for this plant. Use under 18 has not been established. Not recommended in pregnancy or lactation; the EU monograph bases that on pre-clinical safety data. If symptoms last more than two weeks, consult a doctor or pharmacist.

Traditional use

Europe — dyspepsia and spasm

The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": symptomatic relief of dyspepsia and mild spasmodic disorders of the gastrointestinal tract.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the leaves" and carries one of the longest warning lists in this catalog: not to be used in cholangitis, serious liver disease, bile duct obstruction or gallstones; anaphylaxis has been reported; not to be used longer than four weeks in mild liver and biliary disorders; very high doses can cause vomiting and spasms; and use should be stopped at least two weeks before surgery.

Chile — where it comes from

Boldo is a tree native to the dry central regions of Chile, where a tea of its leaf has been drunk for digestion for centuries. It reaches Europe and Turkey as dried leaf.

In nature

Where it grows
Scrub and hills of the dry central regions of Chile, where it is native. It has no wild distribution in Turkey and is imported as dried leaf.
Harvest season
Small whitish flowers in spring. The leaves can be gathered year-round and are dried.
Local names
Boldo; the Turkish monograph gives "boldo, boldu".

Mix-up risk

Because it does not grow wild in Turkey there is no local confusion risk; the thing to watch is the form of preparation. The drug the monograph covers is the leaf, and tea and alcoholic extract differ in ascaridole content. Boldo is also usually sold inside mixed products rather than alone; read the whole ingredient list.

Preparation

The monograph describes preparations of the leaf: herbal tea, infusion, decoction or macerate. Here the form of preparation is a safety matter: according to the Turkish monograph the toxic compound ascaridole is not present in aqueous preparations but can pass into ethanolic products. For dyspepsia and mild spasmodic complaints, consult a doctor or pharmacist if symptoms last more than two weeks; in mild liver and biliary disorders, do not exceed four weeks. Use under 18 has not been established.

What science says

There is no randomised clinical trial of boldo on its own. Human studies measure multi-ingredient products containing the plant or its main alkaloid boldine: the five-plant mixture in the 2026 Urologia trial, and the boldine + Phyllanthus niruri + restharrow product in the 2024 Medicina study. The latter is retrospective and measured a three-ingredient product added to tamsulosin; boldo’s own contribution cannot be drawn from that data. Moreover both test urinary tract problems rather than boldo’s EU indication, which is dyspepsia and gastrointestinal spasm. The rest of the recent literature is at laboratory level — alkaloid content measurements, hepatoprotective studies in cell and animal models. The EU monograph rests its indication on long use. The evidence level is Traditional. The real information in this record is not in the evidence but in the safety: the plant contains a toxic compound called ascaridole, and its warning list is among the longest in this catalog.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Medicines that can harm the liverThe Turkish monograph reports interaction with hepatotoxic drugs. That warning fits the plant’s own profile: boldo is already contraindicated in serious liver disease, and in mild liver disorders its use is limited to four weeks.
Blood-thinning medicinesThe Turkish monograph reports interaction with anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs. Because this group works within a narrow balance, a small shift changes bleeding risk.
TacrolimusThe Turkish monograph names tacrolimus. Tacrolimus is an immunosuppressant used after organ transplantation and its blood level is held within a very narrow range, which is why a reported interaction of this kind should be taken seriously.
LithiumThe Turkish monograph names lithium as well. Anyone on lithium should tell their doctor.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing; the Turkish one reports four medicines or drug groups.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Boldo.
