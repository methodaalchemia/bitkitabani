Greater celandine

Chelidonium majus L.
Evidence: TraditionalGenerally safe

The first plant in this catalog to which the European regulator said an explicit "no". Issuing a public statement instead of a monograph is the most negative decision the HMPC can give a plant — and that decision is information: not an absence of use (it was used in Germany for centuries), nor an absence of data (56 case reports), but the presence of data. A plant known in every village for the yellow latex dabbed on warts, whose internal use left the shelf as the liver cases accumulated — a documented example that "traditional" and "safe" are different words.

What is Greater celandine?

Greater celandine (Chelidonium majus L.) is a plant of the Papaveraceae (poppy family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe, Asia and Siberia. Part used: the flowering aerial parts; the fresh latex (folk use).

Safety label: generally safe. It can damage the liver. Traditional use

Europe — no monograph, a public statement

The European Medicines Agency’s Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products (HMPC) concluded on 13 September 2011 that the "benefit-risk assessment of oral use ... is considered negative" and that "safer herbal medicinal products are available in the indication in question." The reason is the liver: 56 case reports in the WHO database, 124 hepatobiliary adverse reactions (mostly from Germany); marketing authorisations withdrawn in some countries over liver toxicity. The committee was also not reassured by the claim that 2.5 mg of alkaloids a day is a safe limit.

Turkey

There is no monograph for greater celandine on the Turkish list. The plant is common at damp, shady wall bases and in villages in Turkey; dabbing its yellow latex on warts is a folk practice shared by Anatolia and Europe — the EU assessment examined that cutaneous use too and found it insufficiently supported by single-ingredient product data.

Europe — an old bile plant

Greater celandine was for centuries a bile and digestive-spasm plant of central European phytotherapy; Germany had licensed products until the 2000s. Its name comes from the Greek for swallow (chelidon): according to Dioscorides it flowers when the swallows arrive and fades when they leave. It carries more than twenty alkaloids (chelidonine, chelerythrine, sanguinarine).

In nature

Where it grows
Europe and western Asia; wall bases, hedges, shady nitrogen-rich ground. Common in Turkey.
Harvest season
Four-petalled yellow flowers from April to October; the broken stem bleeds orange latex.
Local names
Kırlangıçotu, temreotu, sarı sütleğen; regional names not extended without a source.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ The name "sarı sütleğen" confuses it with the spurges (Euphorbia); both have irritant latex but they are separate families. As a poppy-family plant it is kin to the corn poppy and opium poppy; the Turkish name "kırlangıçotu" can also be used for other plants.

Preparation

This record gives no dose: because the European Medicines Agency found the benefit-risk balance of oral use negative and there is no Turkish monograph, this catalog has no approved preparation. Dabbing the latex on warts is folk practice; the latex is irritant and must not touch the eyes or mouth.

What science says

Greater celandine’s evidence is a story turned on its head: there is evidence, but of harm. A review published in 2017 in the European Review for Medical and Pharmacological Sciences gathers numerous hepatotoxicity cases linked "probably" or "highly probably" to greater celandine by liver-targeted causality assessment and defines the picture as a distinct form of herb-induced liver injury (HILI), an idiosyncratic reaction of the metabolic type; the authors stress that this risk must be read together with the absence of any considerable demonstrated benefit. Two new case reports published in 2024 in the American Journal of Therapeutics present greater celandine as a "hidden cause" of unexplained raised transaminases. The European Medicines Agency likewise writes that evidence of clinical efficacy for monotherapy was lacking. Evidence level Traditional: none for benefit, some for harm.

Safety

Generally safe

It can damage the liver. The European Medicines Agency found the benefit-risk balance of oral use negative; the literature defines greater celandine hepatotoxicity as a distinct form of herb-induced liver injury, with cases presenting as jaundice and raised transaminases. Anyone using a product containing greater celandine who develops fatigue, dark urine, jaundice or right upper abdominal pain should stop it and see a doctor. That it should not be taken orally is this catalog’s position. The fresh latex is irritant; keep it from the eyes and mucous membranes, and children should not play with the plant. Not in pregnancy or lactation. Safety level Low.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Hepatotoxic drugs and alcoholWith no monograph there is no official interaction list; but the plant’s liver risk stacks on anything that burdens the liver (paracetamol, some antibiotics, statins, alcohol). A theoretical note — and this catalog does not recommend oral use in any case.
Herbal mixturesGreater celandine was an ingredient of some digestive mixtures in Europe; anyone using a mixture containing it should check the label. The EU public statement refers precisely to the withdrawal of such mixtures’ licences.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Greater celandine.
