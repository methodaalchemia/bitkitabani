In short

What is Lentil?

Lentil (Lens culinaris Medik.) is a plant of the Fabaceae (legume family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean. Part used: the seed.

What is Lentil used for?

Traditional use: All the lentil recovered from 1,950 litres of floated soil at Göbekli Tepe is half of a single seed. Scientific evidence: clinical. Lentil has been studied in randomised controlled trials in humans.

Is Lentil safe?

Safety label: generally safe. No verified drug interaction, pregnancy or breastfeeding restriction, or record of toxicity was found for lentil. Legume allergy exists, but no lentil-specific verified source was found, so no claim is made. Not eating legumes raw is a general kitchen rule.

Traditional use

Göbekli Tepe — half a seed

All the lentil recovered from 1,950 litres of floated soil at Göbekli Tepe is half of a single seed. Reinder Neef measured it: 2.1 mm across, well within the range of a wild lentil. Not the domesticated plant.

In Anatolian cooking

Lentil is one of the oldest and most widespread staples in Anatolia, made into soups, köfte and pilaf in dozens of regional forms. Even so, none of the Turkish ethnobotanical and folk-medicine papers we searched records lentil as a folk remedy: nothing in the Suruç, Şırnak, Mardin or Develi surveys. Its weight is culinary, not medicinal.

At the beginning of agriculture

Lentil is among the first legumes domesticated alongside cereals in the Fertile Crescent; its wild ancestor is Lens culinaris subsp. orientalis. Archaeobotanical work shows legumes were domesticated more slowly than cereals — the increase in seed size took centuries.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to the Fertile Crescent. Southeastern Anatolia — especially Şanlıurfa, Diyarbakır and Mardin — is one of the world’s largest red lentil producing regions. Drought-hardy and low in water need.
Harvest season
Sown in winter or spring; in Turkey red lentil is mostly sown in autumn and harvested in June and July.
Local names

Mix-up risk

Lentil can be confused with bitter vetch, broad bean and chickpea as dry seed, but these are separate species. Wild lentil (Lens culinaris subsp. orientalis) grows naturally in Anatolia and its seeds are markedly smaller than the domesticated form: archaeologists tell the two apart by that measurement.

Preparation

There is no official preparation: neither a European Medicines Agency monograph nor an NCCIH page exists. Clinical trials use roughly 100–200 g of cooked lentils a day, a food amount.

What science says

Lentil has been studied in randomised controlled trials in humans. Twelve weeks of daily lentils affects fasting cholesterol, post-meal glucose and inflammatory markers; an eight-week trial reports that it slows the progression of insulin resistance without increasing gastrointestinal symptoms. In acute trials, replacing rice or potato with lentils lowers post-meal blood glucose. Iron-fortified lentil improved iron status in adolescent girls in a cluster-randomised trial. All of this is evidence for lentil as a food.

Safety

Generally safe

No verified drug interaction, pregnancy or breastfeeding restriction, or record of toxicity was found for lentil. Legume allergy exists, but no lentil-specific verified source was found, so no claim is made. Not eating legumes raw is a general kitchen rule.
