Maize

Zea mays
Evidence: TraditionalGenerally safe

Every kernel on a cob has its own strand of silk; if that strand is not pollinated, the kernel does not develop. Each strand of silk is a kernel’s stigma.

Other names
Corn, corn silk, sara (Quechua)
Part used
Silk (stigmas), kernel
Region
North America
Andes
Use
Food, Healing, Ritual and shamanic
In short

What is Maize?

Maize (Zea mays) is a plant of the Poaceae (buğdaygiller) family. Traditions that use it: North America, Andes. Part used: silk (stigmas), kernel.

What is Maize used for?

Traditional use: Maize was domesticated from wild teosinte in Mexico about 9,000 years ago and became the staple food of the Americas. Scientific evidence: traditional. Reviews describe corn silk’s traditional use as a diuretic and for urinary complaints; its flavonoids show antioxidant and blood-sugar-lowering effects in lab and animal studies.

Is Maize safe?

Safety label: generally safe. Generally considered safe as a tea. Because it increases urine flow, avoid it where fluid intake must be limited, such as heart or kidney failure. High doses affected early pregnancy in rats, so avoid it in pregnancy. With signs of a urinary infection (fever, blood in urine, burning) see a doctor rather than relying on tea.

Traditional use

Mesoamerica and the Andes

Maize was domesticated from wild teosinte in Mexico about 9,000 years ago and became the staple food of the Americas. In the Maya Popol Vuh the gods make humans from maize dough. In the Andes maize is brewed into chicha, which is offered to Pachamama.

Anatolia and the world

In Türkiye corn-silk tea is drunk to increase urine flow and for kidney and urinary complaints. The silk has also been used for urinary problems in Chinese and Native American medicine.

In nature

Where it grows
Grown worldwide; in Türkiye mostly on the Black Sea coast, in Çukurova and in the south-east.
Harvest season
Silk is gathered while still pale, when the cobs are at the milk stage (July–August).
Local names
mısır, darı, püskül

Mix-up risk

On the Black Sea coast maize is called “darı”, which can be confused with true millet (Panicum miliaceum), also sold as bird seed.

Preparation

A dessertspoon of dried silk is steeped in a cup of boiling water for 10 minutes and drunk with plenty of water during the day.

What science says

Reviews describe corn silk’s traditional use as a diuretic and for urinary complaints; its flavonoids show antioxidant and blood-sugar-lowering effects in lab and animal studies. Controlled studies in people are very few. The European Medicines Agency’s assessment of corn silk is still at draft stage.

Safety

Generally safe

Generally considered safe as a tea. Because it increases urine flow, avoid it where fluid intake must be limited, such as heart or kidney failure. High doses affected early pregnancy in rats, so avoid it in pregnancy. With signs of a urinary infection (fever, blood in urine, burning) see a doctor rather than relying on tea.
