Rocoto

Capsicum pubescens Ruiz & Pav.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

Rocoto occupies an odd place among the domesticated peppers: it has never been found in the wild and exists only as a cultigen, so its wild ancestor is still not firmly known. Its smell surprises too — an aroma study found a distinct cucumber note in the fruit.

Other names
It has no Turkish name. Rocoto and locoto in the Andean countries; manzano and perón in Mexico. Neither the European Medicines Agency nor Türkiye’s TİTCK has a monograph for this species. EMA’s `Capsici fructus` monograph belongs to *Capsicum annuum* L. var. *minimum* and to small-fruited varieties of *Capsicum frutescens* — a separate record on this site — and *C. pubescens* falls outside its scope.

In short

What is Rocoto?

Rocoto (Capsicum pubescens Ruiz & Pav.) is a plant of the Solanaceae (nightshade family) family. Traditions that use it: Andes. Part used: ripe fruit.

What is Rocoto used for?

Traditional use: The species is grown at high altitude across the Andean countries, where its thick-fleshed fruit is stuffed (rocoto relleno) or made into sauce. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is not a single human safety study, case report or toxicology dataset for this species; all the safety information below sits at the level of the capsaicin compound or of the *Capsicum* genus.

Is Rocoto safe?

Safety label: caution. Pungency is a safety matter, and the data below sits at the level of the capsaicin compound and the *Capsicum* genus; there is no human data specific to this species. In its 2024 opinion, Germany’s Federal Institute for Risk Assessment (BfR) concludes that "an increased risk of gastric mucosal damage can be assumed even with intake levels that can be achieved through the traditional consumption of very spicy foods"; the symptoms run from burning in the upper gastrointestinal tract, heartburn and reflux to nausea, vomiting and abdominal and chest pain, with circulatory complaints such as cold sweats, blood pressure changes and dizziness at high intakes. The same body writes that 0.5–1 mg of capsaicinoids can lead to mild undesired effects and that pronounced adverse effects are to be expected around 170 mg, and notes that there are no legally established maximum levels for capsaicinoids in food in the European Union or Germany. That opinion names *Capsicum pubescens* among the species in use. Children are the most sensitive group: a 2023 BfR communication states that "children are particularly sensitive to hot chilli products" and records that serious poisonings in young children from ingesting chilli preparations have been described in the international literature, recommending that hot sauces be kept out of their reach. Two BfR figures stand side by side on this page, so it has to be said which comes from which document. The 0.5–1 mg and 170 mg figures above are from BfR Opinion 027/2024, which states plainly that "it is not possible to derive a specific dose above which the symptoms mentioned occur" and gives no tolerated value per kilogram. The figure that puts the spiciness an adult traditionally tolerates in a meal at "a maximum of one 5 mg dose of capsaicin per kg of body weight", corresponding to "an intake of 300 mg of capsaicin in one meal by an adult weighing 60 kg", comes from a different and older document: BfR Opinion 053/2011 of 18 October 2011 ("Too hot isn’t healthy"). The two figures come from two documents 13 years apart and the body has not reconciled them: the 2011 opinion treats 300 mg as the ceiling of *traditionally accepted* consumption, while the 2024 opinion expects *pronounced effects* around 170 mg. This page gives both and substitutes neither for the other. An exposure assessment in Korea found a mean daily intake of 3.25 mg and proposed 30 mg as the maximum tolerable dose for long-term repeated consumption; the same review reports that even low doses (under 5 mg) can cause acute abdominal pain in healthy people and aggravate symptoms in those with reflux. Contact with the eyes and mucous membranes is avoided, and hands are washed after preparation. Contact dermatitis has been reported with repeated skin contact. Safety level "moderate".

Traditional use

The Andes — a kitchen pepper

The species is grown at high altitude across the Andean countries, where its thick-fleshed fruit is stuffed (rocoto relleno) or made into sauce. In Bolivia and southern Peru it goes by the name locoto.

What sets it apart from other peppers

A 2022 genomic study in Plants describes the species this way: "unlike other cultivated chiles, this species is known only as a cultigen and has not been found in the wild so far". A 2024 follow-up likewise states that it "is not known from the wild and exists only as a cultigen", places its centre of origin in the central-western Bolivian highlands and names *C. eximium* and *C. cardenasii* as putative wild ancestors. Its marked pubescence, large brown-black seeds and mostly purple flowers distinguish it from other peppers.

In nature

Where it grows
Kew records a range of 11 regions: Bolivia, Colombia, Costa Rica, Ecuador, Guatemala, Honduras, two regions of Mexico, Peru, Venezuela and Java. Its native range is recorded as "Peru to Bolivia"; it does not grow in Türkiye. The species tolerates cold better than other peppers and is grown at altitude.
Harvest season
The fruits are picked as they ripen; the crop is not grown in Türkiye.
Local names
No Turkish name. Local names: rocoto, locoto, manzano, perón.

Mix-up risk

It should not be confused with the chilli pepper (*Capsicum annuum* var. *minimum*): that species has an EMA monograph and this one does not, and the monograph’s dosage and usage information does not apply here. The features that identify rocoto are hairy leaves, purple flowers and large brown-black seeds; the seeds of other peppers are pale yellow.

Preparation

Rocoto is a food; no official monograph sets an accepted dose and this page gives no measure of use. The fruit is eaten cooked, stuffed or as a sauce. Gloves are wise if skin contact is repeated: BfR notes that contact dermatitis has been described with repeated skin contact with chilli fruits. Hot chilli sauces are kept out of reach of small children.

What science says

There is not a single human safety study, case report or toxicology dataset for this species; all the safety information below sits at the level of the capsaicin compound or of the *Capsicum* genus. What is species-specific is the measurement of pungency: a 2026 study of 78 Peruvian Andean accessions measured total capsaicinoids at a mean of 2,161 µg/g (dry weight) and Scoville values at a mean of 34,509, with a range of 6,814–83,961 SHU. The same work describes the species’ biochemical signature as "dihydrocapsaicin-dominant", setting it against the capsaicin-predominant pattern of *C. chinense* and *C. frutescens*. A 2011 study assessed ají and rocoto accessions together and called them "mildly pungent compared with *C. chinense*"; the two sets of figures cannot be compared directly, because the weight bases differ and that range covers both species together. The same study’s species-specific and more curious finding is in the aroma: the smell of rocoto comes mainly from methoxypyrazines, and the fruit also carries a distinct cucumber odour. Evidence level "traditional" — this is a food record and carries no therapeutic claim.
