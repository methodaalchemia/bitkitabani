Rowan

Sorbus aucuparia
Evidence: TraditionalGenerally safe

The food preservative sorbic acid (E200) is named after this tree: A. W. Hofmann first isolated it from unripe rowan berries in 1859.

In short

What is Rowan?

Rowan (Sorbus aucuparia) is a plant of the Rosaceae (gülgiller) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: fruit (cooked or frozen).

What is Rowan used for?

Traditional use: Planted by houses against witches and evil spirits; small rowan crosses tied with red thread were hung over doors and hearths. Scientific evidence: traditional. Raw berries contain parasorbic acid; heating or freezing turns it into harmless sorbic acid.

Is Rowan safe?

Safety label: generally safe. In larger amounts the parasorbic acid in raw berries irritates the stomach and can cause indigestion and kidney damage. The seeds contain a cyanogenic glycoside (prunasin) and should be removed. Cooked or frozen berries are safer. Do not confuse rowan with other Sorbus species.

Traditional use

Scotland, Ireland and northern England

Planted by houses against witches and evil spirits; small rowan crosses tied with red thread were hung over doors and hearths. In North Yorkshire, loops of rowan were set on gates as amulets against witchcraft; the Pitt Rivers Museum in Oxford holds examples. In Ireland a rowan branch went over the door on May Eve, and on May Day red ribbons or rowan were tied to cows’ tails against the evil eye. In Scotland cutting down a rowan was taboo.

Norse mythology

In Snorri’s Edda, Thor, about to drown in the flooding river Vimur, saved himself by grabbing a rowan bush; hence the saying “rowan is Thor’s deliverance”.

European folk medicine

The fruit has been used as a laxative and diuretic, against rheumatism and kidney disease, as a gargle for hoarseness and as a source of vitamin C (Sarv et al. 2020).

In nature

Where it grows
Recorded in the flora of Turkey; its fruit has been studied from Bayburt. About 35 Sorbus species grow in the Caucasus and Turkey.
Harvest season
White flower clusters in May–June; orange-red berries ripen in August–October, best picked after the first frost.
Local names
Kuş üvezi

Mix-up risk

Plain “üvez” in Turkish usually means service tree (Sorbus domestica), a different species.

Preparation

Do not eat the berries raw; pick them after the first frost or cook them into jam, jelly or syrup. Remove the seeds.

What science says

Raw berries contain parasorbic acid; heating or freezing turns it into harmless sorbic acid. The fruit is rich in vitamin C and polyphenols; its folk uses have not been tested in clinical studies.

Safety

Generally safe

In larger amounts the parasorbic acid in raw berries irritates the stomach and can cause indigestion and kidney damage. The seeds contain a cyanogenic glycoside (prunasin) and should be removed. Cooked or frozen berries are safer. Do not confuse rowan with other Sorbus species.
