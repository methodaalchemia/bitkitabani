Sweetgrass

In short

What is Sweetgrass?

Sweetgrass (Hierochloe odorata) is a plant of the Poaceae (grass family) family. Traditions that use it: North America. Part used: leaves.

What is Sweetgrass used for?

Traditional use: For the Anishinaabe and many Plains peoples it is one of the four sacred medicines; it is braided and dried, and its tip is burned with prayer to call in good spirits. Scientific evidence: traditional. Its sweet, vanilla-like scent comes from coumarin; how coumarin is produced in this plant was shown in 1960 by an isotope-labelling study.

Is Sweetgrass safe?

Safety label: generally safe. Safe as incense; avoid breathing the smoke in closed, unventilated rooms. Because it contains coumarin, it is not eaten.

Traditional use

North America

For the Anishinaabe and many Plains peoples it is one of the four sacred medicines; it is braided and dried, and its tip is burned with prayer to call in good spirits. It is used in basketry.

Northern Europe

As “holy grass” it was strewn before church doors and hung in homes at feasts.

In nature

Where it grows
Moist meadows of North America, northern Europe and Siberia.
Harvest season
Cut in early summer when the leaves are most fragrant; the root is left.
Local names
No established Turkish name; tatlı ot, kutsal ot

Mix-up risk

Sweet vernal grass (Anthoxanthum odoratum) in Turkish meadows is a relative and smells similar.

Preparation

The leaves are braided into three strands and dried; the tip is lit and the flame put out to use the smoke.

What science says

Its sweet, vanilla-like scent comes from coumarin; how coumarin is produced in this plant was shown in 1960 by an isotope-labelling study. There are no human studies of its medicinal use. On the other hand, a 2016 laboratory study measured that the plant’s steam distillate deters mosquitoes from biting and isolated the compounds responsible — so the habit of the Flathead communities in Montana and the Blackfoot in Alberta of carrying the braid among clothes or burning it has a measurable counterpart.

Safety

Generally safe

Safe as incense; avoid breathing the smoke in closed, unventilated rooms. Because it contains coumarin, it is not eaten.

Conservation

Note
Indigenous gathering grounds have shrunk through urban growth and wetland drainage; it should be cut, not pulled up by the root.
