Sweet orange

The orange is not a species but a backcross hybrid of pummelo and mandarin (*Nature Genetics*, 2013). For this reason the Kew record lists the sweet orange not as a separate species but under the same hybrid name as the bitter orange.

Other names
Orange; common older name Citrus sinensis (Citrus × sinensis (L.) Osbeck); Turkish portakal. Kew’s Plants of the World Online record treats the name Citrus × sinensis as a synonym of Citrus × aurantium f. aurantium; that is, botanically it is gathered under the same hybrid name as the bitter orange. Medical and nutrition publications, however, continue to use the name Citrus sinensis for the sweet orange. This page is for the sweet orange; the bitter orange is a separate record.

In short

What is Sweet orange?

Sweet orange (Citrus × sinensis (L.) Osbeck) is a plant of the Rutaceae family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia, Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the fruit and its juice; the peel and the essential oil obtained from the peel.

What is Sweet orange used for?

Traditional use: The orange is not a natural species but a hybrid. Scientific evidence: limited. There are randomised controlled trials on orange juice.

Is Sweet orange safe?

Safety label: generally safe. As a food it is safe in ordinary amounts. Medicines: orange juice reduces the absorption of some medicines from the gut (below); medicines are taken with water, not with fruit juice. Teeth: excessive and frequent consumption of citrus fruits and juices can damage the teeth (2014). The essential oil is not drunk, is not applied to the skin undiluted and is kept out of the reach of children; if it is swallowed, 114 (the National Poison Information Centre in Türkiye) is called. The sources of this page contain no restriction on eating the fruit as food in pregnancy and breastfeeding; there are no data on the oral or skin use of the essential oil in these periods and in children. People with diabetes should discuss the amount of fruit juice with their doctor or dietitian. ⚠️ The warnings for bitter orange supplements do not apply to this page; they are in the bitter orange record.

Traditional use

Asia — the origin of a hybrid

The orange is not a natural species but a hybrid. The draft genome study published in *Nature Genetics* in 2013 presented evidence that the sweet orange originated from a backcross hybrid between pummelo and mandarin; the study predicted 29,445 protein-coding genes. The 2018 *Nature* study likewise showed that the citrus genus diversified in south-east Asia and that there is an extensive network of relatedness between the sweet orange and the mandarins.

Seafaring — scurvy

Over the last five centuries sailors and some ships’ doctors used oranges and lemons to prevent and cure scurvy. That the disease is caused by a deficiency of ascorbic acid (vitamin C) was shown in the 20th century (*Nutrition Reviews*, 2009). The genome study found that the gene encoding the rate-limiting enzyme in vitamin C production is markedly more active in orange fruit.

Today — juice and scent

Today the orange is consumed mostly as fruit and juice; the essential oil obtained from its peel is used in the perfume, food and beverage industries and in aromatherapy (*International Journal of Molecular Sciences*, 2018). The effect of the scent of orange on anxiety has been tested in dentists’ waiting rooms and in healthy volunteers.

In nature

Where it grows
It is a cultivated plant; no wild range is given in the sources of this page. Genome studies show that the citrus genus diversified in south-east Asia and that the sweet orange originated from a hybrid of pummelo and mandarin.
Harvest season
It is an evergreen fruit tree. The sources this page relies on give no flowering or harvest months.
Local names
The name portakal is in general use; no other local name appears in the sources of this page.

Mix-up risk

The bitter orange sits under the same hybrid name in the Kew record, but its fruit is bitter and the safety profile of its supplements is different; the two are not used in place of each other. Grapefruit juice interacts with other medicines (the 2010 review); the list of medicines given for orange juice does not carry over to grapefruit.

Preparation

The fruit is eaten fresh or its juice is squeezed; there is no accepted dose for medicinal use. No EU, WHO or Turkish (TİTCK) monograph was found among the sources this page relies on. In the meta-analysis below, the effects were marked in the subgroups drinking 500 ml or more of orange juice a day; this is not a treatment dose but the amount consumed in the studies. In the studies the essential oil was used by inhalation; the essential oil is not drunk.

What science says

There are randomised controlled trials on orange juice. A systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2022 (*Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition*) pooled 10 randomised controlled trials: compared with the control group, orange juice lowered fasting glucose by 2.92 mg/dl, total cholesterol by 9.84 mg/dl and LDL cholesterol by 9.14 mg/dl; the index of insulin resistance and CRP also fell. The effect on body composition and other cardiovascular risk factors was not significant. These differences are small, and outcomes such as disease or death were not measured. A meta-analysis (2019) of 10 trials (577 participants) in which hesperidin, the main flavonoid of orange, was given on its own, however, found no effect on cholesterol, triglycerides or blood pressure. On the essential oil, a 2018 review examined 9 clinical studies; the scent of orange reduced anxiety in patients waiting for dental treatment and in healthy volunteers placed in an anxiety-provoking situation. These studies are small and do not show treatment of an anxiety disorder.

Safety

Generally safe

As a food it is safe in ordinary amounts. Medicines: orange juice reduces the absorption of some medicines from the gut (below); medicines are taken with water, not with fruit juice. Teeth: excessive and frequent consumption of citrus fruits and juices can damage the teeth (2014). The essential oil is not drunk, is not applied to the skin undiluted and is kept out of the reach of children; if it is swallowed, 114 (the National Poison Information Centre in Türkiye) is called. The sources of this page contain no restriction on eating the fruit as food in pregnancy and breastfeeding; there are no data on the oral or skin use of the essential oil in these periods and in children. People with diabetes should discuss the amount of fruit juice with their doctor or dietitian. ⚠️ The warnings for bitter orange supplements do not apply to this page; they are in the bitter orange record.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Fexofenadine (an allergy medicine)According to a 2010 review (*British Journal of Clinical Pharmacology*), orange juice lowers the oral absorption of fexofenadine by inhibiting OATP transporters in the gut. In the measurement made with grapefruit juice the effect lasted more than 2 hours but less than 4; the review notes that leaving enough time between the medicine and the juice can prevent the interaction.
Atenolol and celiprolol (beta blockers)The same review reports that orange juice lowers the oral bioavailability of these medicines. The medicine is taken with water.
Ciprofloxacin (an antibiotic)According to the same review orange juice lowers the oral bioavailability of ciprofloxacin. The antibiotic is not taken with fruit juice.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Sweet orange.
