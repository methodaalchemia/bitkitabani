BT-179 · Vitaceae (grape family)

Grapevine leaf

Vitis vinifera L.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

The same part of the same plant in two different categories of use: the wrapper for stuffed vine leaves in an Anatolian kitchen, and a standardised extract for leg circulation in a European pharmacy. The medicinal leaf is the one that reddens in autumn — the anthocyanins that give it that colour are part of the definition of the extract. The leaf for cooking and the leaf for medicine come from the same vine, but not from the same season.

Other names
The plant is the grapevine; this record covers the leaf, because the leaf is the part in the monograph. It is sold medicinally as "red vine leaf": the leaves used are those that have turned red in autumn.

In short

What is Grapevine leaf?

Grapevine leaf (Vitis vinifera L.) is a plant of the Vitaceae (grape family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the leaf.

What is Grapevine leaf used for?

Traditional use: Vine leaves are a staple of Anatolian cooking, fresh or brined, wrapped around a rice filling. Scientific evidence: limited. Red vine leaf extract shows a measurable but small effect in venous insufficiency of the legs.

Is Grapevine leaf safe?

Safety label: caution. Not for anyone hypersensitive to the active substance. The Turkish monograph separately lists the situations that require medical advice during use: inflammation of the skin, thrombophlebitis or subcutaneous induration, severe pain, ulceration, sudden swelling of one or both legs, and cardiac or renal failure. These are the signs that chronic venous insufficiency should not be managed with a herbal product alone: sudden swelling of a leg can indicate a clot. Preparations applied to the skin must not be used on damaged skin, around the eyes or on mucous membranes. Durations of use are limited by indication: four weeks for leg symptoms, one week for haemorrhoids and capillary fragility. The culinary use of vine leaves is not the subject of these warnings.

Traditional use

Anatolia — in cooking

Vine leaves are a staple of Anatolian cooking, fresh or brined, wrapped around a rice filling. That is a food use and not the same thing as the medicinal use below, which involves a standardised extract of autumn-reddened leaves.

Europe — leg circulation

The EMA monograph covers the leaf under two categories at once. Under "well-established use" the indication is chronic venous insufficiency: swelling, heaviness and pain in the legs. Under "traditional use" it also lists itching and burning from haemorrhoids, and cutaneous capillary fragility. Of the 68 plants in this batch, this is the only one whose monograph uses both categories together.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "leaves", requires the herbal substance to comply with the French Pharmacopoeia X monograph, and sets separate durations of use per indication: four weeks for discomfort and heaviness in the legs, one week for haemorrhoids and capillary fragility.

In nature

Where it grows
Originating in the South Caucasus and Anatolia; Turkey holds one of the oldest and most diverse grapevine gene pools in the world. Grown in almost every region of the country.
Harvest season
Leaves for cooking are picked in May and June while still tender. Leaves for the medicinal extract are harvested in autumn, after they have reddened.
Local names
The plant is asma or üzüm in Turkish; the leaf is "asma yaprağı", and brined leaves are simply "yaprak". Commercial medicinal products use the name red vine leaf.

Mix-up risk

The real risk is confusing the culinary and medicinal uses: the dose of a pharmacy extract and the quantity of leaves in a kitchen are not interchangeable, and eating stuffed vine leaves has no effect on leg swelling. Not to be confused with wild grapevine (Vitis sylvestris) either.

Preparation

There is no home preparation for the medicinal use: both the trials and the monographs concern a standardised dry extract of autumn leaves (coded AS 195 in the literature). The culinary use is something else entirely: fresh or brined leaves wrapped around a filling and cooked. The Turkish monograph sets limits on the medicinal use — consult a doctor or pharmacist after four weeks, or if symptoms last longer than two weeks.

What science says

Red vine leaf extract shows a measurable but small effect in venous insufficiency of the legs. In a randomised, placebo-controlled trial published in 2011, 248 patients followed for 12 weeks, the extract reduced lower limb volume by a mean of 19.9 ml against placebo (95% CI 2.3–37.5 ml; standardised effect size 0.28). Leg pain on a visual analogue scale also differed from placebo, but other symptoms showed no significant change. The trial itself flags something important: patients’ overall rating of efficacy did not correlate well with the measured reduction in limb volume. A 2020 systematic review of five trials points the same way: the extract significantly improved endpoints such as limb volume, calf and ankle circumference, tired and heavy legs, tingling and pain in only some of the trials, with good tolerability. The review concludes that higher-quality trials are needed for strong evidence. The evidence level here is therefore Limited rather than Clinical: the effect has been measured against placebo, but it is small and inconsistent.

Safety

Caution

Not for anyone hypersensitive to the active substance. The Turkish monograph separately lists the situations that require medical advice during use: inflammation of the skin, thrombophlebitis or subcutaneous induration, severe pain, ulceration, sudden swelling of one or both legs, and cardiac or renal failure. These are the signs that chronic venous insufficiency should not be managed with a herbal product alone: sudden swelling of a leg can indicate a clot. Preparations applied to the skin must not be used on damaged skin, around the eyes or on mucous membranes. Durations of use are limited by indication: four weeks for leg symptoms, one week for haemorrhoids and capillary fragility. The culinary use of vine leaves is not the subject of these warnings.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe interaction section of the monograph reports none, and the Turkish monograph lists nothing under drug interactions either. That is a statement about what has been studied, not evidence that no interaction exists.
A note for people on anticoagulantsChronic venous insufficiency and leg swelling overlap with vascular conditions that call for anticoagulant treatment. Although no interaction study confirms a problem, anyone on these medicines should tell their doctor about the herbal product — the real risk is not an interaction but a symptom that needs diagnosis being masked by a herbal product.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Grapevine leaf.
