In short

What is Oregon grape?

Oregon grape (Berberis aquifolium Pursh) is a plant of the Berberidaceae family. Traditions that use it: North America. Part used: root; plant extract in creams and ointments for external use.

What is Oregon grape used for?

Traditional use: A study published in the Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine in 2007 is based on interviews with farmers in the Canadian province of British Columbia. Scientific evidence: limited. The evidence concerns external use and psoriasis.

Is Oregon grape safe?

Safety label: caution. In external use, side effects in the 2006 trial were infrequent (under 1%) and minor: rash, a burning sensation on application and staining of clothes. ⚠️ Use by mouth is a different matter, because the plant contains berberine. According to NCCIH, berberine may be unsafe in pregnancy and while breastfeeding and is likely to be unsafe for infants; in infants it has been linked to a build-up of bilirubin that can cause brain damage. For this reason pregnant and breastfeeding women and infants should not use it, and breastfeeding mothers should not apply it to the breast area. Nausea, abdominal pain, bloating, constipation and diarrhoea have been reported in studies of berberine. People on regular medication, transplant patients in particular, should not use it without consulting their doctor.

Traditional use

British Columbia — root tea in animal care

A study published in the Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine in 2007 is based on interviews with farmers in the Canadian province of British Columbia. According to it, the plant is used for abscesses, wounds and injuries in ruminants; a strong tea made by simmering the dried root is added to the drinking water against infection. The authors graded this use as of "mid-level validity" for respiratory support and for wounds.

Dermatology — externally in psoriasis

The 2018 review in the Journal of Clinical and Aesthetic Dermatology presents the plant as a species native to the United States whose anti-inflammatory properties may be useful in skin disorders. Creams and ointments containing the plant extract have been tried mainly in psoriasis and, in one trial, in atopic dermatitis. The review notes that no clinical trial was found in any other skin disorder.

A source of berberine — the supplement market

The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) writes that berberine is a compound found in plants such as goldenseal, barberry and Oregon grape. Berberine supplements are sold on a weight-loss claim. NCCIH’s safety warnings about berberine also apply to products of this plant taken by mouth.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew POWO it is a shrub or subshrub; its native range runs from south-western Canada to northern California (British Columbia, Washington, Oregon, Idaho, Montana, California) and it grows mainly in the temperate zone. It is not native to Türkiye.
Harvest season
The sources relied on here give no calendar for flowering or gathering. In ethnoveterinary records the dried root is used.
Local names
It has no established local name in Turkish.

Mix-up risk

Barberry (Berberis vulgaris) is a different species of the same genus and has its own record on this site. According to NCCIH, goldenseal and barberry also contain berberine; the berberine warnings are shared, but clinical trials cannot be transferred from one species to another. On product labels the plant may appear as Mahonia aquifolium or as Berberis aquifolium; the two are the same species.

Preparation

No EMA, WHO or Turkish (TİTCK) monograph could be found; there is no accepted dose. In clinical trials the plant was used externally only: a cream containing 10% extract, or a cream containing a proprietary extract, was applied twice a day for up to 12 weeks. These are research conditions, not recommendations. Simmering the root as a drink appears in ethnoveterinary records; there is no reliable dose for use by mouth in humans, and because of berberine the warnings below apply.

What science says

The evidence concerns external use and psoriasis. The 2018 review examined 8 clinical trials meeting its criteria out of 502 publications; in 5 of the 7 trials in psoriasis a statistically significant improvement was reported, and the risk of bias was judged low or unclear. In the largest of these, a double-blind trial from 2006, 200 patients applied cream or placebo twice a day for 12 weeks, and disease severity (PASI) and quality-of-life scores improved significantly in the plant group compared with placebo (P < 0.05). In a 2005 report, 84% of 32 patients rated the response as good to excellent; that study was not blinded. A 2025 systematic review (20 randomised trials, 1115 patients) finds the plant "promising" but writes that more research is needed on the true effectiveness and safety of herbal treatments. These sources contain no clinical evidence for use by mouth.

Safety

Caution

In external use, side effects in the 2006 trial were infrequent (under 1%) and minor: rash, a burning sensation on application and staining of clothes. ⚠️ Use by mouth is a different matter, because the plant contains berberine. According to NCCIH, berberine may be unsafe in pregnancy and while breastfeeding and is likely to be unsafe for infants; in infants it has been linked to a build-up of bilirubin that can cause brain damage. For this reason pregnant and breastfeeding women and infants should not use it, and breastfeeding mothers should not apply it to the breast area. Nausea, abdominal pain, bloating, constipation and diarrhoea have been reported in studies of berberine. People on regular medication, transplant patients in particular, should not use it without consulting their doctor.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
CiclosporinAccording to NCCIH, berberine has been shown to interact with ciclosporin, which is used to prevent rejection in organ transplantation. Products of this berberine-containing plant taken by mouth should not be used by people taking ciclosporin.
Other medicines — no dataThe sources relied on here contain no drug interaction study done with this plant itself. People on regular medication should not use products taken by mouth without asking their doctor.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Oregon grape.
