Java tea

Orthosiphon aristatus (Blume) Miq. var. aristatus (syn. O. stamineus Benth.)
Evidence: LimitedHigh risk

Its name is the trace of a colonial route: Indonesia’s "kumis kucing" (cat’s whiskers) entered Europe as "Java tea" by way of Dutch pharmacists and stands under that name in the EU monograph today. Turkish received both names translated. The plant’s only single-ingredient clinical trial, meanwhile, is neither in Java nor in the urinary tract: in Malaysia, in chemotherapy fatigue. A plant’s name, its tradition and its evidence can sit in three different places.

In short

What is Java tea?

Java tea (Orthosiphon aristatus (Blume) Miq. var. aristatus (syn. O. stamineus Benth.)) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia. Part used: the leaves and stem tips.

What is Java tea used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": to increase the amount of urine to achieve flushing of the urinary tract, as an adjuvant in minor urinary tract complaints. Scientific evidence: limited. There is no clinical trial of Java tea in the monograph indication — urinary flushing.

Is Java tea safe?

Safety label: high risk. Not for use in serious cardiac or renal disease requiring fluid restriction — the flushing indication requires plenty of water. See a doctor if fever, painful urination, spasms or blood in the urine appear; these may signal infection or a stone and should not be managed with tea. Not recommended under 18, in pregnancy or lactation. No adverse effects known; in the 110-patient cancer trial adverse events were rare and mild. Liquid extracts containing ethanol must carry the appropriate labelling.

Traditional use

Europe — one indication

The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": to increase the amount of urine to achieve flushing of the urinary tract, as an adjuvant in minor urinary tract complaints. Contraindicated in serious cardiac or renal disease requiring fluid restriction; not established under 18; two weeks.

Turkey

There is no monograph for Java tea on the Turkish list. It has no wild distribution in Turkey and is sold as imported tea and capsules.

Southeast Asia

The plant is native to Indonesia, Malaysia and southern China, recognised by its long white-purple stamens like a cat’s whiskers. In Indonesia it is the kidney and urinary tea of the jamu tradition; it entered European pharmacopoeias in the early 20th century by way of Dutch colonial pharmacy — the name "Java tea" is the trace of that route.

In nature

Where it grows
The humid tropics of Southeast Asia; cultivated. It does not grow in Turkey.
Harvest season
Flowers year-round in a tropical climate; leaves and stem tips are gathered before flowering.
Local names
Java çayı, kedi bıyığı — translations of the international names; no vernacular Turkish name.

Mix-up risk

The name "cat’s whiskers" is also used for other plants (Cleome); "Java tea" may suggest a link with Camellia sinensis tea — there is none, it is a mint-family plant.

Preparation

The EU monograph gives seven forms for adults: tea, 2–3 g of leaf infused in 150 ml of boiling water (6–12 g daily); powdered drug 500–750 mg (1000–1500 mg daily); liquid extract (1:1) 2 g; various dry extracts. Because the indication is flushing, plenty of fluid is essential. See a doctor if symptoms last longer than two weeks. Not recommended under 18.

What science says

There is no clinical trial of Java tea in the monograph indication — urinary flushing. Most human work concerns multi-ingredient nutraceuticals (Orthosiphon-containing mixtures for blood pressure), which measure the mixture. One study rests on the plant alone, in a quite different field: a multicentre, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase II trial published in 2024 in Supportive Care in Cancer gave 110 cancer patients on chemotherapy a standardised, rosmarinic acid-rich extract of Orthosiphon stamineus (a commercial product) or placebo for 9 weeks and reported significant improvement in fatigue scores (BFI, VAS-F) and quality of life. One trial, a patented extract, an indication outside the monograph — evidence level Limited; it cannot be carried over to a urinary tea. A 2022 systematic review gathers diabetes-related studies, but these are mostly animal work.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing; there is no Turkish monograph. As with any diuretic plant, stacking on a diuretic drug and affecting the levels of renally cleared drugs such as lithium should be kept in mind theoretically — not in the monograph.
