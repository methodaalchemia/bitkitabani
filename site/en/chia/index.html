In short

What is Chia?

Chia (Salvia hispanica L.) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: North America. Part used: seed (whole or ground).

What is Chia used for?

Traditional use: An ethnobotanical review published in Economic Botany in 2003 describes chia as an important staple food and medicinal plant of pre-Columbian Mesoamerica. Scientific evidence: limited. The 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis in Nutrition Reviews examined 12 randomised controlled trials; participants were healthy people, athletes, people with diabetes and people with metabolic syndrome.

Is Chia safe?

Safety label: generally safe. In its 2019 opinion EFSA found chia seed safe under the assessed conditions of use; in a 76-person trial giving 50 g a day for 12 weeks no adverse event related to the seed was reported. The one hazard EFSA identified is allergy: two case reports describe itching in the mouth, generalised hives, facial swelling, shortness of breath and hand eczema. Cross-reactivity with the sera of people allergic to peanut and sesame has also been recorded; people with those allergies should be careful. The sources relied on here contain no separate assessment for pregnancy, breastfeeding or children; there are no data for use above food amounts. Because meta-analyses report a fall in blood pressure, people taking blood pressure medicines would do well to tell their doctor about regular, high intake; there is no interaction study on this.

Traditional use

Mesoamerica — a staple food and medicinal plant

An ethnobotanical review published in Economic Botany in 2003 describes chia as an important staple food and medicinal plant of pre-Columbian Mesoamerica. The same review notes that the plant has been studied far less than amaranth and the chenopods. The author compared the records in sixteenth-century Mexican codices with later publications.

Mexico and Guatemala — use that shifted after the colonial period

According to the same review, after Spanish colonisation there were small but traceable shifts in the medicinal, culinary, artistic and religious uses of chia. The author tested this change through fieldwork in western Mexico and the Guatemalan highlands. The review concludes that the decline of ethnobotanical knowledge and the loss of habitat have weakened both the seed resources and the knowledge about the plant.

European Union — authorised as a "novel food"

According to EFSA’s 2019 opinion, chia seed was first authorised for the EU market in 2009, for bread products only; that assessment considered a level of 5% in bread. In later years the authorisation was extended to categories such as baked products, breakfast cereals, fruit, nut and seed mixes, fruit juices, yoghurt and pre-packaged seed. EFSA also records that the seed has a history of consumption in Latin America going back hundreds of years.

Today — consumed as a supplement

A systematic review published in Nutrition Reviews in 2018 writes that chia seed is now a popular dietary supplement, taken mainly for its content of alpha-linolenic acid, vegetable protein and dietary fibre. The same review also states that information on its clinical effects is lacking.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew POWO its native range runs from Mexico to Guatemala; it is an annual and grows mainly in the subtropical zone. According to EFSA, the seed reaching the EU is imported from producing countries in South and Central America, Africa and Australia.
Harvest season
The EFSA opinion describes the plant as a summer annual herb. The sources relied on here give no reliable sowing and harvest calendar for Türkiye.
Local names
It has no established local name in Turkish.

Preparation

No official monograph sets an accepted dose; chia is a food. The EFSA opinion describes the seed being added, whole or ground, to bread, yoghurt, fruit juices and cereal mixes. The table of EU history of use in the same opinion gives 15 g a day for the seed sold on its own. EFSA drew attention to a study showing increased acrylamide formation in biscuits made with chia flour and baked at 190 °C, and left products cooked at high heat for separate assessment.

What science says

The 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis in Nutrition Reviews examined 12 randomised controlled trials; participants were healthy people, athletes, people with diabetes and people with metabolic syndrome. The pooled results showed no significant difference; only in subgroup analyses at higher doses were there a fall in blood glucose after meals, a 0.10 mmol/L reduction in HDL and a 7.14 mmHg fall in diastolic blood pressure. The authors rated the quality of all the evidence as low or very low and stressed that the trials measured surrogate markers only. A meta-analysis of 8 trials published in the same journal in 2025 reported a fall of 7.19 mmHg in systolic and 6.04 mmHg in diastolic blood pressure; it found no significant effect on body weight, waist circumference, fasting blood glucose or HbA1c. That review also notes that the data are limited. None of these sources includes a trial measuring clinical outcomes such as heart disease or death.

Safety

Generally safe

In its 2019 opinion EFSA found chia seed safe under the assessed conditions of use; in a 76-person trial giving 50 g a day for 12 weeks no adverse event related to the seed was reported.
