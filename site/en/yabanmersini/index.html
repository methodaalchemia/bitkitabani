BT-191 · Ericaceae (fundagiller)

Bilberry

Vaccinium myrtillus L.
Evidence: TraditionalGenerally safe

Bilberry’s reputation rests on a wartime story: British pilots in the Second World War are said to have eaten bilberry jam to sharpen their night vision. The story made the plant the world’s best-selling eye supplement. Yet none of the four indications the European Medicines Agency gives for bilberry has anything to do with the eyes — they are heavy legs, capillary fragility, mild diarrhoea and oral inflammation. And the most recent, most rigorous placebo-controlled trials of night vision were negative. A rare case of a plant whose fame, whose evidence and whose official use point in three different directions.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the names "yaban mersini, ayı üzümü, çoban üzümü". ⚠️ "Ayı üzümü" is also used in Turkish for a different plant, Arctostaphylos uva-ursi. In English: bilberry, whortleberry, European blueberry — but the American blueberry (Vaccinium corymbosum) is a different species.

In short

What is Bilberry?

Bilberry (Vaccinium myrtillus L.) is a plant of the Ericaceae (fundagiller) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the fruit.

What is Bilberry used for?

Traditional use: The EMA has two separate monographs, for different things. Scientific evidence: traditional. The most important fact in this record is that the use the plant is most heavily marketed for appears in no monograph at all.

Is Bilberry safe?

Safety label: generally safe. No serious safety problem is reported in the monographs; the plant is also a fruit. Not for anyone hypersensitive to the active substance. The Turkish monograph notes that digestive complaints and nausea can occur because of the high tannin content — which matters especially for the dried fruit. If symptoms last more than two weeks or worsen, or if cardiac or renal insufficiency appears, consult a doctor. For the circulatory indication the EU monograph also asks for a doctor if there is inflammation of the skin, thrombophlebitis, subcutaneous induration, severe pain, ulcers, or sudden swelling of one or both legs; these can signal conditions far more serious than heavy legs. The Turkish monograph does not recommend use under 18, in pregnancy or during lactation; the EU monograph, by contrast, states that the dried fruit can be used in pregnancy and lactation. The two agencies diverge here because of the long history of eating the dried fruit as a food.

Traditional use

Europe — fresh fruit: legs and capillaries

The EMA has two separate monographs, for different things. The fresh-fruit monograph gives two indications, both under "traditional use": relief of discomfort and heaviness of the legs related to minor venous circulatory disturbances, and relief of symptoms of cutaneous capillary fragility.

Europe — dried fruit: diarrhoea and the mouth

The dried-fruit monograph gives two different indications: symptomatic treatment of mild diarrhoea, and of minor inflammations of the oral mucosa. The logic is the tannins: the tannin content of the dried fruit acts as an astringent on the gut lining and in the mouth. The same plant carries different uses fresh and dried at monograph level — rare in this catalog.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the fruits" and notes that digestive complaints and nausea can occur because of the high tannin content. If symptoms last more than two weeks, consult a doctor.

Anatolia

Bilberry is a plant of the Black Sea mountains, gathered locally to eat and to make jam. That food use is independent of the indications in the monographs.

In nature

Where it grows
Acidic, damp forest floors and mountain heaths of Europe and northern Asia. In Turkey it grows in the higher parts of the Black Sea mountains.
Harvest season
Small pink-green bell-shaped flowers in May and June; the fruit ripens and is gathered in July and August.
Local names
Yaban mersini, ayı üzümü, çoban üzümü — all three appear in the Turkish monograph. Names such as "likapa" and "çalı çileği" are also heard on the Black Sea coast.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ Three confusions. The name: "ayı üzümü" covers both this plant and Arctostaphylos uva-ursi in Turkish, and their uses differ — uva-ursi is used for the urinary tract. The species: the American blueberry (Vaccinium corymbosum) is large and pale inside, bilberry small and purple throughout; the monographs cover V. myrtillus only. The preparation: fresh and dried fruit have separate monographs and different indications.

Preparation

The two preparations correspond to the two monographs: fresh fruit for the circulatory indications, dried fruit for diarrhoea and the mouth. Dried fruit should not be used longer than three days for diarrhoea or one week in the mouth; for the circulatory indication, consult a doctor if use goes beyond two weeks. The Turkish monograph does not recommend use under 18.

What science says

The most important fact in this record is that the use the plant is most heavily marketed for appears in no monograph at all. Bilberry is sold worldwide for eye health and night vision in particular; the story comes from an account of British pilots in the Second World War sharpening their night vision with bilberry jam. A systematic review published in Survey of Ophthalmology tested the claim against placebo-controlled trials: 30 trials with outcome measures relevant to vision in reduced light were found, 12 of them placebo-controlled. The four most recent were all randomised controlled trials and all four were negative. The trials reporting positive effects were either not randomised or methodologically weaker; the review notes that negative outcome went together with more rigorous methodology. The conclusion is plain: the hypothesis that V. myrtillus anthocyanosides improve normal night vision is not supported by evidence from rigorous clinical studies. The review leaves one honest opening: 11 of the 12 trials tested healthy people with normal or above-average eyesight, so rigorous research in people with impaired night vision is entirely absent. On the cardiometabolic side, a systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2025 in Phytotherapy Research pooled 11 randomised trials (409 people): no significant change in fasting blood glucose, total cholesterol, triglycerides, blood pressure, body weight or inflammatory markers; a marginally significant reduction in HbA1c (p=0.06), and a significant change in triglycerides in the shorter trials. The review asks for long-term, high-quality trials. The evidence level is therefore Traditional: the EU monographs rest all four indications on long use, and the eye use the marketing leads with is not borne out by randomised trials.

Safety

Generally safe

No serious safety problem is reported in the monographs; the plant is also a fruit. Not for anyone hypersensitive to the active substance. Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Blood-thinning medicinesThe Turkish monograph reports interaction with anticoagulants, antiplatelet and thrombolytic agents and low-molecular-weight heparins. The length of that list is notable: four separate drug groups. Anyone on a blood thinner should tell their doctor about a bilberry supplement — the warning is about supplement doses, not about eating the fruit.
Antidiabetic medicinesThe Turkish monograph lists this group too. The marginally significant reduction in HbA1c in the meta-analysis keeps the warning concrete: for someone on diabetes medication, even a small additional drop is worth accounting for.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographsBoth European monographs leave the interaction section empty; the Turkish one names five drug groups.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Bilberry.
