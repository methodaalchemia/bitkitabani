White dead-nettle

Lamium album L.
Evidence: TraditionalGenerally safe

The Turkish name catches the plant exactly: "honey father". Children pick the flower and suck the drop at its base, and the bee comes for the same drop. In the registration document for Bingöl honey this plant’s pollen comes out dominant — so the honey in its name is not a metaphor but a record.

Other names
Turkish *ballıbaba* ("honey father"), *ak ballıbaba* — the name comes straight from honey: the nectar at the base of the flower is sweet, and children pick the flowers and suck it. English white dead-nettle: the leaves resemble a nettle’s but do not sting, hence "dead". The parts used are the flowers and flowering branches. No Turkish, EU or WHO monograph. *Lamium* sp. is dominant in the Bingöl honey registration.

In short

What is White dead-nettle?

White dead-nettle (Lamium album L.) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe, Asia and Siberia. Part used: the flowers and flowering branches.

What is White dead-nettle used for?

Traditional use: White dead-nettle grows along hedgerows and damp field margins, looking like a nettle but not stinging. Scientific evidence: traditional. The evidence side of white dead-nettle is empty, and it is worth saying so as it is: there is no study in people, and no regulatory monograph — not Turkish, not EU, not WHO.

Is White dead-nettle safe?

Safety label: generally safe. No serious toxicity or adverse effect has been reported for white dead-nettle, and even mistaken for a nettle it does not sting. But there is no human safety data either — nothing supports regular use in pregnancy, lactation or children, so making a habit of it is not advised. Allergy in the mint family is rare but possible. Where it is gathered matters: it grows on roadsides and hedgerows, and should not be collected where exhaust or pesticide may have settled. Safety level: "low risk".

Traditional use

Anatolia and Europe — a plant named after honey

White dead-nettle grows along hedgerows and damp field margins, looking like a nettle but not stinging. Its Turkish name describes the nectar at the base of the flower; children picking the flowers to suck it is a habit shared across Anatolia and Europe. In European folk medicine a tea was made from the flowers; in Turkey it is gathered under the name ballıbaba.

Bingöl — an indicator plant of the honey

In the pollen table of the denomination-of-origin registration, *Lamium* sp. reaches dominant level, and *Lamium macrodon* is listed separately as secondary to minor. The flower structure of the mint family suits bees — they can reach the nectar at the base of the long corolla tube. It is one of the few plants whose name and function coincide.

Research — all of it in the laboratory

The work published on white dead-nettle has so far stayed at laboratory level: the antimicrobial activity of extracts (*Afr J Tradit Complement Altern Med*, 2013), the effect of flower extracts on *Fusarium* mould and mycotoxin production (*Toxins*, 2023), and bioactivity profiling by HPTLC (*J Chromatogr B*, 2026). There is no study in people.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to Europe and Asia; common in Turkey on damp meadows, hedgerows, roadsides and garden corners. In Bingöl along meadows and streams.
Harvest season
In flower for a long season, April to September; gathered while in flower and dried in the shade.
Local names
Ballıbaba, ak ballıbaba, beyaz ballıbaba, ölü ısırgan; white dead-nettle (English).

Mix-up risk

⚠️ Confused with the nettle (*Urtica dioica*) — the leaves are similar, but this does not sting and its flowers are large and two-lipped, while a nettle’s are small and greenish in catkins. ⚠️ Motherwort (*Leonurus cardiaca*) is a different genus in the same family; motherwort’s leaves are deeply cut with pointed lobes, dead-nettle’s heart-shaped and toothed. ⚠️ Red dead-nettle (*Lamium purpureum*) and *L. macrodon* are different species.

Preparation

In folk use an infusion is made from the dried flowers. No standard dose is found in the sources this site relies on, so none is given here. The nectar of the flower is sucked directly — a traditional and harmless habit.

What science says

The evidence side of white dead-nettle is empty, and it is worth saying so as it is: there is no study in people, and no regulatory monograph — not Turkish, not EU, not WHO. The published work is at laboratory level: antimicrobial activity of extracts (2013), suppression of mould growth and mycotoxin production by flower extracts (2023), and chromatographic profiling of its bioactive constituents (2026). These show the plant is interesting, not that it works. The solid ground of this page is the beekeeping and cultural side: its dominant presence in the pollen of Bingöl honey is documented in the registration. Evidence rating "traditional".

Safety

Generally safe

No serious toxicity or adverse effect has been reported for white dead-nettle, and even mistaken for a nettle it does not sting. But there is no human safety data either — nothing supports regular use in pregnancy, lactation or children, so making a habit of it is not advised. Allergy in the mint family is rare but possible. Where it is gathered matters: it grows on roadsides and hedgerows, and should not be collected where exhaust or pesticide may have settled. Safety level: "low risk".

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No recorded drug interactionNo interaction study in people exists for white dead-nettle; nor is there data to say it has none. Anyone on regular medication should tell their doctor.
Blood thinners (theoretical)Many plants of the mint family carry compounds that can affect bleeding; nothing has been shown for white dead-nettle, and the principle comes only from the family resemblance.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying White dead-nettle.
