In short

What is Greek sage?

Greek sage (Salvia fruticosa) is a plant of the Lamiaceae family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean. Part used: leaves.

What is Greek sage used for?

Traditional use: In Aegean and Mediterranean villages it is brewed for sore throats, the stomach and colds, often mixed with linden and quince leaves. Scientific evidence: limited. The EMA recognises sage (Salvia officinalis) for mouth and throat inflammation and excessive sweating as traditional uses.

Is Greek sage safe?

Safety label: caution. Because it contains thujone, it should not be used long-term or in high doses. Not recommended in pregnancy, breastfeeding or epilepsy.

Traditional use

Anatolia

In Aegean and Mediterranean villages it is brewed for sore throats, the stomach and colds, often mixed with linden and quince leaves.

Europe

Its Latin name comes from salvare (to heal, to save); in medieval Europe it was thought to cure everything.

In nature

Where it grows
Maquis and rocky slopes on the Aegean and Mediterranean coasts, up to about 800 m.
Harvest season
Leaves are picked before flowering, in spring and early summer.
Local names
adaçayı, elma çayı (Aegean), şalba, dişotu

Mix-up risk

Several Salvia species are collected as sage in the Aegean, with different thujone levels.

Preparation

Brewed as tea for 5 minutes; used as a gargle.

What science says

The EMA recognises sage (Salvia officinalis) for mouth and throat inflammation and excessive sweating as traditional uses. The Anatolian species is rich in cineole and usually contains less thujone.

How strong is the evidence?

Claims often repeated online about Greek sage, and what science says today.

Helps sore throat and mouth inflammation as a gargleLimited evidenceRecognised by the EMA as traditional; in a small study a sage spray eased sore throat.
Reduces excessive sweatingTraditional onlyRecognised by the EMA as traditional; small studies on menopausal hot flushes exist.
Improves memoryLimited evidenceShort-term gains in attention and memory in very small studies; inconclusive.

Common mistakes

  • Drinking it long-term and in high doses: thujone raises seizure risk. Keep courses to 1–2 weeks.
  • Drinking the essential oil.
  • Assuming Anatolian sage (Salvia fruticosa) and common sage (Salvia officinalis) are the same; their chemistry differs.

Frequently asked

Can I drink sage every day?

A few cups now and then are considered safe. With daily, long-term use, take breaks because of thujone.

Can I drink sage in pregnancy?

Not recommended; it may also reduce milk supply when breastfeeding.

How do I make a gargle?

Steep a teaspoon of dried leaves in a cup of boiling water for 5–10 minutes, let it cool, gargle and do not swallow.

Safety

Caution

Because it contains thujone, it should not be used long-term or in high doses. Not recommended in pregnancy, breastfeeding or epilepsy.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Seizure medicines (antiepileptics)Thujone can lower the seizure threshold and weaken the drug at high doses.
Diabetes medicinesCan lower blood sugar; risk of hypoglycaemia with the drug.
Sedatives and sleeping pillsMay increase the effect.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Greek sage.
