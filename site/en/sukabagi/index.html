BT-301 · Cucurbitaceae (gourd family)

Bottle gourd

Lagenaria siceraria (Molina) Standl.
Evidence: TraditionalHigh risk

It is one of the first plants people domesticated, and they did it not to eat but to carry: the dried shell makes a watertight vessel. The fruit floats in salt water for months, so it crossed the oceans on its own.

Other names
In Turkish su kabağı (water gourd), asma kabağı; English bottle gourd, calabash; in India "lauki", "ghia", "dudhi". Neither the European Medicines Agency nor the Turkish agency has a monograph for this species. The dried shell is used for vessels and musical instruments; the fruit is eaten as a vegetable.

Fruit (as a vegetable); dried shell (vessels and instruments)

In short

What is Bottle gourd?

Bottle gourd (Lagenaria siceraria (Molina) Standl.) is a plant of the Cucurbitaceae (gourd family) family. Traditions that use it: Africa, Asia and Siberia, Anatolia and the Mediterranean. Part used: fruit (as a vegetable); dried shell (vessels and instruments).

What is Bottle gourd used for?

Traditional use: A review published in Frontiers in Nutrition in 2022 writes that the Cucurbitaceae family has been used therapeutically since ancient times and that Lagenaria siceraria is among the most studied species of that family. Scientific evidence: traditional. In the sources relied on here there is no human study testing the plant on its own.

Is Bottle gourd safe?

Safety label: high risk. The vegetable, cooked and eaten, is safe. The real danger is in the juice of a bitter fruit. The 2012 task force report of the Indian Council of Medical Research recorded three deaths and 26 hospital admissions after people drank very bitter bottle gourd juice. Those admitted had abdominal pain, vomiting, diarrhoea and vomiting of blood; endoscopy showed widespread ulceration and bleeding in the stomach, oesophagus and duodenum, and more than half had low blood pressure. All three who died were over 59 and had had diabetes for a long time, and all three had said the juice was extremely bitter. The compounds responsible are the cucurbitacins that make cucurbits bitter, and there is no known antidote. The report’s advice is plain: bitter-tasting bottle gourd juice must not be drunk, and anyone who feels discomfort, nausea, vomiting or diarrhoea after drinking it should go to hospital without delay. Because there is no monograph there are no other official contraindications, and there are no data for pregnancy, lactation or drug interactions.

Traditional use

Worldwide — vessel, instrument and vegetable

A review published in Frontiers in Nutrition in 2022 writes that the Cucurbitaceae family has been used therapeutically since ancient times and that Lagenaria siceraria is among the most studied species of that family. The fruit is eaten as a low-calorie vegetable. The shell, once dried and hard, serves as a water vessel, a spoon and the body of an instrument — and it is this use that gives the plant its Turkish name.

India and South Asia — folk medicine

The 2012 report of the Indian Council of Medical Research records that bottle gourd juice is recommended by traditional healers for diabetes, high blood pressure, liver disease and weight loss. The safety note below concerns this use directly.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew’s records its range covers 119 regions; it is grown throughout the tropical and temperate zones. It is a fast-growing annual climber.
Harvest season
In Turkey it is grown as a summer crop; fruits for eating are picked while young, those for vessels only once they have dried completely on the vine.
Local names
Su kabağı, asma kabağı, sukabağı.

Mix-up risk

Pumpkins, squashes and courgettes (*Cucurbita* species) are a different genus; they do not give the vessels made from the dried shell of the bottle gourd. A bitter taste is a warning in every cucurbit: a bitter gourd or gourd juice, whatever the species, is not eaten or drunk.

Preparation

No official monograph sets an accepted dose; the fruit is a vegetable and is eaten cooked. Pressing and drinking its juice is a separate matter and should not be done without reading the warning in the safety note.

What science says

In the sources relied on here there is no human study testing the plant on its own. The 2022 review lists antioxidant, diuretic, hepatoprotective, analgesic and antihypertensive effects; these come from cell and animal studies. A randomised study published in 2025 tested a multi-herb Unani formulation that contains bottle gourd among other plants — the result of a mixture is not evidence for a single plant in it.
