Immortelle

Xeranthemum annuum L.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

Two different plants carry the same English name: *everlasting flower*. One is this; the other is *Helichrysum*. Both keep their colour after drying, and people gave both the same metaphor — the flower that does not die. Botany says two genera; language has one idea.

In short

What is Immortelle?

Immortelle (Xeranthemum annuum L.) is a plant of the Asteraceae (daisy family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe, Asia and Siberia. Part used: the flowering aerial parts.

What is Immortelle used for?

Traditional use: The outer bracts of this plant are dry and papery, so it keeps its shape and colour after drying and rustles in the hand. Scientific evidence: traditional. The evidence side of this plant is close to empty, and it is worth saying so plainly.

Is Immortelle safe?

Safety label: caution. ⚠️ Two cyanogenic compounds have been isolated from this plant. Cyanogenic glycosides can release cyanide when chewed or wetted; in this species the amounts, and what preparation does to them, are not known. For that reason it is not recommended as a drink. There is no data for pregnancy, lactation, children or people on medication. Nothing is known against its use as a dried ornamental.

Traditional use

Anatolia — the flower that holds when it dries

The outer bracts of this plant are dry and papery, so it keeps its shape and colour after drying and rustles in the hand. That is where its Turkish name comes from. It flowers on the steppe and in fallow fields, and is gathered for dried bunches.

Bingöl — an indicator plant of the honey

The denomination-of-origin registration counts it among the five taxa that distinguish Bingöl honey, and in the pollen table *Xeranthemum* sp. reaches dominant level. It is one of the composites that flower on the dry summer steppe — a genuine nectar source for bees.

Research — a little-studied species

A 2016 study in *Pharmaceutical Biology* describes the plant as an "ornamental and medicinal species with limited bioactivity and phytochemistry data" and assesses its anticholinesterase, antioxidant, analgesic and anti-inflammatory activity in the laboratory. ⚠️ The same study isolated two cyanogenic compounds from it — which is a safety finding in its own right.

In nature

Where it grows
A narrow range: south-eastern Europe, the Caucasus, Turkey, Lebanon, Syria and Iran. Steppe, fallow fields and stony slopes. ⚠️ Endangered in some countries; wild gathering should be measured.
Harvest season
In flower June to August; for drying it is cut fully open and hung.
Local names
Kağıtçiçeği, kâğıt çiçeği, solmaz çiçek, kuru çiçek.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ Ölmez çiçek (*Helichrysum arenarium*) is a different plant — calling both "everlasting flower" in English is the source of the confusion; *Helichrysum* has a Turkish monograph, this does not. ⚠️ The "paper flower" of the florist is sometimes *Xerochrysum bracteatum*, an Australian strawflower, not this species.

Preparation

No established preparation and no reliable dose could be found in the sources this site relies on. It is used dried, as an ornamental. No dose is offered for drinking it — given the cyanogenic finding, that gap matters.

What science says

The evidence side of this plant is close to empty, and it is worth saying so plainly. The one substantial study we have is from 2016 and says itself that bioactivity and phytochemistry data are limited. The anticholinesterase, antioxidant, analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects it measured are at the laboratory and animal level; no use has been tested in people. ⚠️ The study’s second finding is the more practical one: two cyanogenic compounds were isolated from the plant. Cyanogenic substances can release cyanide when chewed or steeped, and the amounts here, and the effect of preparation on them, are unknown. Evidence rating "traditional".

Safety

Caution

⚠️ Two cyanogenic compounds have been isolated from this plant. Cyanogenic glycosides can release cyanide when chewed or wetted; in this species the amounts, and what preparation does to them, are not known. For that reason it is not recommended as a drink. There is no data for pregnancy, lactation, children or people on medication. Nothing is known against its use as a dried ornamental.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) — theoreticalThe extract inhibits cholinesterase in the laboratory, so there is a theoretical additive risk for anyone on a drug with the same mechanism. No interaction has been shown in people.
General — no dataNo human drug-interaction study exists for this plant. Anyone on regular medication should ask a doctor before trying it.
