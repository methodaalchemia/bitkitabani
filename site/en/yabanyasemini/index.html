BT-245 · Solanaceae (patlıcangiller)

Bittersweet

Solanum dulcamara L.
Evidence: TraditionalGenerally safe

Its name misleads twice. It is called "wild jasmine" in Turkish yet has nothing to do with jasmine; it is a nightshade, kin to henbane and thornapple. Its Latin name dulcamara means "bittersweet" — whoever chews the stem tastes bitter first, then sweet. And the part the monograph approves is the stem, externally; the part children are poisoned by is the red berry. One of the clearest examples in this catalog of medicine and poison residing in separate organs of a single plant.

Other names
Turkish yaban yasemini (wild jasmine), acı tatlı, sofur; in English bittersweet, woody nightshade. In short

What is Bittersweet?

Bittersweet (Solanum dulcamara L.) is a plant of the Solanaceae (patlıcangiller) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe, Asia and Siberia. Part used: the 2–3-year-old stems gathered in spring before leaf development.

What is Bittersweet used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": symptomatic relief of mild recurrent eczema — cutaneous use only, the infusion or decoction applied at room temperature with a clean dressing. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no clinical trial of bittersweet in humans; recent publications are genome sequences, metabolite analyses and toxicology.

Is Bittersweet safe?

Safety label: generally safe. The red berries are poisonous, especially for children. According to the 2026 Planta Medica review, poisonings from bittersweet and black nightshade mostly follow berry ingestion and present as an anticholinergic syndrome (dry mouth, dilated pupils, palpitations, confusion); if berries have been eaten, call poison control. The monograph’s drug is the stem, applied to the skin only; the monograph gives no oral use and neither does this record. Hypersensitivity to the active substance can occur. See a doctor if symptoms worsen. Not recommended under 18, in pregnancy or lactation. We record the safety level as Low: use of the drug may be safe, but the plant itself is a poisonous one to be recognised in garden and countryside.

Traditional use

Europe — one indication

The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": symptomatic relief of mild recurrent eczema — cutaneous use only, the infusion or decoction applied at room temperature with a clean dressing. Not established under 18; two weeks. There is no oral use in the monograph.

Turkey

There is no monograph for bittersweet on the Turkish list. It is common in damp scrub, streamsides and hedges in Turkey, known by its purple flowers and glossy red berries. There are records of folk use for the skin, but this catalog does not print unsourced folk use.

Europe — an old eczema plant

Its stems have been used in central European pharmacy for centuries as "Stipites Dulcamarae" for eczema and skin disease; the Latin name means "bittersweet" — the chewed stem tastes bitter first, then sweet. The EU monograph approved only this external use.

In nature

Where it grows
Europe, western Asia, North Africa; damp scrub, streamsides, hedges. Common in Turkey.
Harvest season
Purple flowers from June to September, glossy red berries in autumn; the drug is gathered from the stems in spring, before the leaves.
Local names
Yaban yasemini, acı tatlı, sofur; no unsourced regional names given.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ The red berries can be mistaken for edible ones (currants, rose hips); they are poisonous. Black nightshade (Solanum nigrum) is its black-berried relative and is poisonous too. The stem drug should be bought as a pharmacy product.

Preparation

Cutaneous only: 1–2 g of comminuted stem as an infusion or decoction in 250 ml of water, applied at room temperature with a clean dressing to the affected area 1–5 times daily. See a doctor if symptoms last longer than two weeks or worsen. Not recommended under 18. Not taken by mouth.

What science says

There is no clinical trial of bittersweet in humans; recent publications are genome sequences, metabolite analyses and toxicology. The EU monograph rests the indication on long use. The toxicology, by contrast, is well documented: a comprehensive review published in 2026 in Planta Medica examines poisonings from deadly nightshade, bittersweet and black nightshade together — bittersweet carries glycoalkaloids such as solanine, solasodine and β-solamarine; the literature holds 41 studies reporting poisoning from these two Solanum species, mostly from eating the berries, with an anticholinergic syndrome. So the monograph’s drug (stem, external) and the source of poisoning (berry, oral) are two parts and two routes of one plant. The evidence level is Traditional.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing; with cutaneous use the expectation of systemic interaction is low. There is no Turkish monograph.
Anticholinergic drugs (only if berries are eaten)Not a monograph interaction but a poisoning note: because berry poisoning presents as an anticholinergic picture, drugs acting the same way (some antidepressants, antihistamines) can worsen it.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Bittersweet.
