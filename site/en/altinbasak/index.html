A single sentence in the Turkish monograph sums up a logic that holds for every diuretic plant: "fluid intake should be increased during use, but a low fluid intake is advised in people with cardiac or renal disease". In short

What is European goldenrod?

European goldenrod (Solidago virgaurea L.) is a plant of the Asteraceae (daisy family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the aerial parts.

What is European goldenrod used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": to increase the amount of urine as an adjuvant in minor urinary complaints. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no randomised clinical trial of goldenrod on its own.

Is European goldenrod safe?

Safety label: caution. Not to be used where fluid intake has to be restricted — in serious cardiac or renal disease, for instance. The reason is direct: the plant works by increasing urine output, which in those patients is an intervention in the wrong direction. Not for anyone hypersensitive to the active substance or to plants of the Asteraceae family — a large family, so people allergic to chamomile, chrysanthemum or sunflower should take care. Fluid intake must be increased during use. Hypersensitivity reactions and gastrointestinal complaints may occur; frequencies unknown. If fever, painful urination, spasms or blood in the urine occur, see a doctor — these can signal a urinary infection and should not be managed with a herbal product. The EU monograph does not recommend concomitant treatment with synthetic diuretics. Not recommended under 12, nor in pregnancy or lactation.

Traditional use

Europe — increasing the amount of urine

The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": to increase the amount of urine as an adjuvant in minor urinary complaints. The same logic as horsetail and restharrow in this catalog: the aim is flushing the urinary tract, and that only means anything alongside plenty of fluid.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the aerial parts" and balances a tension in a single sentence: fluid intake should be increased during use, but a low fluid intake is advised in people with cardiac or renal disease. The condition the plant needs in order to work is exactly what some patients must not do — which is why they do not use it at all.

Anatolia

Goldenrod grows in forest clearings and scrub in northern and western Turkey, recognisable by its yellow flower spikes in summer. A tea is made from it for the urinary tract in folk use, matching the monograph’s indication.

In nature

Where it grows
Forest clearings, scrub and dry meadows of Europe, North Africa and Asia. Found in the northern and western forests of Turkey.
Harvest season
Yellow flowers in spike-like clusters from July to September; the aerial parts are gathered in flower.
Local names
Altınbaşak, Yahudi otu — both appear in the Turkish monograph.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ Two confusions. The name: the Turkish monograph’s English name line reads "Java tea", but Java tea is Orthosiphon stamineus, a different plant. The species: the North American Solidago canadensis and S. gigantea have naturalised in Europe and Turkey, look very much like goldenrod and often grow mixed with it; the EU monograph covers S. virgaurea only.

Preparation

The monograph describes oral preparations of the aerial parts. The decisive condition is water: fluid intake must be increased to increase the amount of urine. Tinctures and extracts containing ethanol must carry the appropriate labelling. If symptoms persist or worsen, consult a doctor. Not recommended under 12.

What science says

There is no randomised clinical trial of goldenrod on its own. The human studies measure multi-ingredient mixtures containing it — for instance the 2026 Urologia trial described in the fireweed record, which compared a five-plant mixture including goldenrod with tamsulosin; there the significant difference in urinary symptoms favoured tamsulosin. A trial like that measures the mixture, not any single plant in it: what goldenrod itself contributed cannot be known. The rest of the recent literature is at laboratory level — chemical profiling, effects on Candida biofilm, permeability studies in gut microbiota. These show what is in the plant; they do not show whether it relieves a urinary complaint in a patient. The EU monograph rests its indication on long use. The one mechanism the monograph gives is an increase in the amount of urine, and that explains both the indication and the contraindication. The evidence level is Traditional.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Diuretic medicinesThe Turkish monograph reports interaction with diuretics; the EU monograph states that concomitant treatment with synthetic diuretics is not recommended. Both point the same way: two things working in the same direction can deepen fluid and electrolyte loss. Anyone on a diuretic should tell their doctor.
Multi-ingredient urinary productsNot an interaction warning but important in practice: goldenrod is usually sold in mixtures with restharrow, fireweed and bearberry rather than alone. Read the whole ingredient list, because the warnings apply separately to each plant.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing in its interaction section, but in its warnings it does not recommend concomitant use with synthetic diuretics.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying European goldenrod.
