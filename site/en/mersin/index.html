In short

What is Myrtle?

Myrtle (Myrtus communis) is a plant of the Myrtaceae family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean. Part used: leaves, berries.

What is Myrtle used for?

Traditional use: In Aegean and Mediterranean villages the leaves are boiled for coughs, diarrhoea and blood sugar, and the purple berries eaten. Scientific evidence: traditional. The antimicrobial effect of the leaf and oil has been shown in the lab.

Is Myrtle safe?

Safety label: generally safe. Leaf tea and berries are generally safe. Do not drink the essential oil or apply it to the faces of babies and young children.

Traditional use

Anatolia

In Aegean and Mediterranean villages the leaves are boiled for coughs, diarrhoea and blood sugar, and the purple berries eaten. In some places a myrtle sprig is placed on the bride’s head, and myrtle is planted on graves.

Ancient Mediterranean

Sacred to Aphrodite; bridal crowns were made of myrtle, and commanders who won without battle were crowned with it.

In nature

Where it grows
Coastal maquis and stream beds of the Aegean, Mediterranean and Marmara.
Harvest season
Leaves in early summer, berries November–December.
Local names
mersin, murt, hambeles

Mix-up risk

Do not confuse its purple berries with poisonous maquis berries; crushed myrtle leaves give a distinct, pleasant smell.

Preparation

Leaves are steeped or boiled; berries are eaten fresh or made into liqueur.

What science says

The antimicrobial effect of the leaf and oil has been shown in the lab. There are small clinical studies in sinusitis and mouth ulcers; evidence is limited.

Safety

Generally safe

Leaf tea and berries are generally safe. Do not drink the essential oil or apply it to the faces of babies and young children.
