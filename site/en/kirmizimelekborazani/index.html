BT-319 · Solanaceae (nightshade family)

Red angel’s trumpet

Brugmansia sanguinea (Ruiz & Pav.) D.Don
Evidence: TraditionalHigh risk

The species appears in older literature as *Datura sanguinea*, and that is not merely a naming detail: because the studies that measured this species’ alkaloids in the 1950s and 1960s were published under that name, anyone searching under the current name will not find them. Modern literature records phytochemical data for *B. sanguinea* as "not reported" — part of that gap is real, and part of it is the change of name.

Other names
It has no established Turkish name; "kırmızı melek borazanı" is the descriptive name used here. Red wandug, floripondio rojo and guando rojo in the Andean countries; older botanical writing uses the name Datura sanguinea, so the older literature has to be searched under that name. Neither the European Medicines Agency nor Türkiye’s TİTCK has a monograph for this species. This is not a treatment option; the plant carries tropane alkaloids and is poisonous.

In short

What is Red angel’s trumpet?

Red angel’s trumpet (Brugmansia sanguinea (Ruiz & Pav.) D.Don) is a plant of the Solanaceae (nightshade family) family. Traditions that use it: Andes. Part used: flowers and leaves.

Is Red angel’s trumpet safe?

Safety label: high risk. This plant is poisonous and is not a treatment option. The tropane alkaloids it carries (scopolamine, hyoscyamine, atropine) cause anticholinergic poisoning. Two large published series show the picture. Among 203 cases compiled from the 1986–2015 records of the Taiwan poison control centre (114 *Datura*, 89 *Brugmansia suaveolens*), the most frequent findings were mydriasis (53.2%), confusion (40%), tachycardia (35.5%), dry mouth (35.5%), dizziness (34%), dry skin (32.5%) and delirium (31%); 73 cases (36%) were severe and none of them died. Among 33 *Brugmansia* cases reported in Australia between 1990 and 2000 (the plant was taken to be "presumed *Brugmansia*" from the patient’s own description), mydriasis occurred in 100% (mean 29 hours) and delirium in 88% (mean 18 hours); there were no deaths, no seizures and no arrhythmias other than tachycardia. So no deaths were reported in these two large series. That should not be read as "deaths do not happen", for two reasons. First, deaths have been reported outside those series: in a forensic case from 2002 a person who drank a decoction of angel’s trumpet flowers drowned accidentally while under the influence of tropane alkaloids, and a 2017 review states that in severe cases "deaths have been reported". Second, neither series was designed to measure mortality — one is a series of calls to a poison centre, the other of admissions to a single toxicology unit; they do not capture pre-hospital deaths. Severe and atypical courses do exist, however: ischaemic stroke with seizure, a fulminant Guillain-Barré syndrome reported from Türkiye, and cases of severe self-harm. The seeds are the most toxic part, but the leaves carry significant toxicity too; in one case report a 60-year-old woman drank 350 ml of a herbal beverage she had made from leaves in her garden, not knowing what the plant was, and developed urinary retention, mydriasis and sinus tachycardia — she did not eat the leaves, she drank a drink made from them; another family member who took 50 ml of the same drink had mild confusion, which resolved on its own. In the published series, physostigmine brought an earlier resolution of central nervous system effects with no adverse events reported — that is treatment information and a physician’s decision. The distinction between species matters: almost all the cases above belong to *B. suaveolens*, and no human poisoning case attributed to *B. sanguinea* could be found. That does not mean this species is safer — it means species-specific data is absent. The plant is grown as an ornamental in many countries and the public does not generally know it carries tropane alkaloids; in one case the petals were eaten as a garnish. There is no assessment for pregnancy, breastfeeding or children, and none is conceivable. Keep out of reach of children. Safety level "high risk".

Traditional use

Southern Ecuador — ritual use, applied externally

A 2014 field study in the Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine records that healers among the Saraguro of southern Ecuador call *Brugmansia* species wandug or floripondios, and that four species are found in the area: the red-flowered red wandug (*B. sanguinea*), the yellow-flowered *B. aurea*, the white-flowered *B. candida* and the pink-flowered *B. suaveolens*. In the same study’s table, the entry for *B. sanguinea* reads: "They are used on humans and animals. These species are applied externally." For comparison, *B. suaveolens* is "taken in baths" and *B. candida* used "to treat rheumatic pain".

Regarded as dangerous

The same study relays a Kichwa myth: a goddess, insulted by a vain man, puts a powder made of red wandug leaves and flowers into his drink, and he loses his voice forever — "as this plant is considered very dangerous and may have negative effects if used inappropriately". The authors also record that the flower is added to the San Pedro cactus when it does not produce the desired effect, noting that this use is not general among the Saraguro healers.

In nature

Where it grows
Kew records a range of 12 regions, five of them native: Bolivia, northern Chile, Colombia, Ecuador and Peru. The other seven are introduced: Costa Rica, two regions of Mexico, two regions of New Zealand, the Chatham Islands and the Juan Fernández Islands. (Venezuela does not appear in Kew’s list.) It grows in the high, humid belt of the Andes, and is cultivated as an ornamental elsewhere in the world.
Harvest season
It does not grow naturally in Türkiye; there is no harvest calendar and gathering it does not arise.
Local names
No established name in Turkish. Local names: red wandug, floripondio rojo, guando rojo.

Mix-up risk

Confusion with other species of the same genus is the most important matter on this record, and the distinguishing feature is flower colour: *B. sanguinea* is red, *B. suaveolens* pink, *B. candida* white and *B. aurea* yellow. There is a second *Brugmansia* record on this site under the name "toe"; it is framed not at species level but at genus level, as "*Brugmansia suaveolens*, *B. arborea* and others", and its safety level is also high risk. Almost all published human poisoning cases belong to *B. suaveolens*. The genus is also confused with *Datura*; *Datura* is herbaceous with upward-facing flowers, while *Brugmansia* is woody with flowers that hang down.

Preparation

This page does not describe how it is prepared. The plant carries tropane alkaloids, is poisonous, and has no dose accepted in any official monograph. Writing a preparation method would mean writing out the preparation of a substance of unknown dose that can cause severe anticholinergic poisoning, so it is not written. Ritual use is described on this page as a cultural record, not as a recommendation. When handling the plant, wash the hands and avoid contact with the eyes.

What science says

There is no efficacy study in humans for this species, and honesty requires saying more: there is almost no species-specific modern phytochemical data either. A 2022 inventory of Ecuadorian medicinal plants in Plants writes, verbatim, "No information is reported in literature" in the phytochemistry row for *B. sanguinea* — while the same table does carry alkaloid data for its relative *B. candida*. That the species produces scopolamine has been shown at the enzyme level: the hyoscyamine-6β-hydroxylase gene that catalyses the last two steps of scopolamine biosynthesis was isolated directly from *B. sanguinea* in 2018. But that is not a concentration measurement, and this page gives no numerical alkaloid concentration for the species. For a sense of magnitude, a genus-level measurement offers a guide: a 2022 analysis measured 1,771 mg/kg of scopolamine in the flowers and 297 mg/kg in the leaves of the related *B. versicolor*. Against that, the group acute reference dose set by EFSA for the sum of hyoscyamine and scopolamine is only 0.016 µg/kg body weight, based on a decrease in heart rate. Setting the two figures side by side shows the difference in order of magnitude. Evidence level "traditional".

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Anticholinergic medicines (antihistamines, tricyclic antidepressants, some bladder and gastrointestinal drugs)The effects add up, raising the risk of anticholinergic toxicity and delirium.
Dextromethorphan, diphenhydramine and similar over-the-counter medicinesIn reported cases involving this genus such combinations have accompanied severe courses.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Red angel’s trumpet.

Legal status

The plant itself is not on any international control list, and the scopolamine and atropine it carries are active substances of licensed medicines; those are two separate matters. The position differs from country to country. This is not a legal opinion; what follows is only what the texts list. The Louisiana statute in the United States that enumerates hallucinogenic plants names Brugmansia arborea of this genus individually; the same list carries Datura spp. at genus level and Methysticodendron amesianum as well — the latter being, according to Kew, a synonym of *Brugmansia × candida*, while this species’ own former name was *Datura sanguinea*, so how the naming should be read is open to argument. The same statute exempts possession, planting, cultivation and harvest "strictly for aesthetic, landscaping, or decorative purposes". On the Turkish side it has to be said what was searched, because "there is no restriction" and "it does not appear on this list" are not the same statement. *Brugmansia* appears neither in the 1961 Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs, nor in the 1971 Convention on Psychotropic Substances, nor in the CITES appendices. Three official Turkish lists were checked. (1) TİTCK’s list of medicinal plant monographs: this species is confirmed absent from it. (2) TİTCK’s list of 38 items banned from sale outside pharmacies: *Brugmansia* does not appear among the 38, whereas most of the tropane-alkaloid drugs of the same family are named — thorn apple leaf and flower (*Folia Stramonii*, *Flores Stramonii*), deadly nightshade (*Folia* and *Herba Belladonnae*), henbane (*Folia Hyoscyami*) and mandrake (*Radix Mandragorae*). (3) The Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry’s poisonous plant list (plants treated as poisonous for food use): the file of that list could not be opened for this page, so nothing is said about it. The genus’s absence from the prohibition lists, in other words, comes not from its being treated as harmless but from the way those lists name drugs one by one.

Laws differ by country; check local rules before travelling with or buying any plant product.
