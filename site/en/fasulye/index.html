BT-230 · Fabaceae (legume family)

Common bean

Phaseolus vulgaris L.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

The part approved on the official list is the part thrown away in the kitchen: the pod with its seeds removed. The monograph’s Latin name says so outright — "fructus sine semine", fruit without seed. The bean that is eaten is not on the list as a drug at all; and raw, it is poisonous. So three states of one plant carry entirely different meanings: the casing is a herbal drug, the seed a staple food, the raw seed a cause of poisoning. And there is a fourth: an extract of the seed, sold as a weight supplement, which is where the evidence belongs.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the Turkish name as "sarık fasulyesi" and lists no synonyms; that is an unusual spelling, different from "fasulye", the plant’s ordinary Turkish name, which is what this catalog uses. In English: green bean pod, common bean, kidney bean, navy bean, pinto bean, snap bean, string bean, wax bean. The approved drug is the pod with the seeds removed (fructus sine semine).

In short

What is Common bean?

Common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) is a plant of the Fabaceae (legume family) family. Traditions that use it: North America, Andes. Part used: the fruit (the pod with the seeds removed).

What is Common bean used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": to increase the amount of urine in order to flush the urinary tract, as an adjuvant in minor urinary tract complaints. Scientific evidence: limited. There is no clinical trial of bean pod in the monograph indication — flushing of the urinary tract; the EU monograph rests the indication on long use, and the effect depends largely on an increased volume of urine, that is on the water drunk.

Is Common bean safe?

Safety label: caution. Raw or undercooked beans are poisonous — not in the monograph, but impossible to leave out of a record on beans, because the monograph’s drug is the pod while the risk at home is in the seed. The seed contains a lectin called phytohaemagglutinin, which causes acute poisoning if not cooked enough. A study published in 2023 in Toxins documents an outbreak at a 1,700-person canteen in France in 2018 that affected 200 people: symptoms began a median of 3.3 hours after the meal, the source was traced to a chili con carne containing undercooked beans, and the phytohaemagglutinin level in the dish was measured above the dose considered toxic. A case report published in 2026 in the International Journal of Emergency Medicine describes a severe case in an eight-year-old that progressed to hypovolaemic shock and kidney injury. The practical conclusion: beans should be soaked and boiled thoroughly, and partly cooked beans can be more dangerous than raw ones. The monograph’s own warnings are these: people with diabetes should use it under medical supervision (risk of hypoglycaemia); not recommended under 18, in pregnancy or lactation; not to be used longer than two weeks.

Traditional use

Europe — one indication

The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": to increase the amount of urine in order to flush the urinary tract, as an adjuvant in minor urinary tract complaints. The approved drug is the pod with the seeds removed — that is, not the bean that is eaten but its casing. Not recommended under 18.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph lists the same drug and adds one warning: because the herbal drug contains arginine, silicic acid and chromium salts, it should be used under medical supervision by people with diabetes, owing to the risk of hypoglycaemia. The only drug interaction it reports is with antidiabetic medicines.

The Americas and Anatolia

The bean is native to the Americas; it was domesticated twice independently, in Mesoamerica and in the Andes, and reached Anatolia only after the 16th century. That it is now one of the most basic vegetables of Turkish cooking shows how thoroughly a plant can be naturalised over five hundred years; "kuru fasulye" is an American plant turned into an Anatolian dish.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to Mesoamerica and the Andes; now grown everywhere. In Turkey it is a widespread crop both as fresh beans and as dried beans.
Harvest season
A summer crop; fresh pods through the summer, dried beans in autumn.
Local names
The Turkish monograph prints "sarık fasulyesi"; the plant’s ordinary Turkish name is fasulye, fresh as taze fasulye and dried as kuru fasulye.

Mix-up risk

The confusion is at the level of part: the approved drug is the seedless pod, the thing sold as a weight supplement is a seed extract, and the thing eaten is the seed. ⚠️ Raw or undercooked seeds are poisonous because of phytohaemagglutinin. The Turkish word "fasulye" can also run together with other legumes such as barbunya, börülce and bakla; cowpea (Vigna) and broad bean (Vicia faba) are separate genera.

Preparation

The EU monograph gives a single dose for adults: 2.5 g of the comminuted drug infused in 150 ml of boiling water, 2–6 times daily. Because the indication is flushing of the urinary tract, drinking plenty of fluid is part of the treatment. If symptoms persist longer than two weeks or worsen, consult a doctor or pharmacist. Not recommended under 18.

What science says

There is no clinical trial of bean pod in the monograph indication — flushing of the urinary tract; the EU monograph rests the indication on long use, and the effect depends largely on an increased volume of urine, that is on the water drunk. Evidence does exist, however, in a quite different field, for an extract made from the plant’s seed: a systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2026 in Nutrition Research pooled eight randomised trials (543 people) of white kidney bean extract (rich in α-amylase inhibitors) in overweight and obese adults, and found reductions of 1.62 kg in weight (95% CI −1.99 to −1.25), 0.58 kg/m² in body mass index, 1.17 kg in fat mass and 1.58 cm in waist circumference; no significant difference in fasting glucose, triglycerides or insulin, and no serious adverse events reported. The authors call for larger and longer trials. Two things need separating: the effects are real but small, and what was measured is a seed extract, not the pod that is the monograph’s drug. Two parts of one plant, two purposes, two bodies of evidence. The evidence level is Limited.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Antidiabetic medicinesThe only interaction the Turkish monograph reports. Its reason is written into the monograph: the drug contains arginine, silicic acid and chromium salts and creates a risk of hypoglycaemia in people with diabetes. That the seed extract inhibits α-amylase points the same way: something that slows the digestion of starch, taken alongside a glucose-lowering medicine, can take glucose down too far.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing in its interaction section.
Not attributing seed-extract studies to the podNot a drug interaction, but important for the record: the weight studies were done with a seed extract. A tea of the pod does not inherit that evidence.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Common bean.
