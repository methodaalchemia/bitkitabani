Concept of the day

Icaro

The healing songs that healers in the Peruvian Amazon believe they learn from plant spirits.

The concept’s plantMapachoNicotiana rusticaIn the Amazon tobacco is not a vice but a sacred teacher plant. In Anatolia the same plant became an everyday pleasure.Plant page →

Healers who work with plants in the Peruvian Amazon believe that much of their knowledge comes through songs. These melodies, called “icaros”, are taught to them by the spirits of plants in visions or dreams during the dieta. According to the anthropologist Luis Eduardo Luna, who worked with four mestizo healers around Iquitos in the early 1980s, the number and strength of a healer’s icaros are the best measure of his knowledge and power [1].

Icaros have many functions: strengthening or weakening visions, calling protective spirits, curing specific illnesses, reinforcing a medicinal plant, treating snakebite, awakening love, calling rain or wind. Some protect the healer from rival sorcerers. One of the healers interviewed knew 60–70 icaros; the others said they knew over a hundred [1].

Icaros are sung mostly in Quechua, sometimes in Indigenous languages such as Cocama or Omagua, or a mixture. The healers say they learned these languages not from Indigenous people but from the plant spirits; singing in a native language carries power and prestige [1]. Icaros are often sung while blowing the smoke of Mapacho tobacco.

Shipibo-Konibo healers also say that the mother of tobacco taught them the icaros and how to blow smoke over the patient [2]. This is a cultural record, not a recommendation for ritual use.

Sources

  1. Luis Eduardo Luna, “The concept of plants as teachers among four mestizo shamans of Iquitos, northeastern Peru”, Journal of Ethnopharmacology 11(2), 1984
  2. Ximena Jauregui ve ark., ““Plantas con madre”: Plants that teach and guide in the shamanic initiation process in the east-central Peruvian Amazon”, Journal of Ethnopharmacology 134(3), 2011
