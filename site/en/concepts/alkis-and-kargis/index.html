Alkış and kargış

The old Turkic belief in the power of the spoken word: alkış is blessing and praise, kargış is curse.

The concept’s plantSilver birchBetula pendulaIn Central Asian Turkic “kayın” means birch; in Anatolia the name passed to a different tree, the beech (Fagus). Birch bark owes its whiteness to a compound called betulin.Plant page →

Alkış and kargış come from the Old Turkic verbs alka- (to praise, to pray) and karga- (to curse). Kaşgarlı Mahmud defines alkış as praise, prayer and recounting a person’s virtues, and kargış as a curse, giving the example “God’s curse be upon him” [1].

In Irk Bitig a man asks God for kut on his way and receives the alkış “may there be horses in your fold, may your life be long” [1]. In Sakha (Yakut) epics alkış is used mostly to voice wishes, to praise and to keep evil away, and kargış in moments of danger, against a threatening force or to voice grief. Scholars link the genre to the wish to control nature through words, and to the passage from chaos back to order [2].

Trees were addressed with alkış too: in the Altai the Kaç kam called on the “gold-leafed, eight-shadowed holy birch” and offered it a white lamb, and Sagay, Shor and Teleut kams would not perform a rite without a Silver birch [3].

A curse aimed at plants appears in Dede Korkut: Boğaç Han’s mother curses Kazılık Mountain, where her son was struck down, wishing that its grass would no longer grow. In nomadic culture, wishing someone’s grass to dry up meant wishing them dead [4]. Anatolian sayings such as “may your hearth go out” or “may your road be open” are living traces of this tradition.
