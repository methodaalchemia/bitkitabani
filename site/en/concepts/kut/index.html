Kut and ıduk

Fortune and life force believed to pass from the Sky God to chosen people and beings; ıduk is what is “holy, blessed”.

The concept’s plantJuniperJuniperus excelsaGin takes its name and flavour from juniper berries (Dutch jenever).Plant page →

In Kaşgarlı Mahmud’s dictionary kut means “fortune, rule”: if God gives His servant kut, his affairs always rise. The word’s meaning takes in life, spiritual strength, courage, happiness and good luck. In Turkic belief kut comes from the Sky God and passes to chosen people and beings; it was thought to be handed down by blood to rulers, clan heads, healers, kams and dedes [1].

In the Old Turkic omen book Irk Bitig kut “comes” and is “given” like a concrete thing: a man on his way to a visit meets God and asks for kut, and God blesses him, “may there be horses in your fold, may your life be long” [2]. Among the Sakha, kut is the life-giving force in people, animals and plants [3].

Iduk means “holy, blessed”. The “ıduk yer-sub” (holy earth-water) of the Orkhon inscriptions refers both to protective spirits and to the homeland [4]. Iduk is also used for an animal dedicated to God and set free: it is not loaded, milked or shorn. In the Altai the kam tied a white cloth round the neck of the winning race horse and offered it as ıduk [5].

Trees carry this holiness too: the Yakuts believed every kam had his own tree, which grew and died with him [6]; the Bashkirs would not break a Juniper branch without making a vow [7]. The continuation of Anatolian ocaks by “giving the hand” is also explained as kut passing by blood and by hand [1].
