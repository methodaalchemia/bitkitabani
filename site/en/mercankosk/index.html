Marjoram

Origanum majorana L.

The monograph’s second indication is unique in this catalog: irritated skin around the nostrils — for the skin reddened by constant nose-wiping in a cold, from the age of one. The infant product known in Europe as "Majoranbutter" or marjoram ointment comes from that tradition. The regulator turned so narrow a use into a monograph; the plant’s only clinical trials, meanwhile, are on Parkinson’s. A plant’s official life and its research life may never touch.

In short

What is Marjoram?

Marjoram (Origanum majorana L.) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the aerial parts.

What is Marjoram used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places both under "traditional use": (1) mild spasmodic gastro-intestinal complaints such as bloating and flatulence — as tea, before meals; (2) irritated skin around the nostrils — as an ointment, and in that indication usable from the age of one. Scientific evidence: limited. There is no clinical trial of marjoram in the monograph indications; but there are two single-ingredient, placebo-controlled pilot trials, both in an entirely different field: Parkinson’s disease.

Is Marjoram safe?

Safety label: high risk. Not for anyone hypersensitive to the mint family. The ointment must not be pushed deep into the nostril — the monograph states it can reduce the activity of the ciliary epithelium (the hair-bearing cells that clean the nose); around the nostril, not inside. Touching the eyes with unwashed hands after application can cause irritation. See a doctor if signs of skin infection appear. Tea not recommended under 18; not in pregnancy or lactation. No adverse effects known; none reported in the Parkinson’s pilot either. A safe spice at kitchen doses.

Traditional use

Europe — two indications

The EMA monograph places both under "traditional use": (1) mild spasmodic gastro-intestinal complaints such as bloating and flatulence — as tea, before meals; (2) irritated skin around the nostrils — as an ointment, and in that indication usable from the age of one. The monograph asks that the ointment not be pushed deep into the nostril: it can reduce the activity of the ciliary epithelium.

Turkey

There is no separate monograph for marjoram on the Turkish Medicinal Plant List. In Turkey it is known as a kitchen herb, grown in the Aegean and Mediterranean, its dried leaf sold as a spice.

Mediterranean cooking

Marjoram is an old garden plant of the Mediterranean and Near East, mentioned in Egyptian and Greek sources as a plant of happiness and marriage. Today a staple of German, Italian and Middle Eastern cooking; the monograph’s "tea before meals" indication is an extension of its place in the kitchen.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to the Mediterranean and Near East; grown in the Aegean and Mediterranean regions of Turkey, frost-tender.
Harvest season
Small white flowers in summer; the aerial parts are gathered before and during flowering.
Local names
Mercanköşk, bahçe kekiği; no unsourced regional names given.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ Its name and look run together with oregano (Origanum vulgare, Thymus); marjoram is sweeter, oregano sharper. The monograph is for O. majorana only; mixtures sold as "kekik" do not stand in for it.

Preparation

Digestion: 2–4 g of the comminuted drug infused in 150 ml of boiling water, 1–2 times daily before meals. Around the nostrils: a small amount of ointment spread around the nostrils 2–4 times daily; not deep inside, and no touching the eyes with unwashed hands afterwards. See a doctor if no better within two weeks for digestion or one week for skin. Tea not recommended under 18, ointment not under one.

What science says

There is no clinical trial of marjoram in the monograph indications; but there are two single-ingredient, placebo-controlled pilot trials, both in an entirely different field: Parkinson’s disease. A double-blind study published in 2021 in Parkinsonism & Related Disorders gave 60 medicated Parkinson’s patients marjoram tea or placebo for 30 days (51 completed): no significant difference in the motor score (UPDRS III, p=0.069), significant improvement reported in non-motor symptoms and on a depression scale. A second pilot, published in 2025 in Neuroscience, gave the same tea for 30 days and measured antioxidant enzymes in blood — a biomarker, not a patient outcome. Two pilots, small samples, the same team, an indication outside the monograph: the evidence level is Limited, and for the digestive and skin uses the monograph approves this evidence means nothing.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing; there is no Turkish monograph. In the Parkinson’s trial patients continued their medication, levodopa included, and no interaction was reported — but a 30-day pilot is not designed to measure interactions.
