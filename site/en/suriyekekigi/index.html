Syrian oregano (za’atar)

Origanum syriacum L.
Evidence: LimitedGenerally safe

A 2019 study (Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine) argues that only five species are mentioned directly as medicinal plants in the Bible, and that of these “hyssop” is Origanum syriacum; the other four are fig, nard, balm of Gilead and mandrake.

Other names
Arabic za‘tar (zaatar); English Syrian oregano, Lebanese oregano, Bible hyssop. In some publications it appears under the name Majorana syriaca. The Kew world plant checklist recognises three subspecies: subsp. syriacum, subsp. bevanii and subsp. sinaicum. The same name “zahter / za‘tar” is also used for Thymbra spicata; that species is a separate record in the catalogue. It has no monograph on the EMA or TİTCK lists.

What is Syrian oregano (za’atar)?

Syrian oregano (za’atar) (Origanum syriacum L.) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Asia and Siberia. Part used: leaves and flowering tops; the essential oil.

What is Syrian oregano (za’atar) used for?

Traditional use: According to the 2021 review in the journal Plants, za‘tar is the name both of the plant and of the mix made from it: the dried and ground leaves and flowers are mixed with sesame, sumac and salt. Scientific evidence: limited. The data on the plant’s effects come from laboratory and animal studies; the 2022 review reports no clinical trial carried out in humans.

Is Syrian oregano (za’atar) safe?

Safety label: generally safe. It is a plant widely consumed as a spice. The 2022 review reports that in toxicity studies in animals consumption of the plant showed no adverse effects; it does not, however, report any safety study carried out in humans. There are no data on its use for medicinal purposes or as an essential oil in pregnancy, breastfeeding or children. The pure essential oil is a product concentrated in thymol and carvacrol and is not the same thing as the spice. Ready-made za‘tar mixes contain sesame; this matters for people with a sesame allergy. It is appropriate for people who take regular medication not to use concentrated extracts and oils without consulting a doctor.

Traditional use

Levant — the za‘tar mix and man‘ousheh

According to the 2021 review in the journal Plants, za‘tar is the name both of the plant and of the mix made from it: the dried and ground leaves and flowers are mixed with sesame, sumac and salt. At breakfast the mix is spread together with olive oil on a pizza-like dough called “man‘ousheh”, and it is also used as a seasoning in cooking. According to the 2017 za‘tar standard of the FAO–WHO Codex Alimentarius, “raw broadleaf za‘tar” must contain at least 75 per cent Origanum syriacum.

Eastern Mediterranean — folk medicine

The 2022 review in the journal Molecules writes that the plant is widely used in the cuisines of Lebanon, Syria, Jordan, Egypt, Palestine and Türkiye. Its traditional medical uses include relieving stomach and intestinal pain, and colds, coughs and toothache. It is also drunk as a herbal tea mixed with other herbs; its taste is sharp and bitter.

Palestine — the most cited wild edible plant

A 2008 ethnobotanical study carried out with 190 informants in the northern West Bank recorded 100 wild edible plant species; this species, referred to by the name Majorana syriaca, was among the most quoted plants in almost all areas. According to the 2021 review, the cultivated area in the northern West Bank has risen from 500 dunams before 2010 to more than 6000 dunams.

Türkiye — the forests of Dörtyol

A 2003 study on the species in Türkiye collected the plant from the state forests of the Dörtyol district of Hatay. The Kew world plant checklist counts Türkiye within the native range of the species.

In nature

Where it grows
Its native range extends from the Eastern Mediterranean to the Arabian Peninsula: Türkiye, Cyprus, Lebanon-Syria, Palestine, Sinai and Saudi Arabia (Kew). It is an aromatic perennial plant 60–90 cm tall, with woody roots and hairy stems. Because it is over-collected from the wild in Lebanon, bringing it into cultivation has been studied; irrigation once every two weeks in the dry period was found to increase herb and essential oil yield.
Harvest season
Wild Origanum species are cut once or twice a year, at the flowering stage. In its cultivation in Palestine it has been reported that a harvest is taken on average every 40 days and that one planting keeps producing for 7 years.
Local names
No Turkish local name is given in the sources; its Arabic name is za‘tar (zaatar).

Mix-up risk

“Za‘tar” is not the name of a single plant. According to the Codex standard, raw za‘tar may consist of the leaves and flowers of species from the genera Origanum, Thymbra, Thymus and Satureja; in broadleaf za‘tar the share of species such as Thymbra spicata may be at most 25 per cent. According to the Kew list, the name “Origanum syriacum subsp. dubium” is a synonym of Origanum majorana (sweet marjoram).

Preparation

The leaves and flowering tops are dried, crumbled and used as a spice; in the za‘tar mix they are combined with sesame, sumac and salt. The 2021 review notes that air-drying at low temperature preserves the quality of the product better than oven-drying. In a study in Lebanon, too, the air-dried product gave the desired flavour, aroma and colour attributes. Because there is no monograph for this species on the EMA or TİTCK lists, there is no accepted dose for medicinal use.

What science says

The data on the plant’s effects come from laboratory and animal studies; the 2022 review reports no clinical trial carried out in humans. 121 compounds have been identified in the essential oil; the most frequently reported are thymol, γ-terpinene, carvacrol and α-pinene, and the proportions vary with the place of growth, the harvest season and the distillation method. In the Dörtyol sample 49.02 per cent of the oil was monoterpenes, 36.60 per cent oxygenated monoterpenes and 12.59 per cent sesquiterpenes; the oil was active against 13 of the 16 microorganisms tested. The review summarises antioxidant, antimicrobial, antiparasitic and anti-inflammatory effects, as well as in vitro effects in breast cancer and leukaemia cells; in one study a methanol extract inhibited the release of IL-6 from human blood cells by more than 80 per cent. These are test-tube and animal findings; its efficacy in the treatment of any disease has not been shown in humans.

Safety

Generally safe

It is a plant widely consumed as a spice. The 2022 review reports that in toxicity studies in animals consumption of the plant showed no adverse effects; it does not, however, report any safety study carried out in humans. There are no data on its use for medicinal purposes or as an essential oil in pregnancy, breastfeeding or children. The pure essential oil is a product concentrated in thymol and carvacrol and is not the same thing as the spice. Ready-made za‘tar mixes contain sesame; this matters for people with a sesame allergy. It is appropriate for people who take regular medication not to use concentrated extracts and oils without consulting a doctor.
