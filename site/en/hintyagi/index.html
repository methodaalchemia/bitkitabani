BT-246 · Euphorbiaceae (spurge family)

Castor oil plant

Ricinus communis L.
Evidence: ClinicalGenerally safe

Two things come from the same seed: on one side the world’s oldest and best-known purgative, on the other ricin, one of the most poisonous plant substances known. The distinction is hidden in physics — ricin is a water-soluble protein and does not pass into oil; in cold pressing it stays in the cake. So the spoonful of castor oil forced on a generation’s childhood was safe, while the same plant’s seed in the garden was not. The EU monograph counts the oil as well-established use, and this catalog also places the plant in the poison category: both are right.

Other names
Turkish hintyağı bitkisi, keneotu, kene ağacı; in English castor oil plant, castor bean. There is no Turkish monograph; the record rests on the EU monograph and PubMed. The drug is the fixed oil obtained from the seed by cold pressing (Ricini oleum); ⚠️ the seed itself is lethally poisonous — the toxin (ricin) does not pass into the oil on pressing, it stays in the cake.

In short

What is Castor oil plant?

Castor oil plant (Ricinus communis L.) is a plant of the Euphorbiaceae (spurge family) family. Traditions that use it: Africa, Asia and Siberia. Part used: the fixed oil obtained from the seeds by cold pressing.

What is Castor oil plant used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places castor oil in the "well-established use" category, rare in this catalog: a short-term laxative for occasional constipation. Scientific evidence: clinical. Castor oil sits in the EU monograph’s "well-established use" category: its laxative effect and mechanism — ricinoleic acid reducing fluid and electrolyte absorption and stimulating peristalsis — are settled at pharmacology-textbook level.

Is Castor oil plant safe?

Safety label: generally safe. The seed is lethally poisonous. It carries ricin — according to a comprehensive review published in 2005 in JAMA one of the most potent plant toxins known; a few chewed and swallowed seeds can cause severe poisoning, and there is no specific antidote. Ricin does not pass into the oil on pressing; but because the seed itself turns up as a garden ornamental and as beads in jewellery, it is a real risk for children. Contraindicated in pregnancy (it can influence labour) and in lactation (it may pass into milk). Not for use in intestinal obstruction or stenosis, atony, appendicitis, inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn’s, ulcerative colitis), abdominal pain of unknown origin or severe dehydration with water and electrolyte depletion. Long-term use of laxatives should be avoided; if a laxative is needed every day the cause of constipation should be investigated — prolonged use impairs bowel function. Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain and severe diarrhoea may occur. Not recommended under 18.

Traditional use

Europe — well-established use

The EMA monograph places castor oil in the "well-established use" category, rare in this catalog: a short-term laxative for occasional constipation. A single daily dose of 2–5 g (2.1–5.3 ml); usually 2–3 times a week is enough; onset in 2–6 hours. Contraindicated in pregnancy and lactation — because it can influence labour and may pass into milk.

Turkey

There is no monograph for castor oil on the Turkish list. It grows ornamentally and wild along Turkey’s Mediterranean coast; its oil has been sold in pharmacies as a laxative for centuries, a childhood memory for a generation.

Africa and India — an ancient oil plant

Native to East Africa and India; its seeds have been found in Egyptian tombs and its oil appears in the Ebers Papyrus. More than half of the oil goes to industry: paint, lubricants, cosmetics. The laxative effect comes from ricinoleic acid released in the gut — the monograph classes it as a contact laxative.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to East Africa and India; naturalised throughout the tropics and warm temperate zones. In Turkey it grows along the Mediterranean and Aegean coasts.
Harvest season
Spiny fruit capsules in summer; the seeds ripen in autumn. The oil is cold-pressed from the ripe seed.
Local names
Hintyağı bitkisi, keneotu, kene ağacı; no unsourced regional names given.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ The seeds, with their tick-like mottled look, are used as ornaments and beads; keep them from children. The Turkish name "hintyağı" can also be confused with other industrial oils such as tung oil.

Preparation

Adults: a single daily dose of 2–5 g (2.1–5.3 ml) of oil, orally; usually 2–3 times a week is enough. Onset within 2–6 hours. Use for more than a week requires medical supervision. The monograph is explicit: castor oil should be used only when a change of diet and bulk-forming agents have failed. Not recommended under 18.

What science says

Castor oil sits in the EU monograph’s "well-established use" category: its laxative effect and mechanism — ricinoleic acid reducing fluid and electrolyte absorption and stimulating peristalsis — are settled at pharmacology-textbook level. That is why we record the evidence level as Clinical; but with measure: modern randomised trials are few, because the effect is so old and so obvious. The plant’s contested use lies where the monograph contraindicates it: inducing labour. The 2013 Cochrane review pooled three small trials of 233 women, found no difference in caesarean rates, judged the methodological quality poor, and established one thing firmly: every woman who took castor oil felt nauseous (RR 59.9). A meta-analysis published in 2022 in the Journal of Pharmacopuncture reports an effect on Bishop score and onset of labour; but the quality of the journal and of the included studies is not at Cochrane level, and its "safe" conclusion conflicts with Cochrane’s findings. This catalog’s position is the monograph’s: not in pregnancy.

Safety

Generally safe

The seed is lethally poisonous. It carries ricin — according to a comprehensive review published in 2005 in JAMA one of the most potent plant toxins known; a few chewed and swallowed seeds can cause severe poisoning, and there is no specific antidote. Ricin does not pass into the oil on pressing; but because the seed itself turns up as a garden ornamental and as beads in jewellery, it is a real risk for children. Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Cardiac glycosides and antiarrhythmicsThe monograph states it directly: hypokalaemia (low potassium) resulting from long-term laxative abuse potentiates the action of cardiac glycosides and interacts with antiarrhythmic drugs. For someone on digoxin, a laxative habit is dangerous.
Diuretics, corticosteroids and liquorice rootThe monograph states these three may enhance potassium loss together with castor oil. That liquorice root is named here as a plant is notable: herbal products interact with one another too.
AntihistaminesAccording to the monograph, concomitant use may reduce the laxative action of castor oil.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Castor oil plant.
