Tiger nut

Cyperus esculentus L.
Evidence: TraditionalGenerally safe

The same tuber is both a prized food and one of the world’s hardest farm weeds to kill: a single tuber left in the soil can cover a whole field again.

Other names
Spanish "chufa"; English tiger nut, earth almond; Arabic "habb el-aziz". In Turkish yer bademi (earth almond) and topalak bademi. Despite the name it is no relative of the almond, and the part eaten is a tuber. Neither the European Medicines Agency nor the Turkish agency has a monograph for this species.

In short

What is Tiger nut?

Tiger nut (Cyperus esculentus L.) is a plant of the Cyperaceae (sedge family) family. Traditions that use it: Africa, Europe. Part used: underground tubers.

What is Tiger nut used for?

Traditional use: In the Valencia region of Spain the tubers are soaked and crushed to make a milky drink called "horchata de chufa". Scientific evidence: traditional. In the sources relied on here there is no controlled trial in humans.

Is Tiger nut safe?

Safety label: generally safe. The tuber is a food and has been eaten for centuries; the reviews relied on here report no serious safety problem. Because there is no monograph there is no official list of contraindications. It is high in fat and fibre, and eaten in quantity by someone unused to it can cause bloating. There is no separate assessment for pregnancy, lactation, children or drug interactions, so concentrated extracts of the tuber are not recommended.

Traditional use

Valencia — horchata de chufa

In the Valencia region of Spain the tubers are soaked and crushed to make a milky drink called "horchata de chufa". A review published in Foods in 2022 records milk, snacks, beverages and gluten-free bread being made from the tuber.

West and North Africa — the tuber eaten raw

The same review writes that the plant is a crop rich in lipids, starch, protein, fibre, vitamins and minerals, with a very long history of use as food and feed. The tubers are eaten raw after soaking, or dried for keeping.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew’s records its range covers 194 regions; it has spread through almost all of the temperate and hot zones. It is a sedge of damp, loose, sandy ground, forming its tubers a few centimetres below the surface.
Harvest season
The sources relied on here give no collection calendar for Turkey.
Local names
Yer bademi, topalak bademi.

Mix-up risk

It is the same genus as piri piri (*Cyperus articulatus*) but a different species; the use and the restrictions of one species do not belong to its relative. The "almond" in its name misleads too: it has no relation to the almond (*Prunus dulcis*), and for anyone with a nut allergy the tuber is not an almond product.

Preparation

No official monograph sets an accepted dose; the tiger nut is a food. Because they are hard, the tubers are soaked for a few hours before being eaten or crushed. The sources relied on here give no medicinal preparation or measure.

What science says

In the sources relied on here there is no controlled trial in humans. The 2022 review in Foods reports the tuber’s radical-scavenging ability, its inhibition of lipid peroxidation in vitro and anti-inflammatory properties — these are laboratory measurements. A review published in Plants the same year collects its composition, medicinal efficacy and antibacterial properties. Both reviews show that the plant is studied mainly as a foodstuff.

Safety

Generally safe

The tuber is a food and has been eaten for centuries; the reviews relied on here report no serious safety problem. Because there is no monograph there is no official list of contraindications. It is high in fat and fibre, and eaten in quantity by someone unused to it can cause bloating. There is no separate assessment for pregnancy, lactation, children or drug interactions, so concentrated extracts of the tuber are not recommended.
