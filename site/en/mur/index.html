Myrrh

Commiphora molmol Engler (C. myrrha (Nees) Engl.)
Evidence: LimitedCaution

The same resin has been both incense and medicine for two thousand years: carried from the Arabian Peninsula to the Mediterranean along the incense route, myrrh was burned in temples while being dabbed on mouth sores in pharmacies — and today the EU monograph approves exactly that second use. Its name comes from the Arabic murr, "bitter", and bitter it is. One of the few plants in this catalog where the incense category and a medical monograph meet in the same drug.

Other names
Turkish mür, mürrüsafi, mirra; Arabic murr (bitter). In English: myrrh. There is no Turkish monograph for this plant; the record rests on the EU monograph and PubMed. The drug is the gum-resin that oozes from the trunk and hardens in air; in medicine it is used as a tincture.
Part used
The gum-resin; as a tincture
Region
Africa
Asia and Siberia
Use
Healing, Incense and protection
In short

What is Myrrh?

Myrrh (Commiphora molmol Engler (C. myrrha (Nees) Engl.)) is a plant of the Burseraceae (günlükağacıgiller) family. Traditions that use it: Africa, Asia and Siberia. Part used: the gum-resin; as a tincture.

What is Myrrh used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places both under "traditional use": (1) minor ulcers and inflammation in the mouth (stomatitis, gingivitis) — as a gargle or dabbed on with a cotton bud; (2) minor wounds and small boils (furuncles) — the tincture dabbed on. Scientific evidence: limited. Myrrh has one single-ingredient randomised trial close to the monograph indication.

Is Myrrh safe?

Safety label: caution. Boils on the face should be treated by a doctor — the monograph’s firm provision; infections in the facial area carry a risk of spreading. See a doctor if fever or signs of worsening infection appear. Avoid eye contact with the tincture. Allergic skin reactions have been reported; because the tincture contains alcohol it can cause transient pain and irritation on mucosa or skin. Not recommended under 12, in pregnancy or lactation. A mouth ulcer or boil that is no better after a week should be shown to a doctor.

Traditional use

Europe — two indications

The EMA monograph places both under "traditional use": (1) minor ulcers and inflammation in the mouth (stomatitis, gingivitis) — as a gargle or dabbed on with a cotton bud; (2) minor wounds and small boils (furuncles) — the tincture dabbed on. Boils on the face should be treated by a doctor; avoid eye contact; not recommended under 12.

Turkey

There is no separate monograph for myrrh on the Turkish list. In Turkey it is known from herbalists and as incense; in Ottoman medicine it appears as "mürrüsafi" in mouth and wound remedies.

Arabian Peninsula and Horn of Africa — incense

Myrrh is, with frankincense, one of the two great incense resins of the ancient world, gathered from the dry scrub of Somalia, Ethiopia, Yemen and Oman and carried to the Mediterranean along the incense route. A 2017 review in the Journal of Oleo Science examines the composition and anti-inflammatory terpenoids of these two "incense gum resins" together — the religious incense and the mouth remedy are the same resin.

In nature

Where it grows
Dry scrub of Somalia, Ethiopia, Kenya and the Arabian Peninsula. It does not grow in Turkey and arrives as a product.
Harvest season
The resin oozes from cuts in the trunk in the dry season and hardens in the sun; it is collected and cleaned.
Local names
Mür, mürrüsafi, mirra; no unsourced regional names given.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ Often confused with frankincense (Boswellia): both are incense resins of the same family, but separate genera with separate monographs. Indian myrrh (Commiphora mukul, guggul) is a different species sold as a cholesterol supplement; its studies do not belong to this plant.

Preparation

Oromucosal: 0.5–5 ml of tincture in 150 ml of water, rinsed or gargled three times daily; or the undiluted tincture applied with a cotton bud 2–3 times daily. Cutaneous: the wound or boil dabbed 2–3 times daily with undiluted (preferably) or diluted tincture. See a doctor if no better after a week. Not recommended under 12.

What science says

Myrrh has one single-ingredient randomised trial close to the monograph indication. A three-arm trial published in 2021 in the Journal of Ethnopharmacology assigned 90 women after normal delivery to a myrrh-extract sitz bath, a frankincense-extract sitz bath or a betadine sitz bath for the episiotomy wound (one week, twice daily): improvement in the wound-healing score on days 2 and 7 was significantly greater in the myrrh group than with either frankincense or betadine. One trial, 30 women per arm, one centre, limited blinding — evidence level Limited. For the monograph’s main indication, mouth ulcers, there is no clinical trial. The chemistry is rich: sesquiterpenes and furanosesquiterpenes, with anti-inflammatory activity shown in the laboratory; but a laboratory finding is not clinical evidence.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing; there is no Turkish monograph. Because myrrh is used mainly externally the expectation of systemic interaction is low; the amount of gargle swallowed is small.
