BT-323 · Myrtaceae (myrtle family)

Niaouli

Melaleuca quinquenervia (Cav.) S.T.Blake
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

It is a tree one country is trying to get rid of while another grows it for its oil. The genome study published in GigaScience in 2022 describes it as a foundation species of the coastal wetlands of eastern Australia, Indonesia, Papua New Guinea and New Caledonia, and writes that it "has also become invasive in regions like Florida, USA". The review by the United States Department of Agriculture’s Agricultural Research Service, published in Pest Management Science in 2003, lists it among the selected case studies of classical biological control using Old World arthropods. The sources relied on here give no figure or account of when or why the tree was planted in Florida, or of how large an area it covers; the widely repeated details — that it was planted to dry out the swamps, that it covers hundreds of thousands of hectares — appear in neither citation, and so are not written here.

Other names
The name comes from the form "niaouli" used in New Caledonia, and it entered Turkish in that form. English broad-leaved paperbark, punk tree. In the aromatherapy trade the oil is sold as "niaouli" or under the abbreviation "MQV". Neither the European Medicines Agency nor the Turkish agency has a monograph for this species.

In short

What is Niaouli?

Niaouli (Melaleuca quinquenervia (Cav.) S.T.Blake) is a plant of the Myrtaceae (myrtle family) family. Traditions that use it: Oceania. Part used: leaf; the essential oil distilled from the leaf (the fruit only in research).

What is Niaouli used for?

Traditional use: A study published in the Asian Pacific Journal of Tropical Medicine in 2016 tested the species’ essential oil against the larvae of three mosquito species using the World Health Organization’s standard method. Scientific evidence: traditional. In the sources available to us there is no study in humans at all for this species.

Is Niaouli safe?

Safety label: caution. What is at issue is a concentrated essential oil, and there is no assessment of human safety made for this species. The monograph and restrictions written for tea tree oil do not belong to this species, but the reason needs stating carefully: the European Medicines Agency’s *Melaleucae aetheroleum* monograph (EMA/HMPC/320930/2012) names in its title not only *Melaleuca alternifolia* but also *M. linariifolia*, *M. dissitiflora* "and/or other species of Melaleuca". The substance it covers is nonetheless tea tree oil: the monograph requires the material to comply with the European Pharmacopoeia monograph for tea tree oil (01/2008: 1837), and its doses and indications are written for tea tree oil. Niaouli oil is not an oil that meets that pharmacopoeial standard, and *Melaleuca quinquenervia* is never named in the monograph; so the monograph’s doses and rules cannot be carried over to this species’ oil. Cajuput (*Melaleuca cajuputi*), in the same genus, is also a separate species. It should be borne in mind that compounds isolated from the species’ fruit were found cytotoxic in cell lines; the fruit and its extract are research material, not food. The sources relied on here contain no separate assessment for pregnancy, breastfeeding, children or drug interactions; as there are no data, it is appropriate not to use it in these groups.

Traditional use

In mosquito control — a measurement made in Cuba

A study published in the Asian Pacific Journal of Tropical Medicine in 2016 tested the species’ essential oil against the larvae of three mosquito species using the World Health Organization’s standard method. The LC50 values calculated were 0.0021% for *Culex quinquefasciatus*, 0.0047% for *Aedes aegypti* and 0.0049% for *Aedes albopictus*; activity against adults was obtained with bottles impregnated at 40 and 50 mg/mL, and at sublethal doses larval development was slowed. These are measurements made on mosquitoes in the laboratory; they are not a trial of a repellent applied to human skin.

Isolating compounds from the fruit — in vitro

A study published in ACS Omega in 2024 isolated nineteen compounds from the species’ fruit extract, two of them newly described. Four compounds were found markedly cytotoxic against cell lines; pyracrenic acid showed growth inhibition of non-small-cell lung cancer cells (A549) comparable to cisplatin (IC50 2.8 µM against 3.3 µM). This is an in vitro measurement; it is not a treatment and does not mean the fruit is eaten.

In nature

Where it grows
Kew records it in nineteen regions, a good many of them places it later spread to (the Bahamas, the Caroline Islands, Costa Rica, Florida, French Guiana, Hawaii, Honduras, Jamaica, Louisiana, the Mariana Islands and others). Its natural range is the east coast of Australia, New Guinea and New Caledonia; it is known for bark that peels like paper and it tolerates wet, swampy ground.
Harvest season
The sources relied on here give no collection calendar for Turkey; the plant does not grow there naturally.
Local names
It has no established Turkish local name.

Mix-up risk

It is in the same genus as tea tree (*Melaleuca alternifolia*) and cajuput (*Melaleuca cajuputi*) but a separate species; one species’ monograph and restrictions do not belong to its relative. Tea tree oil and niaouli oil are not the same thing, and a concentration or application rule written for one cannot be carried over to the other.

Preparation

No dose is accepted in an official monograph. The sources relied on here give no measure, dilution or duration for use in humans; the concentrations in the mosquito measurements belong to laboratory conditions and cannot be converted into a ratio for application to skin. The essential oil is not taken internally.

What science says

In the sources available to us there is no study in humans at all for this species. The mosquito measurements were made in insects, the compound work in cell lines. The species’ genome was published in 2022, and a review published in Pest Management Science in 2003 concerns its biological control as a weed in Florida — these are botanical and agricultural studies about the plant, not grounds for a health claim.
