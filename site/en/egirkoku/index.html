Sweet flag

Acorus calamus L.

In short

What is Sweet flag?

Sweet flag (Acorus calamus L.) is a plant of the Acoraceae family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia, Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the rhizome (underground stem) and the essential oil obtained from the rhizome.

What is Sweet flag used for?

Traditional use: Sweet flag is known in Indian traditional medicine as vacha. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no sound evidence of efficacy in humans.

Is Sweet flag safe?

Safety label: high risk. ⚠️ Taking it by mouth is not recommended. Beta-asarone has been found genotoxic and liver-carcinogenic in rodents; the European Medicines Agency asks that the amount of asarone in herbal medicines be reduced as far as possible. The toxicology review also reports cardiac and liver toxicity and reproductive toxicity; for this reason it is not used in pregnancy, while breastfeeding or in children. In the 30 Swedish cases the main symptom was vomiting that sometimes lasted more than 15 hours. The essential oil is especially dangerous. If poisoning is suspected, 114 (the National Poison Information Centre in Türkiye) or 112 is called.

Traditional use

India — vacha

Sweet flag is known in Indian traditional medicine as vacha. A 2020 review (*Journal of Clinical Medicine*) writes that the plant is used traditionally across a wide field covering complaints of the nervous system, digestion, respiration, metabolism, the kidneys and the liver. According to the same review, 145 compounds have so far been isolated from the plant and identified. The efficacy and safety of these uses in humans have not been shown.

Anatolia — eğir, eğer, hazanbel

Asuman Baytop’s 1979 article reported that the plant, until then unrecorded in the flora of Türkiye, grows wild on the shores of lakes Sapanca (Sakarya) and Yeniçağa (Bolu). According to the article the rhizomes were sold in Istanbul under the names eğir, eğer and hazanbel and were used in folk medicine against enlargement of the prostate. Both specimens examined were found to be triploid (2n = 36).

Europe and the USA — from flavouring to prohibition

Asarone isomers have been used in fragrances, food and traditional herbal medicines for their flavouring properties. In the USA, since the order of 9 May 1968, food containing added calamus, oil of calamus or its extract is deemed adulterated. In 2005 the herbal medicines committee of the European Medicines Agency published a public statement on herbal medicines containing asarone.

Internet trade — the "hallucinogen" claim

Between 2003 and 2006 the Swedish Poisons Information Centre received inquiries about 30 cases of intentional intoxication with calamus products marketed on the internet as hallucinogenic. The study that examined urine samples from the cases (2009) found no evidence for the presence of the hallucinogenic compound attributed to the plant; the main symptom the patients reported was vomiting that sometimes lasted more than 15 hours.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew’s Plants of the World Online record its native range is Central Asia (the Irtysh River valley); it is a perennial, rhizomatous plant and grows primarily in the temperate biome. Türkiye and many European countries are listed among the areas where it has been introduced. In Türkiye it has been recorded on the shores of lakes Sapanca and Yeniçağa.
Harvest season
The plants in Türkiye flower in May and June but set no fruit (Baytop, 1979). In the same study the rhizomes and roots were collected in October.
Local names
Eğir, eğer, hazanbel (the names under which the rhizome is sold in Istanbul; Baytop, 1979).

Mix-up risk

Three varieties of the same species go by the same name, but their beta-asarone contents differ greatly: it is not detected in the oil of the diploid var. americanus, is at a moderate level in the triploid var. calamus and very high in the tetraploid var. angustatus. For this reason a "safe" finding in one source cannot be carried over to another variety.

Preparation

Preparing and using it is not recommended; this page deliberately gives no recipe. The reason is that the rhizome and its essential oil contain beta-asarone, which has been shown to be genotoxic and to cause liver cancer in rodents. The European Medicines Agency asks that the amount of asarone in herbal medicines be reduced as far as possible and that diploid varieties always be preferred; as a temporary limit it regards about 115 micrograms a day (about 2 micrograms per kilogram of body weight) as acceptable. Which variety a root bought from a herb shop comes from, and how much beta-asarone it carries, cannot be known at home.

What science says

There is no sound evidence of efficacy in humans. A 2018 review (*Chemico-Biological Interactions*) states that no human studies at all have been carried out on the plant’s effects on the cardiovascular system and that the available data come only from cell and animal studies. A 2021 toxicology review (*Journal of Applied Toxicology*) finds that the efficacy and safety of asarone isomers in humans are still not known, and that cardiac and liver toxicity, reproductive toxicity, mutagenicity and carcinogenicity have been reported for alpha- and beta-asarone. According to the European Medicines Agency’s 2005 statement, beta-asarone is not detected in the oil of the diploid variety; it makes up 9–19 percent of the rhizome oil of the triploid variety and 85–95 percent of the rhizome oil of the tetraploid variety. The specimens Baytop examined from Türkiye were triploid and their rhizomes carried 2.4–2.7 percent essential oil. The favourable results reported on memory and the nervous system rest on laboratory, animal and computer models.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Blood thinners (theoretical)The European Medicines Agency statement reports that beta-asarone showed an anticoagulant effect in mice and rats. No interaction has been shown in humans.
Sedatives and sleeping medicines (theoretical)The same statement reports the sedative and body-temperature-lowering effect of beta-asarone in rats; an additive effect with medicines that depress the central nervous system is theoretically possible. No interaction has been shown in humans.
Medicines that affect the liver (theoretical)Liver toxicity has been reported for asarone isomers; use together with medicines that burden the liver may increase this risk. There is no human interaction study in the sources of this page.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Sweet flag.
