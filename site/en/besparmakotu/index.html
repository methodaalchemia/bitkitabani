Tormentil

Potentilla erecta (L.) Raeusch.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

For this plant the clinical evidence and the approved use sit in different age groups. The best data we have — the evidence a systematic review in Pediatrics called "promising" for diarrhoea — comes from trials in children. The monographs, meanwhile, do not use the plant under 18, because they are not based on those trials and do not consider the data sufficient. Situations like this are not rare in phytotherapy, and both halves need stating: where the evidence was produced and where permission was given need not be the same place.

In short

What is Tormentil?

Tormentil (Potentilla erecta (L.) Raeusch.) is a plant of the Rosaceae (rose family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the rhizome.

What is Tormentil used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places both indications under "traditional use": (1) symptomatic treatment of mild diarrhoea; (2) symptomatic treatment of minor inflammations of the oral mucosa. Scientific evidence: limited. This record is one of the clearest examples of a gap between where the evidence was produced and where the approved use lies.

Is Tormentil safe?

Safety label: caution. Hypersensitivity to the active substance can occur. Taken orally it can cause mild gastrointestinal complaints such as nausea and vomiting — a known effect of tannin-rich drugs. If recurrent diarrhoea or bloody stools occur, see a doctor; these can signal something other than mild diarrhoea and should not be managed with a herbal product. Because it delays absorption, take it at least an hour before or after other medicines. The duration of use is short: consult a doctor if symptoms have not resolved after three days for diarrhoea or a week for oral inflammation. Not used under 18 for lack of data; the paediatric trials described in the science section do not change this, because the monographs are not based on them. Not to be used in pregnancy or lactation.

Traditional use

Europe — two indications

The EMA monograph places both indications under "traditional use": (1) symptomatic treatment of mild diarrhoea; (2) symptomatic treatment of minor inflammations of the oral mucosa. Both come from the same chemistry: the rhizome’s high tannin content acts as an astringent.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the rhizome" and adds a practical warning: because it delays absorption, it should be taken at least an hour before or after other medicines. It is also not to be used under 18 — a point that returns in the evidence section.

Anatolia

Tormentil grows in the northern forests, damp meadows and heaths of Turkey. Its Turkish name comes from the shape of the leaf; the rhizome turns red when cut, which is why it carries names meaning "blood root" in Europe.

In nature

Where it grows
Acidic meadows, heaths and forest clearings of Europe and western Asia. Found in the northern forests of Turkey.
Harvest season
Yellow flowers with four petals from May to September — five petals is the rule in the rose family, and four is a distinguishing feature of this species. The rhizome is lifted in autumn.
Local names
Beşparmak otu, incibar — both appear in the Turkish monograph.

Mix-up risk

Many Potentilla species grow in Turkey and several are called beşparmak otu; the EU monograph covers P. erecta only. Two features distinguish it: the flower has four petals (most of its relatives have five), and the rhizome turns red when cut.

Preparation

The monograph describes oral and oromucosal preparations of the rhizome. Take it at least an hour before or after other medicines. The duration differs by indication: consult a doctor or pharmacist if symptoms have not resolved after three days for diarrhoea or a week for oral inflammation. Tinctures containing ethanol must carry the appropriate labelling. Not used under 18.

What science says

This record is one of the clearest examples of a gap between where the evidence was produced and where the approved use lies. A systematic review published in 2017 in Pediatrics searched herbal treatments for gastrointestinal disorders in children and adolescents: 14 randomised controlled trials, 1,927 participants. The review names three things for which it found promising evidence of effectiveness in treating diarrhoea, and tormentil is the first — the other two are carob bean juice and a herbal compound preparation including chamomile. No serious adverse events were reported. The review’s own caveat matters: because the number of studies is small, the results have to be interpreted carefully and more rigorous trials are needed. The real tension here is this: that evidence was produced in children, whereas both the EU and the Turkish monograph do not use the plant under 18 — on the grounds of insufficient data. So the best clinical data we have comes from the age group the monographs exclude. Both things are true and both need writing down: the evidence is promising, but the use the agencies approve is in adults. The evidence level is Limited.

Safety

Caution

Hypersensitivity to the active substance can occur. Taken orally it can cause mild gastrointestinal complaints such as nausea and vomiting — a known effect of tannin-rich drugs. If recurrent diarrhoea or bloody stools occur, see a doctor; these can signal something other than mild diarrhoea and should not be managed with a herbal product. Because it delays absorption, take it at least an hour before or after other medicines. The duration of use is short: consult a doctor if symptoms have not resolved after three days for diarrhoea or a week for oral inflammation. Not used under 18 for lack of data; the paediatric trials described in the science section do not change this, because the monographs are not based on them. Not to be used in pregnancy or lactation.
