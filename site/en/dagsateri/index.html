Wild savory

Satureja cuneifolia Ten.
Evidence: LimitedGenerally safe

The oil of the same species comes in two different chemical types depending on where it is collected: of 19 samples from Türkiye, 11 were of the carvacrol type and 8 of the thymol type (Planta Medica, 1998). In samples from southern Italy, on the other hand, the main constituents have been reported as linalool, borneol and α-pinene.

Other names
No established Turkish name specific to the species appears in the sources; in Türkiye the genus Satureja is known by the names “sivri kekik” and “taş kekiği”, and this species is used in place of thyme. In short

What is Wild savory?

Wild savory (Satureja cuneifolia Ten.) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: flowering aerial parts; the essential oil.

What is Wild savory used for?

Traditional use: A study published in Planta Medica in 1998 writes that Satureja cuneifolia is used in Türkiye as a substitute for thyme. Scientific evidence: limited. The available data come from laboratory and animal studies; there is no clinical trial in humans.

Is Wild savory safe?

Safety label: generally safe. No harm has been reported for the amounts added to food as a spice; however, no safety study in humans has been carried out on the species either. In the rat study mild granular degeneration was seen in the liver in the group given 80 mg/kg of S. cuneifolia tea, and the same paper reports that anaemia was common among Taurus villagers who consume thyme excessively; the data are insufficient for long-term and heavy consumption. There are no safety data for pregnancy, breastfeeding or children. The pure essential oil, rich in carvacrol and thymol, is a concentrated product; the sources contain no safety data on its oral use. It is appropriate for people with anaemia or liver disease to consult a doctor before regular and heavy use.

Traditional use

Türkiye — a spice and tea in place of thyme

A study published in Planta Medica in 1998 writes that Satureja cuneifolia is used in Türkiye as a substitute for thyme. A 2024 study likewise states that the plant is widely used as a spice, a tea and a traditional medicine. A 2025 review (Records of Natural Products) reports that the genus Satureja is used in general for gastrointestinal complaints, respiratory problems and pain; this use has not been tested specifically for the species.

Aegean and Mediterranean — wild collection and trade

According to the 2008 study in the journal Ekoloji, about 700–800 tonnes of sivri kekik are collected from the wild in Türkiye each year, and S. cuneifolia is listed first among the four species collected commercially. Collection is concentrated in the Aegean and Mediterranean regions; the work is mostly done by low-income villagers. The study draws attention to the pressure on the commercial species and recommends that collection be restricted.

Taurus Mountains — an observation of excessive consumption

A paper in the journal Toxicology and Industrial Health starts from an observation that anaemia was common among villagers on the outskirts of the Taurus Mountains who make their living from thyme and consume it excessively by adding it to their tea and to many of their dishes. In the paper three species are treated together under the name “thyme”: Origanum onites, Thymbra spicata and Satureja cuneifolia.

Italy — a wild species in need of protection

A 2021 study carried out in the Apulia region of southern Italy describes the species there as one with a narrow range and of conservation concern. The authors state that in that region the plant is not officially used in traditional medicine or as a spice.

In nature

Where it grows
Kew’s world plant checklist gives the native range of the species as from southern and eastern Spain to northern Iraq (Spain, Sardinia, Italy, the north-western Balkans, Albania, Greece, Bulgaria, Lebanon-Syria, Iraq). Publications from Türkiye also report the species from Türkiye and write that it is collected commercially in the Aegean and Mediterranean regions. There are 17 taxa of the genus Satureja in Türkiye, 5 of them endemic; S. cuneifolia is not among the endemics.
Harvest season
In the essential oil studies the plant was collected in the pre-flowering, flowering and post-flowering stages; the main constituents remained the same in all three stages. The sources do not give a specific month.
Local names
The names sivri kekik and taş kekiği appear in the sources for the genus Satureja; no local name specific to the species is given in the sources.

Mix-up risk

The names “kekik” (thyme) and “sivri kekik” cover more than one species: in trade S. cuneifolia, S. thymbra, S. hortensis and S. spicigera are gathered together; in the Taurus Mountains the name kekik is also used for Origanum onites and Thymbra spicata. Which species the product on sale is cannot be told from the name.

Preparation

The dried flowering branches are used as a spice like thyme, or infused to make tea. Because there is no monograph for this species on the EMA or TİTCK lists, there is no accepted dose. The composition of the essential oil varies markedly from sample to sample: of 19 samples from different regions of Türkiye, the main constituent was carvacrol (26–72 per cent) in 11 and thymol (22–58 per cent) in 8. For this reason the scent and content of two separate bunches may not be the same.

What science says

The available data come from laboratory and animal studies; there is no clinical trial in humans. In the 2008 study from Anadolu University, the main constituents of the essential oil in the pre-flowering, flowering and post-flowering stages were measured as thymol (42.5–45.2 per cent), p-cymene (19.4–24.3 per cent) and carvacrol (8.5–13.2 per cent). In the same study the oil inhibited some human pathogens, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and Candida albicans, at minimum inhibitory concentrations of 62.5–250 µg/ml, moderately in the authors’ words; and it prevented the oxidation of linoleic acid at a level close to that of the synthetic antioxidant BHT (93.54–94.65 per cent). In the 2024 study the ethanol extract inhibited the enzyme lipoxygenase by 84.50 per cent. In a 30-day experiment in rats, 80 mg/kg of thyme tea showed a cholesterol-lowering effect against a high-cholesterol diet, but mild granular degeneration was found in the liver in the same group. None of these findings has been confirmed in humans.

Safety

Generally safe

No harm has been reported for the amounts added to food as a spice; however, no safety study in humans has been carried out on the species either. In the rat study mild granular degeneration was seen in the liver in the group given 80 mg/kg of S. cuneifolia tea, and the same paper reports that anaemia was common among Taurus villagers who consume thyme excessively; the data are insufficient for long-term and heavy consumption. There are no safety data for pregnancy, breastfeeding or children. The pure essential oil, rich in carvacrol and thymol, is a concentrated product; the sources contain no safety data on its oral use. It is appropriate for people with anaemia or liver disease to consult a doctor before regular and heavy use.
