In short

What is Pot marigold?

Pot marigold (Calendula officinalis L.) is a plant of the Asteraceae (papatyagiller) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the flower.

What is Pot marigold used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph gives two indications, both under "traditional use": (1) symptomatic treatment of minor inflammations of the skin, such as sunburn, and as an aid in healing minor wounds; (2) symptomatic treatment of minor inflammations in the mouth or throat. Scientific evidence: traditional. Pot marigold sits at an interesting point on this catalog’s evidence scale: clinical trials do exist, but the use they are most often cited for does not come out positive.

Is Pot marigold safe?

Safety label: caution. Not for anyone sensitive to the active substance or to the Asteraceae family — a large family, so people allergic to chamomile, chrysanthemum or sunflower should take care. Contact dermatitis can occur; the EU monograph reports skin sensitization with an unknown frequency. If signs of skin infection appear, or an existing infection worsens during use, consult a doctor — that warning matters, because the plant is applied precisely to wounds, and covering an infected wound with a herbal cream can cause delay. Not used under 6 on the skin or under 12 in the mouth. Not recommended in pregnancy or lactation for lack of data. The Turkish monograph also requires the drug used to conform to the European Pharmacopoeia definition.

Traditional use

Europe — skin and mouth

The EMA monograph gives two indications, both under "traditional use": (1) symptomatic treatment of minor inflammations of the skin, such as sunburn, and as an aid in healing minor wounds; (2) symptomatic treatment of minor inflammations in the mouth or throat. The two carry different age limits: 6 years for skin use, 12 years for use in the mouth.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the flowers" and states two further things: that contact dermatitis can occur, and that for safety the herbal drug must conform to the European Pharmacopoeia definition. The second is an important detail: not every flower sold under the same name is the drug the monograph covers.

Anatolia

Pot marigold is widely grown as a garden ornamental in Turkey, flowering orange and yellow through the summer. In folk use an oil is made from it and applied to the skin, which matches the monograph’s first indication.

In nature

Where it grows
Mediterranean in origin; widely grown in gardens and public planting in Turkey, and naturalised in places.
Harvest season
Orange and yellow flowers from June into autumn; the flowers are gathered fully open and dried in shade.
Local names
Aynısefa, tıbbi nergis — both appear in the Turkish monograph.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ Two separate name confusions, both recorded at monograph level. In English, "marigold" covers both Calendula and the genus Tagetes; the Turkish monograph puts this among its safety warnings. In Turkish, "nergis" is used both for this plant and for the genus Narcissus, whose bulbs are poisonous. The Latin name on the label settles it.

Preparation

The monograph describes topical preparations of the flower and mouthwash forms. If symptoms last longer than a week, consult a doctor. Not used under 6 on the skin or under 12 in the mouth. If signs of skin infection appear, or an existing infection worsens during use, see a doctor.

What science says

Pot marigold sits at an interesting point on this catalog’s evidence scale: clinical trials do exist, but the use they are most often cited for does not come out positive. That use is preventing radiation dermatitis in patients having radiotherapy — calendula cream has been used in oncology clinics for this for years. A systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2023 in Supportive Care in Cancer searched randomised trials (19 in the review, 16 in the meta-analysis): of five natural products studied (aloe vera, oral enzymes, olive oil, calendula and curcumin), only oral enzymes and olive oil significantly reduced the incidence of grade 2+ radiation dermatitis; the others, calendula among them, showed no significant effect. A systematic review published the same year in Phytotherapy Research, focused on breast cancer patients, assessed 35 studies and pointed the same way: henna ointments, silymarin gel and Juango cream were reported to reduce severity, while the data on aloe gel and calendula ointment were conflicting. Both reviews complain that the trials are few, small and methodologically weak. So this use of calendula is not "disproved" but "not shown". The two indications the EU monograph gives rest on long use rather than clinical evidence in any case. The evidence level is therefore Traditional: no measured clinical benefit.

Caution

Not for anyone sensitive to the active substance or to the Asteraceae family — a large family, so people allergic to chamomile, chrysanthemum or sunflower should take care. Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Sedative medicines (central nervous system depressants)The Turkish monograph reports one interaction, and this is it. The EU monograph lists nothing, so this warning exists only in the Turkish text. Anyone taking a sleeping tablet, a sedative or a similar medicine should tell their doctor.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing. No systemic interaction is expected because the approved uses are topical and oromucosal; the Turkish monograph nevertheless reports one group.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Pot marigold.
