Gumweed

Its Turkish name on the official list is not even a translation but the Latin genus name read in Turkish: grindelya. Like Java turmeric, this plant never entered Anatolian folk medicine; it reached the official list directly by way of the European monograph. The name itself states the age of the plant’s relationship with this land: zero.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the name "grindelya" — the Latin genus name in Turkish spelling; the plant has no established Turkish name. In English: gumweed, gumplant, tarweed (the Turkish table prints "gumfeed herb"). The monograph covers four Grindelia species together.

What is Gumweed?

Gumweed (Grindelia robusta Nutt.; G. squarrosa (Pursh) Dunal; G. humilis Hook. et Arn.; G. camporum Greene) is a plant of the Asteraceae (daisy family) family. Traditions that use it: North America. Part used: the aerial parts.

What is Gumweed used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": relief of cough associated with cold. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no single-ingredient clinical trial of gumweed in humans.

Is Gumweed safe?

Safety label: caution. If dyspnoea, high fever or purulent sputum occurs, see a doctor immediately — these may signal not a cold but something more serious such as pneumonia, and are not things to wait out for a week with a herbal tea. Not for anyone sensitive to the daisy family (Asteraceae); sunflower, chrysanthemum and mugwort belong to it. Nausea and diarrhoea have been reported. Not to be used under 18. Not recommended in pregnancy or lactation. Tinctures and extracts may contain ethanol. Not to be used longer than a week.

Traditional use

Europe — one indication

The EMA monograph places its single indication under "traditional use": relief of cough associated with cold. The red line is plain: if symptoms worsen during use, or if dyspnoea, high fever or purulent sputum occurs, see a doctor immediately. One week’s use.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph lists the aerial parts; it says not to be used under 18 (the EU says "not established", Turkey is firm) and adds that it may cause nausea and diarrhoea — a line sourced from a consumer medicines database. The interaction section is empty.

North America

Gumweed is a resinous plant of western North America, from the California coast to dry meadows; its flower buds are coated in a sticky white resin, which is where the English name comes from. It entered European pharmacy in the 19th century. It does not grow in Turkey and arrives as a product.

In nature

Where it grows
Western North America, the California coast and dry meadows. It does not grow in Turkey.
Harvest season
Yellow flowers in late summer; the aerial parts are gathered in flower.
Local names
Grindelya — the Turkish monograph gives one name; there is no vernacular one.

Mix-up risk

Because the monograph covers four Grindelia species together, species confusion is not an issue. The real risk is daisy-family allergy: cross-sensitivity within the family is common.

Preparation

For adults the EU monograph gives three forms: herbal tea, 2–3 g of the comminuted drug infused in 150 ml of boiling water, up to three times daily; liquid extract 0.6–1.2 ml three times daily; tincture 0.5–1 ml three times daily. If symptoms persist longer than a week, consult a doctor or pharmacist. Not used under 18.

What science says

There is no single-ingredient clinical trial of gumweed in humans. Recent publications are reviews assessing natural cough products for children collectively — the title of a 2025 review in Minerva Pediatrics sums up the state of things: "darkness and shadow". The EU monograph rests the indication on long use. The plant’s resin and diterpene acids are known, but a known composition is not clinical evidence. The evidence level is Traditional.

Safety

Caution

If dyspnoea, high fever or purulent sputum occurs, see a doctor immediately — these may signal not a cold but something more serious such as pneumonia, and are not things to wait out for a week with a herbal tea. Not for anyone sensitive to the daisy family (Asteraceae); sunflower, chrysanthemum and mugwort belong to it. Nausea and diarrhoea have been reported. Not to be used under 18. Not recommended in pregnancy or lactation. Tinctures and extracts may contain ethanol. Not to be used longer than a week.
