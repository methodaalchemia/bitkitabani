In short

What is Ispaghula?

Ispaghula (Plantago ovata Forssk.) is a plant of the Plantaginaceae (plantain family) family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia. Part used: the seed and the seed husk.

What is Ispaghula used for?

Traditional use: The seed husk forms a gel many times its own volume on contact with water, and the traditional use follows from that physical property. Scientific evidence: clinical. Psyllium fibre has some of the firmest randomised evidence of any herbal product.

Is Ispaghula safe?

Safety label: caution. Not for anyone with difficulty swallowing or throat problems. Not to be used in intestinal obstruction (ileus), intestinal paralysis, megacolon, problems of the gastrointestinal tract or oesophagus, or heart disease. The Turkish monograph states that people with diabetes or thyroid disease should not use it. It must be taken with enough fluid; too little fluid creates a risk of obstruction. Use is not recommended in pregnancy or lactation, nor in children under 12, for lack of data. Hypersensitivity to the active substance can occur.

Traditional use

India and Iran — a fibre crop

The seed husk forms a gel many times its own volume on contact with water, and the traditional use follows from that physical property. Most of the world crop comes from Gujarat in India.

Europe — an approved use in constipation

The European Medicines Agency has separate monographs for the seed and the husk, and in both the indication for habitual constipation sits under "well-established use". Alongside constipation, the monographs also list conditions in which a softened stool is medically necessary.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the seeds and seed husks (testa epidermis)" and lists its warnings, citing the 2013 EMA monograph.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to the Mediterranean basin and western Asia; grows on dry, sandy soils. Commercial production is concentrated in north-western India and Iran.
Harvest season
An annual; flowers in spring, seeds ripen in early summer.
Local names
The Turkish monograph gives "sarı karnıyarık otu". Sold as psyllium or ispaghula.

Mix-up risk

In Turkish, karnıyarık and sinirotu are used for several Plantago species, most often P. major and P. lanceolata. For those the part used is the leaf, whereas here it is the seed and husk, and the uses differ. The Latin name on the package is what settles it.

Preparation

The Turkish monograph stresses two points: it must be taken with enough fluid (water, milk, fruit juice or similar), and the effect begins after 12–24 hours. The fluid requirement is not a formality: because the husk works by forming a gel in the gut, taking it with too little fluid can cause obstruction. It should not be taken at the same time as other medicines — leave at least an hour between them. If symptoms persist for three days, consult a doctor.

What science says

Psyllium fibre has some of the firmest randomised evidence of any herbal product. A meta-analysis of 28 randomised trials (1,924 participants) found that a median dose of 10.2 g of psyllium a day lowered LDL cholesterol by 0.33 mmol/L (95% CI 0.27–0.38), non-HDL cholesterol by 0.39 mmol/L and apolipoprotein B by 0.05 g/L. Under GRADE the evidence was rated moderate quality for LDL and non-HDL cholesterol (downgraded for inconsistency) and high quality for apoB. The constipation side is weaker: a meta-analysis of five randomised trials found that fibre significantly increased stool frequency against placebo, but found no significant difference in stool consistency, treatment success, laxative use or painful defecation. A separate randomised trial compared P. ovata seed with mesalamine for maintaining remission in ulcerative colitis. In short: the lipid effect is well documented and of measured size, while the constipation effect has been shown on one narrow endpoint.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
All medicines — delayed absorptionThe Turkish monograph asks anyone taking other medicines to leave at least an hour between them and the herbal product. The reason is physical: the gel that forms in the gut can delay and reduce the absorption of a medicine taken alongside it. This is not an interaction specific to particular drugs but a general consequence of the fibre itself.
Vitamin B12, minerals, cardiac glycosides, coumarin derivatives, carbamazepine, lithiumThe Turkish monograph names these groups: minerals and vitamins absorbed from the gut (B12), cardiac glycosides, coumarin anticoagulants, carbamazepine and lithium. For all of them the effect depends on blood levels staying within a narrow range, which is why a change in absorption matters.
Medicines that slow gut motility (opioids)The Turkish monograph says it should not be used together with medicines that inhibit peristalsis. A gel-forming fibre may fail to move on when motility is suppressed, and can cause obstruction.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Ispaghula.
