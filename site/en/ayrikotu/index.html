Couch grass

Elymus repens (L.) Gould
Evidence: TraditionalGenerally safe

What makes couch grass a nuisance in the field is what makes it a drug in the pharmacy: the rhizome running underground. Because every fragment broken by the plough makes a new plant it is among a farmer’s most stubborn weeds; and the rhizome is precisely the part gathered for medicine. The same relationship appears once more in this catalog: restharrow is named for the root that stops the plough, and that root is the part used.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the Latin name as Agropyron repens (L.) P. Beauv.; the currently accepted name is Elymus repens (L.) Gould, with Triticum repens L. as another synonym. The Turkish name is "ayrık otu".

In short

What is Couch grass?

Couch grass (Elymus repens (L.) Gould) is a plant of the Poaceae (grass family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the rhizome.

What is Couch grass used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": to increase the amount of urine and so flush the urinary tract, as an adjuvant in minor urinary complaints — the same indication as the other two plants in this batch. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no randomised clinical trial of couch grass in humans.

Is Couch grass safe?

Safety label: generally safe. No serious safety problem is reported in the monographs and their interaction sections are empty. Not for anyone hypersensitive to the active substance. Fluid intake must be increased during use, but it is not recommended in conditions where fluid intake has to be reduced — the shared condition of diuretic plants. If urinary complaints worsen, or if fever, painful urination, spasms or blood in the urine occur, see a doctor; these can signal a urinary infection and should not be managed with a herbal product. The age limit varies by preparation: 12 for the herbal tea, 18 for the liquid extract and tincture. Not recommended in pregnancy or lactation. If symptoms do not resolve over two weeks, consult a doctor.

Traditional use

Europe — flushing the urinary tract

The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": to increase the amount of urine and so flush the urinary tract, as an adjuvant in minor urinary complaints — the same indication as the other two plants in this batch.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the rhizomes" and splits the age limit by preparation: 12 years for the herbal tea, 18 for the liquid extract and tincture. Fluid intake must be increased during use, but it is not recommended in conditions where fluid intake has to be reduced.

Anatolia — the field’s nuisance

Couch grass is a perennial grass found everywhere in Turkey. Because it spreads by rhizomes running underground it counts among the most stubborn arable weeds: every rhizome fragment broken by the plough makes a new plant. And that rhizome is exactly the part used medicinally.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to Europe, Asia and North Africa, now spread through the temperate world. Found everywhere in Turkey.
Harvest season
It heads from June to September. The rhizome is lifted in spring or autumn, washed and dried.
Local names
Ayrık otu — the Turkish monograph gives one Turkish name.

Mix-up risk

Many grasses are called ayrık in Turkey and they look much alike in leaf; the distinguishing feature is the whitish, jointed rhizome underground. The EU monograph covers this species only. The name Agropyron repens used by the monographs is also no longer the accepted one — that is Elymus repens — and both can be seen on labels.

Preparation

The monograph describes preparations of the rhizome. The decisive condition is water: fluid intake must be increased for the amount of urine to rise. The age limit is 12 or 18 depending on the preparation. Tinctures and extracts containing ethanol must carry the appropriate labelling. If symptoms do not resolve over two weeks, consult a doctor.

What science says

There is no randomised clinical trial of couch grass in humans. Recent publications are at laboratory level: reviews of its chemistry and pharmacological activity, antibacterial measurements against urinary pathogens in a multi-ingredient formulation, studies of its element content. These show what is in the rhizome; they do not show whether it relieves a urinary complaint in a patient. The EU monograph rests its indication on long use. The one mechanism the monograph gives is an increase in the amount of urine. All three plants in this batch share the same indication, the same mechanism and the same contraindication — and none of the three has clinical evidence. The evidence level is Traditional.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No drug interactions reported in either monographBoth the EU and the Turkish monograph leave the interaction section empty. "None reported" is not evidence of no interaction; it means not studied.
Diuretic medicinesNothing is reported, but the mechanism points the same way: using two things that work in the same direction can deepen fluid and electrolyte loss. The same note appears for the other two plants in this batch.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Couch grass.
