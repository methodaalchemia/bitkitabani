BT-198 · Rosaceae (rose family)

Raspberry leaf

Rubus idaeus L.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

Here fame, evidence and official use point in three different directions — but the direction of the divergence is what matters. The plant’s best-known use in the world is preparing for birth at the end of pregnancy; none of the EU monograph’s three indications touches that; and both agencies say it should not be used in pregnancy. So the divergence here does not merely say "no evidence", it says "the agencies say the opposite". A plant whose most popular use collides with its monograph’s warning is rare, and that is why it needs writing down.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the name "ahududu" and lists no synonyms. In English: raspberry, red raspberry. ⚠️ The monograph is about the leaf, not the fruit: the raspberries we eat are a food, while the drug in the monograph is the leaf.

In short

What is Raspberry leaf?

Raspberry leaf (Rubus idaeus L.) is a plant of the Rosaceae (rose family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the leaf.

What is Raspberry leaf used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places all three indications under "traditional use": (1) symptomatic relief of minor spasm associated with menstrual periods; (2) symptomatic treatment of mild inflammation in the mouth or throat; (3) symptomatic treatment of mild diarrhoea. Scientific evidence: traditional. Raspberry leaf’s worldwide reputation comes from labour preparation: drinking it as a tea in the last weeks of pregnancy is widely believed to make birth easier, especially in English-speaking countries.

Is Raspberry leaf safe?

Safety label: caution. Not to be used in pregnancy or lactation. That is the most important sentence in this record, because the plant’s commonest use is in pregnancy: the EU monograph does not recommend it for lack of data, and the Turkish monograph says outright that it must not be used. The Turkish monograph also states that the plant may act like oestrogen and that, for this reason, people with breast, uterine or ovarian cancer, endometriosis or uterine fibroids should not use it. It should be used with care because it may lower blood sugar. Because it may reduce blood clotting, it can raise the risk of bruising and bleeding alongside blood thinners. Hypersensitivity to the active substance can occur. Not recommended under 18. If symptoms worsen, consult a doctor. Any decision to use a herbal product in pregnancy should be discussed with a midwife or doctor.

Traditional use

Europe — three indications

The EMA monograph places all three indications under "traditional use": (1) symptomatic relief of minor spasm associated with menstrual periods; (2) symptomatic treatment of mild inflammation in the mouth or throat; (3) symptomatic treatment of mild diarrhoea. None of the three has anything to do with pregnancy or labour — which is precisely the plant’s best-known use in the world.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the leaves" and carries one of the longest safety lists in this catalog: because it may act like oestrogen, it should not be used by people with breast, uterine or ovarian cancer, endometriosis or uterine fibroids; it should be used with care because it may lower blood sugar; and because it may reduce blood clotting, it can raise bleeding risk alongside blood thinners. Not recommended under 18.

Anatolia

Raspberry grows wild at forest edges in the Black Sea region and northern Anatolia, and is also grown for fruit. A tea of the leaf is used in folk practice for diarrhoea and the throat, matching the monograph’s second and third indications. The tannin content is the basis of both: an astringent effect.

In nature

Where it grows
Forest edges and clearings of Europe and northern Asia. Grows wild in the Black Sea region and northern Anatolia in Turkey, and is also cultivated for fruit.
Harvest season
White flowers in spring, red fruit in summer. The leaf, the part used medicinally, is gathered before and during flowering.
Local names
Ahududu; "ağaç çileği" (tree strawberry) in some regions.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ The most important confusion is of parts: the raspberries we eat are the fruit, while the monograph is about the leaf. Their uses and warnings are not the same; the Turkish monograph’s pregnancy and hormone warnings are given for the leaf. Blackberry leaf (Rubus fruticosus) of the same genus is also sold as a tea, and the EU monograph covers R. idaeus leaf only.

Preparation

The monograph describes oral and oromucosal preparations of the leaf. If symptoms worsen during use, consult a doctor. Not recommended under 18. Not to be used in pregnancy or lactation — the EU monograph does not recommend it, and the Turkish monograph says outright that it must not be used.

What science says

Raspberry leaf’s worldwide reputation comes from labour preparation: drinking it as a tea in the last weeks of pregnancy is widely believed to make birth easier, especially in English-speaking countries. What is known about that use? A prospective observational study published in 2024 in BMC Complementary Medicine and Therapies gathered 91 records in Australia: 44 women had used raspberry leaf and 47 had not, with clinical outcomes taken from the maternity service’s own database and analysed by Bayesian regression. The findings look favourable for the exposed group: less augmentation of labour, less epidural, fewer instrumental births, fewer caesareans, less postpartum haemorrhage; more vaginal births and shorter phases of labour. But the authors’ own caveats matter more than those findings and should be passed on as written: the results cannot be relied on or generalised to the wider population of pregnant women; the absence of safety concerns in the study should not be taken as evidence that raspberry leaf is safe; and a randomised controlled trial is urgently needed to give women and clinicians a sound basis for practice. Why an observational study is not enough: women who take raspberry leaf may be a different group from the start — preferring unmedicated birth, carrying less risk, or using a different kind of maternity care. Those differences can produce the result themselves. The EU monograph rests its three indications on long use and gives no pregnancy indication. The evidence level is Traditional.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Insulin and blood-sugar medicinesThe Turkish monograph reports interaction with insulin and states that the plant may lower blood sugar. Anyone on diabetes medication should tell their doctor: when two things work in the same direction, blood sugar can fall further than expected.
Blood-thinning medicinesThe Turkish monograph reports interaction with anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs and gives the reason: the plant may reduce blood clotting, so combined use can raise the risk of bruising and bleeding.
Hormone-sensitive conditions (a contraindication, not an interaction)The Turkish monograph states the plant may act like oestrogen. This is not a drug interaction but requires the same care in practice: people with breast, uterine or ovarian cancer, endometriosis or uterine fibroids should not use it. Anyone on hormone therapy should also ask their doctor.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing; the Turkish one reports two drug groups and adds a contraindication.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Raspberry leaf.
