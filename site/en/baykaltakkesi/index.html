BT-294 · Lamiaceae (mint family)

Baikal skullcap

Scutellaria baicalensis Georgi
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

Turkish has no name for this plant but has one for its genus: the native Scutellaria species of Anatolia are called takke otu or kaside otu — from the cap-shaped calyx of the flower. That is how the "yellow gold" of two thousand years of Chinese medicine can enter Turkish under a relative’s name. On the evidence side the situation is reversed: the WHO said "no controlled trials" in 2007, and every human trial since has mixed it with other plants. Two thousand years of use and never having been tested alone can both be true at once.

Other names
No established Turkish name; "Baykal takke otu" renders the English Baikal skullcap (the genus Scutellaria is called takke otu, "cap herb", in Turkish). Chinese huáng qín ("yellow gold"), Japanese ōgon. Synonyms S. grandiflora Adams, S. lanceolaria Miq., S. macrantha Fisch. No Turkish or EU monograph; the record rests on the WHO monograph (Vol. 3, 2007) and PubMed.

In short

What is Baikal skullcap?

Baikal skullcap (Scutellaria baicalensis Georgi) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia. Part used: the dried roots.

What is Baikal skullcap used for?

Traditional use: The World Health Organization monograph (Vol. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no clinical trial of Baikal skullcap alone in the monograph indication.

Is Baikal skullcap safe?

Safety label: caution. Contraindicated in pregnancy, lactation and under 12 — the WHO monograph, owing to possible teratogenic and mutagenic effects and lack of data. The monograph also reports the conflict in animal data: in one study an aqueous extract was teratogenic in pregnant rats, in another a methanol extract was not. Rare gastrointestinal discomfort and diarrhoea have been reported with oral use. Liver: the monograph writes that liver damage from the root has been suggested but no direct link to published cases established; given liver cases later reported with skullcap-containing mixtures, anyone developing jaundice or dark urine while using it should stop and see a doctor. No interaction data.

Traditional use

WHO monograph — the root

The World Health Organization monograph (Vol. 3, 2007): no use supported by clinical data — case reports suggest immune stimulation, but data from controlled trials are lacking. Uses described in pharmacopoeias: fever, nausea and vomiting, acute dysentery, jaundice, cough, carbuncles and sores, threatened abortion. In traditional medicine: allergies, arteriosclerosis, diarrhoea, dermatitis, hypertension. Daily dose 3–9 g of root as infusion or decoction.

Turkey

There is no monograph for this plant on the Turkish list; it does not grow in Turkey. Native Scutellaria species (S. orientalis, S. salviifolia) occur in Anatolia, but they are different species outside this monograph.

China — huáng qín

One of the oldest "heat-clearing" bitter-cold drugs of Chinese medicine, listed in the Shennong Bencao Jing. Its yellow root carries the flavones baicalin, baicalein and wogonin; today it is an ingredient of hundreds of compound formulas in China (including Tanreqing injection).

In nature

Where it grows
The Korean peninsula, China, Japan, Mongolia and Russia; dry slopes. It does not grow in Turkey.
Harvest season
Blue-purple cap-shaped flowers in summer; the roots are lifted in autumn or spring and are yellow when dried.
Local names
No Turkish name; Chinese huáng qín, Japanese ōgon.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ American skullcap (Scutellaria lateriflora) is a different species with a different tradition (a nervine); the two are confused under the name "skullcap" and may substitute for each other in products. The native skullcaps of Anatolia are also outside this monograph.

Preparation

The WHO monograph gives a daily dose of 3–9 g of dried root as infusion or decoction. Root, extracts, infusions and decoctions. Contraindicated in pregnancy, lactation and under 12.

What science says

There is no clinical trial of Baikal skullcap alone in the monograph indication. Recent human studies all use mixtures: in 2025, in the Journal of Psychopharmacology, skullcap + hawthorn + magnesium + chromium in stressed people; in Nutritional Neuroscience, a combined extract with Acacia catechu for cognition; in Nutrients, a combination with saffron for depressive symptoms. All measure the mixture, not the skullcap. The Chinese trials use multi-herb formulas (such as the 2024 meta-analysis of Tanreqing injection). The WHO monograph said in 2007 that controlled-trial data were lacking; for single-ingredient evidence nothing has changed. Evidence level Traditional.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
WHO: no interaction dataThe monograph states that no information is available on drug interactions. Because its flavones are known to affect CYP enzymes in the laboratory, it is theoretically right for anyone on regular medication to tell their doctor.
Mixture productsSkullcap is almost never sold alone in the West; all the trials use mixtures. A mixture’s data cannot be attributed to this plant.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Baikal skullcap.
