Bishop’s weed

Ammi majus L.
Evidence: ClinicalGenerally safe

PUVA therapy for vitiligo began not in a laboratory but in an Egyptian folk tradition: the hıltan seed eaten for vitiligo along the Nile for centuries became the drug of photochemotherapy when xanthotoxin was purified in Cairo in the 1940s. The WHO monograph puts this plant in the "use supported by clinical data" class and in the same breath says "treatment requires management by a health-care provider". One of the rare records in this catalog where a clinical evidence level and a low safety level stand together: dangerous because it works.

In short

What is Bishop’s weed?

Bishop’s weed (Ammi majus L.) is a plant of the Apiaceae (carrot family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Africa, Europe. Part used: the dried ripe fruit.

What is Bishop’s weed used for?

Traditional use: The World Health Organization monograph (Vol. Scientific evidence: clinical. Bishop’s weed is one of the plants in this catalog whose active principle passed into modern medicine: the xanthotoxin (8-MOP) of the fruit is the drug of PUVA photochemotherapy, applied together with UVA light, and has been used in psoriasis and vitiligo for decades.

Is Bishop’s weed safe?

Safety label: generally safe. Contraindicated in diseases with photosensitivity, cataract, invasive squamous-cell cancer, known sensitivity to psoralens and under 12; also in pregnancy, lactation, tuberculosis, liver and kidney disease, HIV and other autoimmune diseases — the WHO monograph. Serious burns may result, even through glass, if the correct dose and exposure schedule is not kept. Avoid sunlight for 8 hours after oral and 12–48 hours after topical treatment; sunglasses for 24 hours. In people who handled the fruit and then went into sunlight, erythema began within 48–72 hours and lasted days — even gathering the plant is phototoxic. Prolonged use or overdose may cause nausea, vertigo, constipation and sleeplessness. Avoid foods containing furanocoumarins (limes, figs, parsley, celery, carrots, mustard) during treatment. Safety level Low: this is not a household plant but a clinical drug.

Traditional use

WHO monograph — use supported by clinical data

The World Health Organization monograph (Vol. 3, 2007) places this plant where few in this catalog stand: use supported by clinical data: treatment of skin disorders such as psoriasis and vitiligo. The reason is the furanocoumarins of the fruit — above all xanthotoxin (8-methoxypsoralen) — and their use together with UVA light in photochemotherapy (PUVA). In traditional medicine: emmenagogue, diuretic; leprosy, kidney stones, urinary infections.

Turkey

There is no monograph for this plant on the Turkish list. It grows on field margins in western and southern Turkey. Native to Egypt, the species was used for vitiligo in Egyptian folk medicine for centuries; modern PUVA therapy emerged from that tradition in Egypt in the 1940s.

From Egypt to the dermatology clinic

The WHO monograph gives a dose of 0.02–0.04 g of fruit a day or 0.25–0.7 mg/kg of xanthotoxin and adds: clinical treatment requires management by a health-care provider. The only monograph sentence in this catalog that means, outright, "not for home use".

In nature

Where it grows
Native to Egypt; widespread in the Mediterranean, Europe and western Asia. Field margins in western and southern Turkey.
Harvest season
White umbels in summer; the fruit ripens in late summer.
Local names
Hıltan, büyük hıltan — common Turkish names; Arabic khella.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ It shares both the Turkish "hıltan" and the Arabic "khella" with toothpick weed (Ammi visnaga); their active principles (psoralen / khellin) and uses are entirely different. Umbellifer seeds should not be gathered from the wild; the family contains lethal species such as hemlock.

Preparation

This record gives no dose for home use. The WHO monograph’s dose (0.02–0.04 g of fruit daily; xanthotoxin 0.25–0.7 mg/kg) is for photochemotherapy, and the monograph requires the treatment to be managed by a physician. Lotions are applied only under direct medical supervision and are not dispensed to the patient.

What science says

Bishop’s weed is one of the plants in this catalog whose active principle passed into modern medicine: the xanthotoxin (8-MOP) of the fruit is the drug of PUVA photochemotherapy, applied together with UVA light, and has been used in psoriasis and vitiligo for decades. The 2015 Cochrane review (interventions for vitiligo; 96 studies, 4,512 participants) takes PUVA as a comparator standard: in a meta-analysis of three studies, narrowband UVB showed a non-significant advantage over PUVA in >75% repigmentation (RR 1.60; 95% CI 0.74–3.45) with less nausea and erythema — PUVA works, but has largely given way to the safer UVB. Most studies are small; combination treatments do better. Evidence level Clinical — but that evidence belongs not to a tea of the plant but to purified psoralen and a measured light dose.

Safety

Generally safe

Contraindicated in diseases with photosensitivity, cataract, invasive squamous-cell cancer, known sensitivity to psoralens and under 12; also in pregnancy, lactation, tuberculosis, liver and kidney disease, HIV and other autoimmune diseases — the WHO monograph. Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Other photosensitisersThe WHO monograph: toxicity is increased with coal tar, dithranol, griseofulvin, nalidixic acid, phenothiazines, sulfonamides and tetracyclines. Most of these drugs cause photosensitivity themselves.
Foods with furanocoumarinsThe monograph’s food warning is an interaction list: limes, figs, parsley, celery, cloves, lemons, mustard and carrots add to the psoralen load.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Bishop’s weed.
