Tea tree

Melaleuca alternifolia (Maiden & Betche) Cheel
Evidence: LimitedCaution

One of the most misleadingly named plants: it is called tea tree in English and çay ağacı in Turkish, yet it is not related to the tea we drink — it is in the myrtle family, a distant relative of eucalyptus. The name comes from eighteenth-century sailors on the Australian coast brewing a drink from its leaves. So the word "tea" is a borrowed comparison, and today it travels with the opposite warning: this plant’s oil is not drunk.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the names "melaloyka, çay ağacı"; M. linariifolia var. alternifolia is a synonym. ⚠️ It has nothing to do with the tea we drink (Camellia sinensis) — the shared name is misleading.

In short

What is Tea tree?

Tea tree (Melaleuca alternifolia (Maiden & Betche) Cheel) is a plant of the Myrtaceae (mersingiller) family. Traditions that use it: Oceania. Part used: leaves and branch tips, the essential oil.

What is Tea tree used for?

Traditional use: The plant is native to the coast of New South Wales; the oil distilled from its leaves was commercialised in the early twentieth century and is now among the best-selling essential oils. Scientific evidence: limited. On acne the evidence is no longer a single trial.

Is Tea tree safe?

Safety label: caution. Not to be swallowed and not to be inhaled. Both the EMA and the Turkish monograph state this explicitly: the Turkish one says "not to be used internally or by inhalation", and the EMA keeps the "not to be swallowed" condition even on its oral-mucosa indication. Never use undiluted. Avoid contact with the eyes and ears. Not for anyone hypersensitive to the active substance or to colophony (rosin); the Turkish monograph adds that people sensitive to plants of the Myrtaceae family can have serious allergic reactions even from external use. Not used under 12. Reported undesirable effects: smarting pain, mild pruritus, burning, irritation, itching, stinging, erythema, oedema (contact dermatitis) and other allergic reactions; frequencies unknown. Burn-like skin reactions have also been reported, rarely (<1/10,000). If a rash develops, stop. Not recommended in pregnancy or lactation for lack of data.

Traditional use

Australia — where it comes from

The plant is native to the coast of New South Wales; the oil distilled from its leaves was commercialised in the early twentieth century and is now among the best-selling essential oils.

Europe — four indications

The EMA monograph places all four indications under "traditional use": (1) small superficial wounds and insect bites; (2) small boils — furuncles and mild acne; (3) relief of itching and irritation in mild athlete’s foot; (4) symptomatic treatment of minor inflammation of the oral mucosa. The first three are topical. The fourth is applied inside the mouth, but the monograph attaches one condition there too: not to be swallowed. So the "not for oral use" warning and the oral-mucosa indication do not conflict — it is used like a mouthwash and spat out, not drunk.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "leaves and branch tips, the essential oil" and sets three firm limits: not to be used orally or by inhalation, avoid contact with eyes and ears, and never use undiluted.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to the swamps and streamsides of the northern New South Wales coast in Australia. It has no wild distribution in Turkey and is imported as oil.
Harvest season
Evergreen; leaves can be harvested year-round, and the oil is obtained by steam distillation.
Local names
Çay ağacı, melaloyka — both appear in the Turkish monograph.

Mix-up risk

The most dangerous confusion comes from the name: "tea tree oil" suggests something drinkable. It is not taken by mouth. Oils of other Melaleuca species (niaouli, cajuput) are also sold; their composition differs and the monograph covers M. alternifolia only.

Preparation

The essential oil is never used undiluted; products contain it at a low proportion in a carrier oil or base. Not swallowed and not inhaled — even on the oral-mucosa indication it is used like a mouthwash and spat out. Keep away from eyes and ears. Not used under 12. If a rash develops, stop using it.

What science says

On acne the evidence is no longer a single trial. A systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2026 in Phytotherapy Research pooled seven clinical studies (445 patients) and found that tea tree oil was associated with a reduction in acne severity compared with control groups — a pooled odds ratio of 0.74 (95% CI 0.63–0.88). The effect is statistically significant but modest, which is the review’s own word for it. Adverse events were predominantly local and mild; one striking detail is that mild itching was less frequent with tea tree oil than with the control treatments (pOR 0.09, 95% CI 0.03–0.23), with similar trends for dryness, burning, erythema and scaling. No publication bias was evident and overall risk of bias was assessed as low. The review concludes that short-term tolerability is generally acceptable, but that larger, high-quality trials with standardised formulations and long-term follow-up are needed to define its clinical role. Before that meta-analysis the evidence rested on one trial: a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled study published in 2007 in the Indian Journal of Dermatology, Venereology and Leprology found a 5% tea tree oil gel superior to placebo in mild to moderate acne. Cochrane’s 2015 review of complementary therapies for acne singles out that same trial and states its caution plainly: there is low quality evidence that tea tree oil may reduce total skin lesion counts, and it came from a single trial of 60 participants. The evidence level is Limited: there is now a pooled finding, but the effect is modest and both reviews ask for better trials.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No drug interactions reported in either monographBoth leave the interaction section empty. No systemic interaction is expected because the approved use is topical.
Use alongside other topical productsNo interaction study exists, but applying several irritant products to the same area raises the risk of a skin reaction. If a prescription acne treatment is in use, ask a doctor before adding tea tree oil.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Tea tree.
