Schisandra

Schisandra chinensis (Turcz.) Baill.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

Its name is a taste experiment: Chinese wǔ wèi zi, "five-flavour seed" — believed to carry sweet, sour, bitter, salty and pungent in one fruit. The most striking finding in the WHO monograph, though, is not taste but enzymes: the plant induces the liver’s drug-metabolising enzymes much as St John’s wort does, and the monograph, while saying "no interactions reported", places a list running from warfarin to ciclosporin under medical supervision. Behind the berry sold as an "adaptogen" on the supplement shelf stand a labour-inducing tincture and enzyme-inducing lignans.

Other names
No established Turkish name; on the supplement market it goes as "şisandra" or "beş tat meyvesi" — a translation of the Chinese wǔ wèi zi ("five-flavour seed"): the fruit is said to carry sweet, sour, bitter, salty and pungent at once. Korean omija. Synonyms Kadsura chinensis Turcz., Maximowiczia chinensis Rupr. No Turkish or EU monograph; the record rests on the WHO monograph (Vol. 3, 2007) and PubMed.

In short

What is Schisandra?

Schisandra (Schisandra chinensis (Turcz.) Baill.) is a plant of the Schisandraceae family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia. Part used: the dried ripe fruit.

What is Schisandra used for?

Traditional use: The World Health Organization monograph (Vol. Scientific evidence: limited. There is no evidence for schisandra in the monograph indications, but there are two single-ingredient, placebo-controlled randomised trials — both in other fields.

Is Schisandra safe?

Safety label: caution. Symptoms of overdose include restlessness, insomnia and dyspnoea — the WHO monograph’s warning. Not in pregnancy: the monograph reports an uncontrolled study in which a tincture given to pregnant women induced labour after the second dose, and increased uterine contractions in rabbits — the plant stimulates the uterus. Heartburn, stomach pain, anorexia, allergic skin reactions and urticaria have been reported. The monograph gives no contraindications; but its interaction section (below) is among the most extensive in this catalog.

Traditional use

WHO monograph — the fruit

The World Health Organization monograph (Vol. 3, 2007): no use supported by clinical data — some clinical evidence exists for psychosis, gastritis, hepatitis and fatigue, but controlled trials are lacking. Uses described in pharmacopoeias: chronic cough and asthma, diabetes, urinary tract disorders; as a general tonic for fatigue associated with illness. In traditional medicine: astringent, antitussive, antidiarrhoeal, expectorant, sedative. Daily dose 1.5–6 g of dried fruit.

Turkey

There is no monograph for this plant on the Turkish list; it does not grow in Turkey. It is sold as an imported adaptogen supplement, in capsules and as dried fruit.

Korea and China — omija tea

In Korea a cold tea and syrup are made from the omija (five-flavour) berry; in Chinese medicine it is a liver and kidney tonic. In the 20th century Soviet researchers studied it alongside Siberian ginseng as an "adaptogen"; its Western reputation comes from there.

In nature

Where it grows
The Russian Far East, north-eastern China, Korea, Japan; a climber of damp forests. Cultivated in China and Korea. It does not grow in Turkey.
Harvest season
Bright red grape-like clusters in autumn; the fruit is gathered ripe and dried.
Local names
No Turkish name; Chinese wǔ wèi zi, Korean omija.

Mix-up risk

Southern schisandra (Schisandra sphenanthera) is a separate drug in the Chinese pharmacopoeia and can stand in for S. chinensis in products; the monograph is for S. chinensis only.

Preparation

The WHO monograph gives an average daily dose of 1.5–6.0 g of dried fruit. Not before bedtime (it may cause insomnia); not together with sedatives or alcohol. Not in pregnancy.

What science says

There is no evidence for schisandra in the monograph indications, but there are two single-ingredient, placebo-controlled randomised trials — both in other fields. A double-blind trial published in 2016 in Climacteric reported that 6 weeks of a standardised extract lowered the Kupperman index significantly against placebo in 36 menopausal women (p=0.042) — notably for hot flushes, sweating and palpitations; 36 people, one centre. A double-blind trial published in 2021 in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition found that in 54 adults over 50, 12 weeks of 1 g of extract a day plus light walking raised knee-extensor strength against placebo (right, 10.2 Nm; 95% CI 3.7–16.8), but no difference in muscle mass, inflammatory or antioxidant markers or quality of life; no adverse events. Two small trials, two separate outcomes, both outside the monograph — evidence level Limited. No trial directly tests the monograph’s "tonic for fatigue" indication.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Sedatives and alcoholThe WHO monograph states the fruit may have CNS-depressant effects and should not be used together with sedatives or alcohol.
Drugs metabolised by CYP450 — ciclosporin, warfarin, protease inhibitors, hormonesThe monograph’s most important line: the fruit stimulates hepatic cytochrome P450 (inducing phase I and II enzymes). Though no interaction has been reported, it may lower the levels of drugs processed by those enzymes — ciclosporin, warfarin, HIV protease inhibitors, oestrogen-progestogen combinations — and the monograph allows co-administration only under medical supervision. The same logic and the same list as St John’s wort.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Schisandra.
