BT-261 · Campanulaceae

Balloon flower root

Platycodon grandiflorus (Jacq.) A. DC.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

The same root is a pickle on the Korean table, a cough remedy in a Chinese pharmacy, an ornament in a Turkish garden — and in its latest clinical trials a granule protecting the hearts of breast-cancer patients from chemotherapy. The WHO found no clinical data for any use of this plant in 1999; the randomised trials that came twenty years later are in none of the indications the monograph wrote. A plant’s evidence can turn up somewhere entirely other than where tradition looked.

Other names
Turkish balon çiçeği — a rendering of the English name balloon flower; Chinese jiégěng, Korean doraji, Japanese kikyō. The WHO monograph defines the drug as "Radix Platycodi" — the root. No Turkish or EU monograph. Known in Turkey as a garden plant; in Korea the root is a vegetable.

In short

What is Balloon flower root?

Balloon flower root (Platycodon grandiflorus (Jacq.) A. DC.) is a plant of the Campanulaceae family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia. Part used: the root.

What is Balloon flower root used for?

Traditional use: "Uses supported by clinical data": none. Scientific evidence: limited. There is no clinical trial of balloon flower in the monograph indication (cough); but it does have single-ingredient, placebo-controlled randomised trials — in a quite different field.

Is Balloon flower root safe?

Safety label: caution. Not to be given by injection — the WHO monograph’s one warning: the extracts are strongly haemolytic (they rupture red cells); taken orally this is not a problem, as the saponins are not absorbed. CNS depressant; driving and machinery inadvisable together with alcohol and other depressants. Not recommended in pregnancy or lactation. No adverse-reaction information in the monograph; no serious adverse events reported in the cardiac trials. Chinese tradition does not combine it with Gentiana scabra or Bletilla.

Traditional use

WHO monograph (1999)

"Uses supported by clinical data": none. In pharmacopoeias and traditional systems: expectorant and antitussive — coughs, colds, upper respiratory infections, sore throat, tonsillitis, chest congestion; in Chinese medicine productive cough, pertussis and asthma. 2–9 g daily. Warning: the extracts have a very pronounced haemolytic effect and must not be given by injection. CNS depressant; driving with alcohol inadvisable.

Turkey

Not on the Turkish list; grown in Turkish gardens as an ornamental, with no medicinal use.

Korea — doraji

In Korean cooking the root (doraji) is a vegetable and a pickle; in Korea and Japan an ingredient of cough syrups and lozenges. In Chinese medicine it counts as the root that "carries the medicine upward to the lung" — a guiding ingredient in formulas.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to China, Korea, Japan and eastern Siberia; widely cultivated in Korea and China. An ornamental in Turkey.
Harvest season
Blue-purple bell flowers opening from balloon-like buds in summer; the root is lifted from 2–3-year-old plants in autumn.
Local names
Balon çiçeği; Chinese jiégěng, Korean doraji, Japanese kikyō.

Mix-up risk

On the Korean market doraji, ginseng and Adenophora roots look alike; the monograph is for Platycodon only. Garden cultivars may be white or pink too.

Preparation

The WHO monograph gives 2–9 g of root daily as a decoction or extract, orally only. Never by injection. Not recommended in pregnancy or lactation for lack of data.

What science says

There is no clinical trial of balloon flower in the monograph indication (cough); but it does have single-ingredient, placebo-controlled randomised trials — in a quite different field. A trial published in 2020 in Integrative Cancer Therapies randomised 125 early breast-cancer patients on anthracycline chemotherapy to balloon flower granules or placebo: no difference in severe heart failure (rare events), but subclinical heart failure (8.2% vs 21.9%) and troponin rises were less frequent in the balloon flower group; the chemotherapy’s antitumour effect was unchanged. A 2026 study by the same team in patients on trastuzumab continues the line. One centre, one team, China — and nothing to do with the cough indication the WHO wrote. Evidence level Limited.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Alcohol, tranquillisers and sleeping medicinesThe WHO monograph notes a possible synergistic effect with CNS depressants.
Gentiana scabra and Bletilla (traditional incompatibility)The monograph relays, from Chinese sources, that it is not to be combined with these two drugs — a rule of tradition, not pharmacological data.
