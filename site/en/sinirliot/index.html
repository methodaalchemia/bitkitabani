BT-197 · Plantaginaceae (plantain family)

Ribwort plantain

Plantago lanceolata L.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

The Turkish name is an anatomical comparison: the prominent parallel veins running the length of the leaf were taken for nerves, making the plant "sinirli ot", the nervy herb. The English name, ribwort, looks at the same thing through a different comparison: the veins were taken for ribs. Two languages looked at the same lines on the same leaf and saw two different body parts — one nerves, one ribs.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the name "dar yapraklı sinirli ot" (narrow-leaved sinirli ot); P. lanceolata var. lanceolata and f. lanceolata are synonyms. The Turkish name comes from the prominent parallel veins of the leaf — "sinir" here means vein.

What is Ribwort plantain?

Ribwort plantain (Plantago lanceolata L.) is a plant of the Plantaginaceae (plantain family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the leaf.

What is Ribwort plantain used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places all three indications under "traditional use": (1) as a demulcent for the symptomatic treatment of oral or pharyngeal irritations and the associated dry cough; (2) relief of cough associated with a cold; (3) treatment of minor inflammation of the skin. Scientific evidence: traditional. Ribwort plantain is among the commonest plants in the cough-syrup market, and the evidence picture is clear: there is no randomised trial of it on its own, and the products it appears in are multi-ingredient.

Is Ribwort plantain safe?

Safety label: caution. If dyspnoea, fever or purulent sputum occur, consult a doctor — the most important warning here, because the plant is used for cough and those three signs can mean the cough belongs to an infection or something more serious. The Turkish monograph also states that it can cause diarrhoea and low blood pressure. Not for anyone hypersensitive to the active substance or to ribwort plantain pollen — naming pollen sensitivity as a contraindication is a detail particular to this plant. Age limits vary by preparation: the lowest for oral and oromucosal use is 3 years, rising to 6 or 12 for some preparations; 3 years for skin use. The Turkish monograph, for its part, does not recommend use under 18. Not recommended in pregnancy or lactation for lack of data. If symptoms do not resolve within a week, consult a doctor.

Traditional use

Europe — three indications

The EMA monograph places all three indications under "traditional use": (1) as a demulcent for the symptomatic treatment of oral or pharyngeal irritations and the associated dry cough; (2) relief of cough associated with a cold; (3) treatment of minor inflammation of the skin. The mechanism of the first is in its name: the mucilage of the leaf forms a thin layer over the irritated surface of the throat.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the leaves" and adds two warnings: it can cause diarrhoea and low blood pressure; and if dyspnoea, fever or purulent sputum occur, consult a doctor. If symptoms do not resolve within a week, see a doctor.

Anatolia

Ribwort plantain grows everywhere in Turkey — meadows, roadsides, gardens — its leaves arranged in a flat rosette. In folk use it is crushed and laid on wounds and insect bites, matching the monograph’s third indication.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to Europe, Asia and North Africa, now spread worldwide. Very common in Turkey in meadows, roadsides, gardens and lawns.
Harvest season
From spring to autumn it flowers in short brown spikes at the tip of a long stalk; the leaves are gathered during flowering.
Local names
Sinirli ot, dar yapraklı sinirli ot. Names such as "sinir otu" and "damar otu" are also used in Turkey.

Mix-up risk

Broad-leaved plantain (Plantago major) of the same genus grows side by side with it everywhere and gets confused with it; the distinguishing feature is the leaf shape — this one’s leaf is long and spear-like, P. major’s leaf is broad and egg-shaped. Karnıyarık (psyllium, Plantago ovata) in our catalog, for its part, is of the same genus but is another plant whose seed is used and which works through its fibre effect; the EU monographs deal with the two separately.

Preparation

The monograph describes oral, oromucosal and topical forms, with a separate age limit for each preparation: the general limit for oral and oromucosal use is 3 years, rising to 6 or 12 for some preparations; for skin use, 3 years. If symptoms do not resolve within a week, consult a doctor.

What science says

Ribwort plantain is among the commonest plants in the cough-syrup market, and the evidence picture is clear: there is no randomised trial of it on its own, and the products it appears in are multi-ingredient. A review published in 2025 in Minerva Pediatrics addresses this field directly — the nutraceuticals and medical devices task force of the Italian Society of Pediatric Allergy and Immunology (SIAIP) examined the best-selling natural cough products in Italy. Ribwort plantain is among them (alongside marshmallow, grindelia, Italian everlasting, thyme and honey). The review’s verdict is in its title — "darkness and shadow" — and stated plainly in the text: few clinical studies have investigated such remedies, and the evidence generally is scarce and of poor quality. The review tells doctors to prescribe these products only knowing their precise composition, literature, posology and safety, and sets against them the observation that peripheral antitussive agents are effective and safe. The EU monograph rests its three indications on long use. The one mechanism the monograph gives is plausible and modest: the mucilage of the leaf acts as a demulcent — it coats the irritation; it does not treat an infection. The evidence level is Traditional.

Safety

Caution

If dyspnoea, fever or purulent sputum occur, consult a doctor — the most important warning here, because the plant is used for cough and those three signs can mean the cough belongs to an infection or something more serious. The Turkish monograph also states that it can cause diarrhoea and low blood pressure. Age limits vary by preparation: the lowest for oral and oromucosal use is 3 years, rising to 6 or 12 for some preparations; 3 years for skin use. The Turkish monograph, for its part, does not recommend use under 18. Not recommended in pregnancy or lactation for lack of data. If symptoms do not resolve within a week, consult a doctor.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
WarfarinThe Turkish monograph reports one interaction, with warfarin. The EU monograph lists nothing, so this warning exists only in the Turkish text. Because warfarin’s effect is balanced within a narrow range, a small shift changes bleeding risk; anyone on warfarin should tell their doctor.
Oral medicines (through the mucilage)No study exists; the issue comes from the mechanism. Mucilage-containing plants can form a layer in the gut and slow the absorption of medicines taken at the same time. The practical advice is simple: do not take a mucilaginous preparation at the same hour as a medicine; leave a gap.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing; the Turkish one reports one medicine.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Ribwort plantain.
