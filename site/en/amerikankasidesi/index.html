American skullcap

Scutellaria lateriflora L.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

The English name "mad-dog skullcap" survives from the plant’s entry into the medical literature as a remedy against canine rabies. The review in the Journal of Medicinally Active Plants writes that the claim was later disproven; the name stayed.

Other names
English American skullcap, blue skullcap, mad-dog skullcap. The genus was first named Cassida by Tournefort in 1716 and later took the name Scutellaria. The part used is the dried leaves and stems. The Baikal skullcap of Chinese medicine (Scutellaria baicalensis) is a different species.

In short

What is American skullcap?

American skullcap (Scutellaria lateriflora L.) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: North America. Part used: dried aerial parts (leaves and stems).

What is American skullcap used for?

Traditional use: A review published in the Journal of Medicinally Active Plants reports that the Cherokee used an infusion or decoction of the plant for breast pain, to expel the afterbirth and to bring on menstruation. Scientific evidence: limited. In the 2003 animal study in Phytomedicine, an extract given by mouth produced behavioural changes in rats that indicate reduced anxiety in the open-field and elevated plus-maze tests.

Is American skullcap safe?

Safety label: caution. ⚠️ LiverTox, the database of the US National Institutes of Health, writes that this plant has been linked to cases of clinically apparent liver injury, though in most of them it was used together with other botanicals. According to the 2009 study in Phytochemical Analysis, products have occasionally been adulterated with germander (Teucrium) species, which contain diterpenes that can harm the liver. People with liver disease should not use it; if jaundice, dark urine, nausea or weakness appear during use, the product should be stopped and a doctor consulted. There are no safety data for pregnancy, breastfeeding or children; because traditional records list it as a means of bringing on menstruation, it should not be used in pregnancy. People taking sedative medicines should consult their doctor. In the 2025 trial no adverse effect was reported over 56 days of use.

Traditional use

Cherokee and Iroquois — women’s health and throat complaints

A review published in the Journal of Medicinally Active Plants reports that the Cherokee used an infusion or decoction of the plant for breast pain, to expel the afterbirth and to bring on menstruation. According to the same review, the Iroquois used a preparation of the root to prevent smallpox and for throat ailments. These uses are traditional only; none has been tested clinically.

North America — a mild sedative

The review writes that the plant has been used as a mild sedative for about 200 years. LiverTox states that the dried leaves and stems are used as a herbal medicine and as tea for anxiety, stress and insomnia. A study published in Phytomedicine in 2003 likewise describes the plant as a traditional sedative and a remedy for nervous disorders such as anxiety.

Medical history — the rabies claim

According to the review, the plant’s first mention in the medical literature was its use against canine rabies, and the name "mad-dog" comes from this. The same review notes that the claim was later disproven.

Today — the supplement market

The plant is now sold as tea, capsules, extracts, tinctures and granules. The review reports that the world harvest rose from about 6,364 kg in 1997 to 15,910 kg in 2001. The same source draws attention to the problem of products on the market being adulterated with other species.

In nature

Where it grows
According to the review it is a wetland plant; it grows on stream banks, in marshes, in wet meadows and in moist wooded areas. It occurs across most of North America, from Canada to Florida and west to British Columbia, Oregon and New Mexico. It is not native to Türkiye.
Harvest season
The corolla of its blue flowers is 6–8 mm long. The sources relied on here give no calendar for flowering or gathering.
Local names
It has no established local name in Turkish.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ Germander species (Teucrium canadense and Teucrium chamaedrys) look similar to this plant and have found their way into commercial products. The 2009 study showed that the two genera can be told apart by their phenolic profiles and that even a 1% admixture can be measured by mass spectrometry. Baikal skullcap (Scutellaria baicalensis) is a different species and has its own record on this site.

Preparation

No EMA, WHO or Turkish (TİTCK) monograph could be found; there is no accepted dose. According to the review the plant is prepared as tea, capsules, extracts and tinctures; the 40–60% ethanol used in tinctures extracts about 70% of the flavonoids. The amounts used in clinical trials are research doses, not recommendations: the 2014 trial gave 350 mg three times a day, the 2025 trial 400 mg a day of an extract-based supplement. The label cannot show that a product really contains this species; the adulteration problem is described below.

What science says

In the 2003 animal study in Phytomedicine, an extract given by mouth produced behavioural changes in rats that indicate reduced anxiety in the open-field and elevated plus-maze tests. The same study measured 40 mg/g of baicalin in a 50% ethanol extract and 33 mg/g of baicalein in a 95% ethanol extract, and recalled that these flavonoids are known to bind to the benzodiazepine site of the GABA-A receptor. Evidence in humans is thin. A double-blind, placebo-controlled study from 2003 reported an anxiety-reducing effect in healthy volunteers. In a crossover trial published in Phytotherapy Research in 2014, in 43 healthy volunteers no significant difference from placebo was found on the anxiety scale (p = 0.191); only the overall mood score improved, and the authors list a carryover effect and the participants’ already low anxiety as limitations. In a 66-person crossover trial published in Nutrients in 2025, 56 days of use significantly improved the sleep quality score in mild to moderate primary insomnia. The trials are small and short.

Safety

Caution

⚠️ LiverTox, the database of the US National Institutes of Health, writes that this plant has been linked to cases of clinically apparent liver injury, though in most of them it was used together with other botanicals. According to the 2009 study in Phytochemical Analysis, products have occasionally been adulterated with germander (Teucrium) species, which contain diterpenes that can harm the liver. People with liver disease should not use it; if jaundice, dark urine, nausea or weakness appear during use, the product should be stopped and a doctor consulted. There are no safety data for pregnancy, breastfeeding or children; because traditional records list it as a means of bringing on menstruation, it should not be used in pregnancy. People taking sedative medicines should consult their doctor. In the 2025 trial no adverse effect was reported over 56 days of use.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Sedatives, sleep medicines and benzodiazepines (theoretical)The baicalin and baicalein in the plant are known to bind to the benzodiazepine site of the GABA-A receptor (Phytomedicine, 2003). Drowsiness may increase with medicines acting in the same direction; no interaction study has been done in humans.
Medicines that burden the liver and other herbal productsAccording to LiverTox, most of the reported cases of liver injury involved products in which the plant was taken together with other botanicals. People taking medicines that affect the liver should not use this plant without asking their doctor.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying American skullcap.
