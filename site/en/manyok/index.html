In short

What is Cassava?

Cassava (Manihot esculenta Crantz) is a plant of the Euphorbiaceae (spurge family) family. Traditions that use it: Amazon, Africa. Part used: starchy storage roots.

What is Cassava used for?

Traditional use: A review in Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition describes cassava as a principal energy source for 300–500 million people in South America, Africa and Asia. Scientific evidence: traditional. A 2017 review in the Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences reports that in communities relying on cassava, blood cyanide concentrations of up to 80 micromoles per litre are commonplace and produce signs of acute toxicity.

Is Cassava safe?

Safety label: caution. Adequately processed cassava is the daily food of hundreds of millions. The risk lies not in the plant but in the insufficiently processed bitter variety. According to the reviews relied on here the outcome can be konzo, a symmetrical, permanent and irreversible paralysis of the legs, and cognitive delay in children; the reported epidemics have struck children and women of childbearing age hardest. Susceptibility is increased by younger age, female sex and a protein-deficient diet; the epidemics have appeared during droughts, when the diet narrows to cassava almost alone. These sources name no known antidote. No source assesses non-food use in pregnancy, lactation or children.

Traditional use

Tropical America, Africa and Asia — the energy source of 300–500 million people

A review in Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition describes cassava as a principal energy source for 300–500 million people in South America, Africa and Asia. Its cultivation covers some 11 million hectares a year, and more than half of the 105 million tonnes produced is eaten by people. The same review notes that it is among the crops converting the most solar energy per unit area into soluble carbohydrate, while its protein content and quality are poor and the lifted roots spoil within a few days.

Africa — processing the bitter variety, and konzo

A review in the Journal of the Neurological Sciences records that the paralytic condition called konzo has invariably been seen alongside the monotonous consumption of inadequately processed bitter cassava roots with very little protein in the diet. Traditional processing — soaking, fermenting and cooking — is therefore not a culinary preference but a required step.

Brazil — composition that differs by cultivar

A 2024 study in Food Research International compared four cassava cultivars from north-eastern and southern Brazil and found that fibre, resistant starch, fructo-oligosaccharide, organic acid and phenolic content varied between them. The work was carried out in the laboratory.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew’s records its range covers 90 regions; it is grown almost throughout the tropics. Its origin is the Amazon basin, and because it withstands drought its planting increases in dry years.
Harvest season
It does not grow in Turkey; the sources relied on here give no collection calendar for Turkey. Roots are lifted 8–24 months after planting and spoil within days.
Local names
In Turkish manyok, kasava; its starch tapioca.

Mix-up risk

Bitter and sweet varieties look alike from the outside but differ in cyanogenic glycoside content; every reported poisoning involves the insufficiently processed bitter variety. Tapioca pearls or flour are not the same thing as the root itself.

Preparation

No official monograph sets an accepted dose; cassava is a food. The sources relied on here describe no home medicinal preparation. Roots of the bitter variety are not eaten without soaking, fermenting and cooking; wherever konzo has been reported, the common factor is that these steps were left incomplete. Because the roots are poor in protein, the review relied on here stresses the importance of a protein source alongside them. Tapioca sold in Turkey is starch separated from the root.

What science says

A 2017 review in the Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences reports that in communities relying on cassava, blood cyanide concentrations of up to 80 micromoles per litre are commonplace and produce signs of acute toxicity. The long-term effects are the irreversible spastic paralysis called konzo and cognitive loss, mainly in sequential processing (visual-spatial analysis). The compounds the review holds responsible are cyanide, thiocyanate, cyanate and 2-iminothiazolidine-4-carboxylic acid; susceptibility is increased by younger age, female sex and a protein-deficient diet. A review in Brain Research Bulletin records konzo in the Democratic Republic of Congo, Mozambique, Tanzania, the Central African Republic, Angola, Cameroon and Zambia, describes the picture as symmetrical, permanent and irreversible with no sensory or genitourinary signs, and states that the mechanism of the disease is still unknown. An analysis in Annals of Neurology shows the epidemics arriving cyclically with droughts, the dominant cycle in Congo being 3–6 years. These sources contain no clinical trial testing cassava as a treatment for any condition.
