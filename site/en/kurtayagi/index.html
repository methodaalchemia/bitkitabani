Gypsywort

Lycopus europaeus L.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

The effect of gypsywort on the thyroid has been studied for more than 70 years: an experimental study published in Germany in 1954 examined the effect of concentrates of the plant on the thyroid gland and iodine metabolism. The 2008 study, in turn, was the first to use T3 and T4 excretion in 24-hour urine as a measure.

Other names
English gypsywort, European bugleweed; the pharmaceutical drug name is Lycopi herba (Lycopi europaei herba). According to Kew’s plant list its native range is the whole of Europe, Türkiye, North Africa, the Caucasus, Iran, Central Asia and Siberia; it was later carried to North America. There is no EMA, WHO or TİTCK monograph.

In short

What is Gypsywort?

Gypsywort (Lycopus europaeus L.) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: Europe, Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Asia and Siberia. Part used: flowering aerial parts.

What is Gypsywort used for?

Traditional use: A 1994 study (Winterhoff et al.) writes that gypsywort extracts are used in folk medicine for symptoms caused by an overactive thyroid gland. Scientific evidence: limited. The human data come from two open studies; neither has a placebo group.

Is Gypsywort safe?

Safety label: caution. ⚠️ It affects thyroid hormones. In rat studies it lowered T3, T4 and TSH levels; for this reason people with an underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism), those taking thyroid medication and people without a diagnosed thyroid condition should not use it without consulting a doctor. Symptoms such as palpitations, weight loss and tremor may be the sign of a serious thyroid or heart disease; temporising with a herb delays diagnosis. In the same rat study the reproductive hormone LH also fell; it should not be used in pregnancy or while breastfeeding, and there are no data for children. In the two open studies (4 weeks and 3 months) the product was well tolerated and no adverse events were reported; the safety of longer use is unknown.

Traditional use

Central Europe — a folk remedy for mild thyroid overactivity

A 1994 study (Winterhoff et al.) writes that gypsywort extracts are used in folk medicine for symptoms caused by an overactive thyroid gland. A 2006 study (Vonhoff et al.) describes this use as “mild forms of hyperthyroidism”. This is the plant’s traditional field of use; treating thyroid disease is a matter for a doctor.

Germany and Austria — a ready-made herbal medicine

According to a 2013 study (Eiling et al.), gypsywort extracts are used clinically to control the autonomic and irritative symptoms of mild hyperthyroidism; the extract is sold as a ready-made medicine in tablet and drop form. Experimental studies of the plant’s effect on the thyroid go back to 1954.

In nature

Where it grows
A perennial herb. Its native range is Europe, Türkiye, North Africa, the Caucasus, Iran, Central Asia and Siberia; it was later carried to eastern North America.
Harvest season
Flowers in summer; the flowering aerial parts are gathered in this period.
Local names
No established local name in Turkish.

Mix-up risk

According to Kew’s plant list, some plants recorded in the past in North America as Lycopus europaeus are in fact Lycopus americanus, a separate species. The species within the genus resemble one another; the results of the studies apply only to the standardised extracts examined.

Preparation

The traditional form is a hydroalcoholic extract of the flowering aerial parts; the clinical studies used standardised tablets and drops that are pharmacy products. Because there is no EMA, WHO or TİTCK monograph, no accepted dose is given on this page. ⚠️ This is a plant that affects thyroid hormones; it is not for treating thyroid disease on one’s own by preparing a tea or extract at home. It should not be used without thyroid values being measured and without medical monitoring.

What science says

The human data come from two open studies; neither has a placebo group. In the 2008 prospective, two-armed open study (Beer et al.) patients with a low TSH level and symptoms of hyperthyroidism were followed for 3 months; in those taking the plant urinary T4 excretion increased significantly (p=0.032) and symptoms such as morning heart rate decreased. In the 2013 post-marketing surveillance study 403 patients were observed; the responder rate was 72.6% in the prospective arm, 96.5% in the retrospective arm and 41.2% in the untreated control arm, and no adverse events were reported. In rats an extract given by mouth lowered T3, T4 and TSH levels within 24 hours and also reduced the hormone LH (Winterhoff et al.). In rats made hyperthyroid with thyroxine, on the other hand, the extract reduced heart rate, blood pressure and cardiac enlargement without changing hormone levels; its effect was close to that of the beta blocker atenolol (Vonhoff et al.). There is no randomised, placebo-controlled trial; the mechanism of action is also unclear.

Safety

Caution

⚠️ It affects thyroid hormones. In rat studies it lowered T3, T4 and TSH levels; for this reason people with an underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism), those taking thyroid medication and people without a diagnosed thyroid condition should not use it without consulting a doctor. Symptoms such as palpitations, weight loss and tremor may be the sign of a serious thyroid or heart disease; temporising with a herb delays diagnosis. In the same rat study the reproductive hormone LH also fell; it should not be used in pregnancy or while breastfeeding, and there are no data for children. Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Thyroid hormone medicines (levothyroxine)The plant lowers thyroid hormone levels in animal studies and increases urinary T4 excretion in humans; it may work against the effect of the drug.
Antithyroid drugs (methimazole, propylthiouracil)By acting in the same direction it may cause thyroid hormones to fall too far; combined use is considered only under medical monitoring. There is no interaction study in humans.
Beta blockers and medicines that slow the heart rateIn rats the extract lowered heart rate and blood pressure to an extent close to atenolol; an additive effect is possible.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Gypsywort.
