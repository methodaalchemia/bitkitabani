BT-295 · Rhamnaceae (buckthorn family)

Wild jujube

Ziziphus lotus (L.) Lam.
Evidence: TraditionalGenerally safe

The "lotus" in Homer’s Odyssey, which made the sailors forget their homeland, is most probably the fruit of this shrub, according to a botanical study from 2020. The same study shows that in antiquity the name "lotus" was given to very different plants, and that each has to be resolved separately.

Other names
In North Africa the shrub is called "sedra" and its fruit "nbeg" in Arabic; English wild jujube, French jujubier sauvage. It has no established Turkish name — the plant does not grow wild in Turkey, and "yabani hünnap" is a descriptive name used here to distinguish it from jujube (Ziziphus jujuba). Neither the European Medicines Agency nor the Turkish agency has a monograph for this species.

In short

What is Wild jujube?

Wild jujube (Ziziphus lotus (L.) Lam.) is a plant of the Rhamnaceae (buckthorn family) family. Traditions that use it: Africa, Anatolia and the Mediterranean. Part used: fruit; in traditional use also the leaf, root and root bark.

What is Wild jujube used for?

Traditional use: A comprehensive review published in Pharmaceuticals in 2023 writes that Ziziphus lotus is a species found across the Mediterranean area, traditionally used for diabetes, digestive problems, urinary tract problems, infectious diseases, cardiovascular disorders, neurological diseases and skin problems. Scientific evidence: traditional. In the sources relied on here there is no controlled trial in humans.

Is Wild jujube safe?

Safety label: generally safe. The fruit is a food and is traditionally eaten. The 2023 review reports that toxicity studies found plant extracts safe and free from toxicity; those studies were done in animals, and safety in humans has not been measured. The sources relied on here contain no separate assessment for pregnancy, lactation, children or drug interactions; because there are no data, concentrated extracts are best avoided in those groups. Since blood pressure was reported to fall in an animal study, anyone taking blood pressure medicine would do well to mention regular consumption to their doctor; no interaction study has been done. Cyclopeptide alkaloids have been identified in the stem and root bark; concentrated bark extracts are not the same thing as the fruit.

Traditional use

North Africa — fruit and folk medicine

A comprehensive review published in Pharmaceuticals in 2023 writes that Ziziphus lotus is a species found across the Mediterranean area, traditionally used for diabetes, digestive problems, urinary tract problems, infectious diseases, cardiovascular disorders, neurological diseases and skin problems. The review collected these uses from the literature; it makes no claim of efficacy.

Algeria and around — "sedra" and "nbeg"

According to a mini review published in the Journal of Medicinal Food in 2025, the plant is known in North Africa as "sedra" and its fruit as "nbeg". The same review records the fruit being used in traditional medicine for tuberculosis, bronchitis, liver disorders and gastrointestinal complaints, and argues that it is a neglected food resource.

The ancient Mediterranean — the "lotus" of the Lotophagi

A study published in Harvard Papers in Botany in 2020 compared the plants called "lotus" in antiquity through historiography, biogeography and ethnobotany, and concluded that the lotus of the Lotophagi (the lotus-eaters) is best explained as Ziziphus lotus. The authors rest this on the descriptions of the fruit by Herodotus, Theophrastus, Polybius and Pliny. The same study states that the lotus of Cyrenaica was instead Ziziphus spina-christi, and that this distinction is reliable.

In nature

Where it grows
According to observation records in GBIF the species is found across the Mediterranean belt, with most records from Israel, Spain, Morocco, Algeria, Cyprus, Tunisia, Italy, Syria and Jordan. There are no records from Turkey. It is a thorny shrub of dry, stony ground.
Harvest season
The sources relied on here give no collection calendar for Turkey; the plant does not grow there naturally.
Local names
It has no established Turkish local name. In North Africa the plant is known as "sedra" and its fruit as "nbeg".

Mix-up risk

It belongs to the same genus as jujube (Ziziphus jujuba) but is a different species: jujube comes from China, is grown in gardens and has a large fruit, while Ziziphus lotus is a thorny shrub growing wild on dry Mediterranean ground with a small fruit. What is written about jujube does not apply to this species, and the reverse. Ziziphus spina-christi, taken to be the "lotus" of Cyrenaica, is another species again.

Preparation

No official monograph sets an accepted dose; the fruit is a food and is eaten fresh or dried. The sources relied on here give no measure for a home preparation. Concentrated extracts made from the root, root bark and leaf are research material rather than food, and these sources give no dose for their use in people.

What science says

In the sources relied on here there is no controlled trial in humans. The 2023 review reports that plant extracts showed antidiabetic, antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects in cell and animal studies; the same review writes that more than 181 compounds have been identified, among them terpenoids, polyphenols, flavonoids, alkaloids and fatty acids. In their conclusion the authors state plainly that clinical trials are needed to prove efficacy. A study published in the Journal of Medicinal Food in 2026 examined an aqueous extract in rats, reporting increased expression of genes involved in nitric oxide synthesis and reductions in systolic, diastolic and mean arterial blood pressure. That is an animal study; it does not show the same effect in people. A study published in Chemistry & Biodiversity in 2022 examined the stem bark extract of the plant growing in Sicily and found a high phenolic and flavonoid content with tyrosinase-inhibiting and antioxidant activity in vitro — these are laboratory measurements.

Safety

Generally safe

The fruit is a food and is traditionally eaten. The 2023 review reports that toxicity studies found plant extracts safe and free from toxicity; those studies were done in animals, and safety in humans has not been measured. The sources relied on here contain no separate assessment for pregnancy, lactation, children or drug interactions; because there are no data, concentrated extracts are best avoided in those groups. Since blood pressure was reported to fall in an animal study, anyone taking blood pressure medicine would do well to mention regular consumption to their doctor; no interaction study has been done. Cyclopeptide alkaloids have been identified in the stem and root bark; concentrated bark extracts are not the same thing as the fruit.
