BT-258 · Apocynaceae (dogbane family)

Indian snakeroot

Rauvolfia serpentina (L.) Benth. ex Kurz
Evidence: ClinicalGenerally safe

From one plant root came one of the most important substances in the history of medicine: in the 1950s reserpine became both one of the first effective blood-pressure drugs and one of the first antipsychotics, and opened the door to understanding how the brain stores neurotransmitters. That is why the WHO monograph writes snakeroot not like a tea plant but like a prescription drug: two pages of contraindications, a depression and suicide warning, ten-day dose-adjustment intervals. The record in this catalog where the distance between "natural" and "harmless" is longest.

Other names
Turkish yılankökü, Hint yılankökü; English rauwolfia, Indian snakeroot; Hindi sarpagandha. The WHO monograph defines the drug as "Radix Rauwolfiae" — the dried root. No Turkish or EU monograph. The source of the modern blood-pressure drug reserpine; hence the most "drug-like" plant in this catalog.

In short

What is Indian snakeroot?

Indian snakeroot (Rauvolfia serpentina (L.) Benth. ex Kurz) is a plant of the Apocynaceae (dogbane family) family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia. Part used: the dried root.

What is Indian snakeroot used for?

Traditional use: "Uses supported by clinical data": mild essential hypertension — usually with a diuretic to prevent fluid retention. Scientific evidence: clinical. Snakeroot is the clearest example in this catalog of the passage from plant to drug: the reserpine in its root is a licensed antihypertensive.

Is Indian snakeroot safe?

Safety label: generally safe. It can cause depression — the WHO monograph’s gravest warning: drug-induced depression may persist for months after withdrawal and be severe enough to result in suicide; it must be stopped at the first sign of depression (despondency, early-morning insomnia, loss of appetite, impotence, self-deprecation). Contraindicated with a history of depression, during MAO-inhibitor therapy, in active ulcer, sinus-node disorders, ulcerative colitis, epilepsy and renal insufficiency. It impairs alertness; driving and operating machinery are inadvisable. Bradycardia and arrhythmia (especially with digoxin or quinidine), nasal congestion, oedema and increased gastric secretion have been reported. Not in pregnancy or lactation. Safety level Low: this is not a plant but a drug in the shape of a plant.

Traditional use

WHO monograph (1999)

"Uses supported by clinical data": mild essential hypertension — usually with a diuretic to prevent fluid retention. In pharmacopoeias: a tranquilliser in nervous and mental disorders. The monograph’s contraindication and warning sections are as long as a prescription drug’s: history of depression (especially with suicidal tendency), MAO-inhibitor therapy, active peptic ulcer, sinus-node disorders, ulcerative colitis, epilepsy, reduced renal function, electroconvulsive therapy.

Turkey

Snakeroot is not on the Turkish list; it does not grow in Turkey and is not found at herbalists. Drugs containing reserpine were used for years in Turkey too for hypertension; today they have given way to newer drug classes.

India — sarpagandha

Ayurveda’s plant for snakebite, insomnia and "madness"; its name is Sanskrit sarpagandha (snake scent). Reserpine, isolated from its root in the 1950s, became one of modern medicine’s first effective blood-pressure and antipsychotic drugs — the textbook case of a plant reaching the pharmacy shelf through a single unsynthesised substance.

In nature

Where it grows
Humid forests of India, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia; depleted in the wild by over-collection, listed in CITES Appendix II. It does not grow in Turkey.
Harvest season
White-pink flower clusters in early summer; the root is lifted in autumn from 3–4-year-old plants.
Local names
Yılankökü, Hint yılankökü; Sanskrit sarpagandha, Hindi chandrika.

Mix-up risk

The African species of the same genus (Rauvolfia vomitoria) is also a reserpine source; the Turkish name "yılankökü" (snakeroot) is used for other plants too (such as Polygala senega). None is a plant to use on one’s own.

Preparation

This record gives no dose for use: the doses in the WHO monograph (powdered drug 200 mg daily, maintenance 50–300 mg; adjusted by reserpine alkaloid content in small increments at intervals of at least ten days) are those of a drug used under medical supervision. Snakeroot is not a plant to use on one’s own and should not be bought as a herbalist’s or supplement product.

What science says

Snakeroot is the clearest example in this catalog of the passage from plant to drug: the reserpine in its root is a licensed antihypertensive. The 2016 Cochrane review examined reserpine’s effect in primary hypertension: in four randomised trials with 237 participants reserpine lowered systolic blood pressure significantly against placebo, and the authors judged the effect "roughly to the same degree as other first-line antihypertensive drugs"; but with so few trials the dose-response pattern and dose-related safety profile could not be established. The WHO monograph likewise counts hypertension as the sole "use supported by clinical data". We record the evidence level as Clinical — but that evidence belongs not to a tea of the plant but to the alkaloid isolated from its root and dosed. The same conclusion does not hold for the plant itself: alkaloid content varies from root to root and cannot be dosed.

Safety

Generally safe

It can cause depression — the WHO monograph’s gravest warning: drug-induced depression may persist for months after withdrawal and be severe enough to result in suicide; it must be stopped at the first sign of depression (despondency, early-morning insomnia, loss of appetite, impotence, self-deprecation). Contraindicated with a history of depression, during MAO-inhibitor therapy, in active ulcer, sinus-node disorders, ulcerative colitis, epilepsy and renal insufficiency. It impairs alertness; driving and operating machinery are inadvisable. Bradycardia and arrhythmia (especially with digoxin or quinidine), nasal congestion, oedema and increased gastric secretion have been reported. Not in pregnancy or lactation. Safety level Low: this is not a plant but a drug in the shape of a plant.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Digoxin and quinidineThe WHO monograph states the risk of bradycardia and arrhythmia rises with concurrent use.
MAO inhibitorsThe monograph’s contraindication: not during or shortly after MAO-inhibitor therapy.
Alcohol and CNS depressants, antihypertensives, diureticsThe monograph states these may potentiate snakeroot: sedation deepens, blood pressure falls too far.
Levodopa and tricyclic antidepressantsBecause reserpine depletes monoamines in the brain it works against antidepressants and Parkinson’s drugs; the monograph lists them among interactions.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Indian snakeroot.
