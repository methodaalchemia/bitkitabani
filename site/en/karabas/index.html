French lavender

Lavandula stoechas
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

The purple “ears” on top of the spike are not flowers but coloured leaf-like bracts that attract pollinators.

Other names
Spanish lavender, topped lavender, ustukhuddus, karabaş
Part used
Flowering spikes
Region
Anatolia and the Mediterranean
Use
Healing, Incense and protection
In short

What is French lavender?

French lavender (Lavandula stoechas) is a plant of the Lamiaceae family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean. Part used: flowering spikes.

What is French lavender used for?

Traditional use: Known as karabaş on the Aegean and Mediterranean coasts; the tea is drunk for headache, nerves and cough, and the dried flowers are put in chests against moths. Scientific evidence: traditional. Calming, muscle-relaxing and antimicrobial effects have been shown in lab and animal studies.

Is French lavender safe?

Safety label: caution. Moderate amounts of tea are generally considered safe. The essential oil is rich in camphor; it must not be swallowed or applied to the faces of babies and young children, as camphor can cause seizures. Avoid in pregnancy.

Traditional use

Anatolia

Known as karabaş on the Aegean and Mediterranean coasts; the tea is drunk for headache, nerves and cough, and the dried flowers are put in chests against moths.

Arab and Persian medicine

The “ustukhuddus” of medieval Arab and Persian physicians, called “the broom of the brain” in old books and still used in Unani medicine in India.

In nature

Where it grows
Turkish pine forests, maquis and rocky slopes of the Aegean and Mediterranean; prefers lime-free soil.
Harvest season
Flowers March–May; spikes are cut before fully open.
Local names
karabaş, karabaş otu, keşiş otu

Mix-up risk

True lavender (L. angustifolia) has long-stalked spikes without ears; French lavender has conspicuous purple ears on top.

Preparation

A teaspoon of dried flowers is steeped in a cup of boiling water for 5–10 minutes; no more than one or two cups a day.

What science says

Calming, muscle-relaxing and antimicrobial effects have been shown in lab and animal studies. Human studies are few; one meta-analysis reports a possible benefit of aromatherapy for labour pain, but the studies are small. Unlike true lavender (L. angustifolia), its essential oil is rich in camphor and fenchone.

Safety

Caution

Moderate amounts of tea are generally considered safe. The essential oil is rich in camphor; it must not be swallowed or applied to the faces of babies and young children, as camphor can cause seizures. Avoid in pregnancy.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Sleeping pills and sedativesCalming effects can add up.
Seizure medicinesThe camphor-rich oil may lower the seizure threshold.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying French lavender.
