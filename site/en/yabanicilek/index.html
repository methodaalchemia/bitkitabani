Wild strawberry

Fragaria vesca L.; F. moschata Weston; F. viridis Weston; F. × ananassa (Weston) Duchesne ex Rozier
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

The monograph covers four Fragaria species together, and one of them is the garden strawberry (F. × ananassa) — the strawberry sold in shops. But the approved drug is not the fruit; it is the dried leaf. That produces a rare situation in this catalog: the plant behind one of the world’s most-eaten fruits appears on an official list, and what the list names is not that fruit but the leaf pruned off and thrown away each year. The garden strawberry itself is not a wild species at all: it arose in 18th-century Europe as a hybrid of two American strawberries.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the names "yabani çilek, dağ çileği, yer çileği, misk çileği, çilek" and lists no synonyms. In English: wild strawberry, mountain strawberry, wood strawberry, alpine strawberry. The monograph covers four species together — including the garden strawberry (F. × ananassa). What is Wild strawberry?

Wild strawberry (Fragaria vesca L.; F. moschata Weston; F. viridis Weston; F. × ananassa (Weston) Duchesne ex Rozier) is a plant of the Rosaceae (rose family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe, Asia and Siberia. Part used: the dried leaves.

What is Wild strawberry used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph places both indications under "traditional use": (1) relief of symptoms associated with minor urinary tract complaints, in addition to the general recommendation of sufficient fluid intake to increase the amount of urine; (2) symptomatic treatment of mild diarrhoea. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no clinical trial of wild strawberry leaf in humans; the EU monograph rests both indications on long use and states plainly in its interaction section that "no interaction studies have been performed" — which is a more honest formulation than "none reported", because it names the absence of knowledge directly.

Is Wild strawberry safe?

Safety label: caution. Not for use in serious cardiac or renal disease where reduced fluid intake is advised — because the urinary indication requires drinking plenty of fluid. In urinary use, if fever, painful urination, spasms or blood in the urine appear; in diarrhoea use, if diarrhoea recurs or stools are bloody, see a doctor — these are not situations to be managed with a herbal tea. Anyone hypersensitive to the rose family, and to strawberry in particular, should not use it; strawberry allergy is common. The Turkish monograph also asks that use be stopped two weeks before surgery. Not to be used longer than two weeks for the urinary indication or three days for diarrhoea. Not recommended under 12, in pregnancy or in lactation.

Traditional use

Europe — two indications

The EMA monograph places both indications under "traditional use": (1) relief of symptoms associated with minor urinary tract complaints, in addition to the general recommendation of sufficient fluid intake to increase the amount of urine; (2) symptomatic treatment of mild diarrhoea. Dose, duration and warnings are given separately for each.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the dried leaves" and writes out the red lines for each indication. For urinary use: if fever, dysuria (painful urination), spasms or blood in the urine appear, see a doctor — these are not minor complaints but may signal infection or something else. For diarrhoea: if diarrhoea recurs or stools are bloody, see a doctor, and use is limited to three days. Not for use in serious cardiac or renal disease where reduced fluid intake is advised.

Anatolia

Wild strawberry grows in forest clearings and along roadsides in Turkey; its fruit is much smaller and more fragrant than the garden strawberry’s. What the monograph is interested in, though, is the leaf — the part discarded every year in a strawberry field or garden.

In nature

Where it grows
The temperate northern hemisphere; common in forest clearings and roadsides in Turkey. The garden strawberry is cultivated everywhere.
Harvest season
The leaves are gathered and dried while the plant is in flower or fruit; the fruit ripens in late spring and early summer.
Local names
Yabani çilek, dağ çileği, yer çileği, misk çileği, çilek — all five appear in the Turkish monograph.

Mix-up risk

Confusion among the four species the monograph covers is not a problem, since all four are approved. The real confusion is of part: the approved drug is the leaf while the part eaten is the fruit. Some products sold as "strawberry tea" are fruit-flavoured black tea or pieces of fruit, not the monograph’s drug.

Preparation

The EU monograph gives separate doses for the two indications. For the urinary tract: 5–10 g of the comminuted drug as a decoction in 200 ml of water, 2–3 times daily. For diarrhoea: 4–5 g of the comminuted drug in 150–200 ml of boiling water as an infusion or decoction, 2–3 times daily for adults, half the prepared tea for adolescents. In the urinary indication, drinking plenty of fluid is part of the treatment — the monograph builds this into the wording of the indication itself, "in addition to the general recommendation of a sufficient fluid intake". Two weeks is the limit for urinary use and three days for diarrhoea. Not recommended under 12.

What science says

There is no clinical trial of wild strawberry leaf in humans; the EU monograph rests both indications on long use and states plainly in its interaction section that "no interaction studies have been performed" — which is a more honest formulation than "none reported", because it names the absence of knowledge directly. The mechanism of the urinary indication is already implied in the monograph: the effect comes from flushing the urinary tract with an increased volume of urine, and that requires drinking plenty of fluid — so part of the effect belongs to the water itself. For the diarrhoea indication there is the classic mechanism of the rose family: the tannins in the leaf act as an astringent. Neither substitutes for a clinical trial. The evidence level is Traditional.

Safety

Caution

Not for use in serious cardiac or renal disease where reduced fluid intake is advised — because the urinary indication requires drinking plenty of fluid. In urinary use, if fever, painful urination, spasms or blood in the urine appear; in diarrhoea use, if diarrhoea recurs or stools are bloody, see a doctor — these are not situations to be managed with a herbal tea. Anyone hypersensitive to the rose family, and to strawberry in particular, should not use it; strawberry allergy is common. The Turkish monograph also asks that use be stopped two weeks before surgery. Not to be used longer than two weeks for the urinary indication or three days for diarrhoea. Not recommended under 12, in pregnancy or in lactation.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
P-glycoprotein substrates and anticoagulant/antiplatelet drugsThe Turkish monograph states it may interact with these two groups; the source it gives for that line is a consumer medicines database, not a clinical study. It is reproduced here as it stands because it is on the official list, and anyone on a blood thinner would do well to mention it to their doctor.
The EMA monograph: "no interaction studies have been performed"The European monograph’s wording is notable: not "none reported" but no studies performed. The two are not the same — the first describes a gap in observation, the second a gap in research. Neither amounts to reassurance.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Wild strawberry.
