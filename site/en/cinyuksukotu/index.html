BT-311 · Orobanchaceae

Chinese foxglove

Rehmannia glutinosa Libosch.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

Its name and its kinship do not agree: it is called "Chinese foxglove", and the old classification put it in the foxglove family (Scrophulariaceae) — the WHO monograph still does — but modern classification moved it to the broomrape family (Orobanchaceae), and it carries no cardiac glycosides. In Chinese medicine, meanwhile, three states of the same root are three separate drugs: raw, dried and wine-steamed. The clearest example in this catalog of how the way a plant is prepared can make it a different medicine.

Other names
No established Turkish name; "Çin yüksükotu" renders the English Chinese foxglove — its flower resembles foxglove, but it is not a foxglove and carries no cardiac glycosides. Chinese dì huáng ("earth yellow"). Synonyms Digitalis glutinosa Gaertn., Rehmannia chinensis Libosch. No Turkish or EU monograph; the record rests on the WHO monograph (Vol. 3, 2007) and PubMed.

In short

What is Chinese foxglove?

Chinese foxglove (Rehmannia glutinosa Libosch.) is a plant of the Orobanchaceae family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia. Part used: the dried roots and rhizomes.

What is Chinese foxglove used for?

Traditional use: The World Health Organization monograph (Vol. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no clinical trial of Chinese foxglove on its own; all the human data come from multi-herb formulas in which it is the chief ingredient.

Is Chinese foxglove safe?

Safety label: caution. Contraindicated in chronic liver and gastrointestinal disease and in diarrhoea — the WHO monograph, from the Chinese pharmacopoeia. Contraindicated in pregnancy: it has been shown to inhibit embryo implantation in mice (a possible anti-implantation effect). Diarrhoea, abdominal pain, oedema, fatigue, vertigo and palpitations have been reported; the monograph writes that these were transient and disappeared within days. Not recommended in lactation or in children without medical supervision. The "foxglove" in its name should not mislead: it carries no cardiac glycosides.

Traditional use

WHO monograph — the root

The World Health Organization monograph (Vol. 3, 2007): no use supported by clinical data — case reports in rheumatoid arthritis and hypertension, controlled trials lacking. Uses described in pharmacopoeias: fevers, diabetes, hypertension, skin eruptions, sore throat, hypermenorrhoea and polymenorrhoea; a tonic to stimulate the immune system. In traditional medicine: antispasmodic, diuretic; burns, diarrhoea, dysentery. Daily dose 9–15 g of root.

Turkey

There is no monograph for this plant on the Turkish list; it does not grow in Turkey and is rare on the Turkish supplement market. It enters this catalog because of the WHO monograph and its central place in Chinese medicine.

China — dì huáng and Six-Ingredient Rehmannia

One of the most-used roots of Chinese medicine; its raw (shēng), dried and wine-steamed (shú) forms count as separate drugs. It is the chief ingredient of the world-famous "Liu Wei Di Huang Wan" (Six-Ingredient Rehmannia Pill) — and most of the evidence debate concerns that formula, not the plant.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to China; cultivated in China, Japan and Korea. It does not grow in Turkey.
Harvest season
Purple-brown thimble-shaped flowers in spring; the roots are lifted in autumn and either dried or steamed with wine.
Local names
No Turkish name; Chinese dì huáng, shēng dì (raw), shú dì (prepared).

Mix-up risk

⚠️ Its name and flower run together with foxglove (Digitalis); foxglove carries lethal cardiac glycosides, this plant does not. In the Chinese pharmacopoeia the raw and prepared roots are separate drugs used for separate purposes.

Preparation

The WHO monograph gives a daily dose of 9–15 g of dried roots and rhizomes as infusion or decoction. Contraindicated in chronic liver or gastrointestinal disease, in diarrhoea and in pregnancy. Not recommended in lactation or in children without medical supervision.

What science says

There is no clinical trial of Chinese foxglove on its own; all the human data come from multi-herb formulas in which it is the chief ingredient. A multicentre randomised trial published in 2023 in the Clinical Journal of the American Society of Nephrology (148 patients, 48 weeks) found that Rehmannia-6-based granules added to standard care in type 2 diabetes with kidney disease slowed the decline in eGFR by 2.7 ml/min a year — but there was no significant difference in albuminuria, and the trial was assessor-blind without placebo. A meta-analysis published in 2025 in the Saudi Medical Journal (17 RCTs, 1,139 patients) examines formulas with Rehmannia as the "monarch drug" for perimenopausal symptoms in breast-cancer patients and stresses methodological flaws. Both measure the formula; Rehmannia’s share is not separated. Evidence level Traditional.

Caution

Contraindicated in chronic liver and gastrointestinal disease and in diarrhoea — the WHO monograph, from the Chinese pharmacopoeia. Diarrhoea, abdominal pain, oedema, fatigue, vertigo and palpitations have been reported; the monograph writes that these were transient and disappeared within days. Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
WHO: no interaction dataThe monograph states no information is available on drug interactions. As a plant credited with effects on blood glucose, stacking on antidiabetics is a theoretical note; in the diabetes-kidney trial patients continued their medication and no serious interaction was reported.
Not attributing formula data to the plantRehmannia-6 and similar formulas contain five to seven plants; their results cannot count as evidence for this root.
