BT-322 · Myrtaceae (myrtle family)

Narrow-leaved peppermint

Eucalyptus radiata Sieber ex DC.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

Despite the English name "peppermint" it has no relation to mint; the name comes from the scent of the crushed leaf. Several Australian eucalyptus species carry that name, and the name alone does not tell you which one is meant.

Other names
English narrow-leaved peppermint, grey peppermint. It has no established Turkish name; "dar yapraklı okaliptüs" is a descriptive name for the shape of its leaf. In the aromatherapy trade its oil is sold under the name "Eucalyptus radiata" as a milder-smelling alternative to eucalyptus oil. Neither the European Medicines Agency nor the Turkish agency has a monograph for this species.
Part used
Leaf; the essential oil distilled from the leaf
Region
Oceania

In short

What is Narrow-leaved peppermint?

Narrow-leaved peppermint (Eucalyptus radiata Sieber ex DC.) is a plant of the Myrtaceae (myrtle family) family. Traditions that use it: Oceania. Part used: leaf; the essential oil distilled from the leaf.

What is Narrow-leaved peppermint used for?

Traditional use: A study published in Veterinary Sciences in 2023 tested eucalyptus essential oils in vitro against bacterial isolates from equine wounds, alongside chlorhexidine gluconate and povidone-iodine. Scientific evidence: traditional. In the sources available to us there is no study in humans at all for this species.

Is Narrow-leaved peppermint safe?

Safety label: caution. What is at issue is a concentrated essential oil, and there is no safety assessment made for this species. The restrictions written for eucalyptus oil do not belong to this species: the European Medicines Agency’s monograph on eucalyptus oil covers *Eucalyptus globulus*, *E. polybractea* and *E. smithii*, and the leaf monograph only *E. globulus*; neither includes this species. Even so the rule those monographs set for cineole-bearing eucalyptus oils is worth carrying over exactly as written, because it is not a caution but a contraindication. The European Medicines Agency monograph (EMA/HMPC/320292/2023, section 4.3) contraindicates: hypersensitivity to eucalyptus oil or 1,8-cineol; children with a history of seizures (febrile or not); and children under 24 months of age, because 1,8-cineole containing preparations, like other essential oils, can induce laryngospasm. The Turkish agency’s *Eucalyptus globulus* monograph goes further still: it states that the essential oil must absolutely not be used in infants under 30 months of age, as it may cause laryngospasm; the same monograph also states that in external use it should not be applied to the faces of infants and children, and particularly not around the nose. Both documents are written for *E. globulus* (the EMA one also for *E. polybractea* and *E. smithii*) and do not cover this species. But this species’ oil is sold in the aromatherapy trade as a 1,8-cineole-bearing oil and its composition is not measured in the sources relied on here; knowing which species the source was written for does not lessen the weight of the warning. So the strictest rule should be observed here too: the oil is not to be used in infants and young children, and not at all in children with a history of seizures. The sources relied on here contain no separate assessment for pregnancy, breastfeeding or drug interactions.

Traditional use

Among the eucalyptus oils — in vitro comparisons

A study published in Veterinary Sciences in 2023 tested eucalyptus essential oils in vitro against bacterial isolates from equine wounds, alongside chlorhexidine gluconate and povidone-iodine. The study is not a treatment applied to a wound but a susceptibility test measured in the laboratory.

On inflammatory markers — cell culture

A study published in Plants in 2020 assayed twenty essential oils from eighteen species for the release of TNF-α and CCL2 by THP-1 cells stimulated with a bacterial component; this species’ oil is among those tested, but the study reports no effect for this species. The two oils reported to reduce marker release were those of *Thymbra capitata* and *Lavandula luisieri*. This is a cell culture measurement, and it produced no positive result for this species.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew’s records its natural range covers only three regions: New South Wales, Tasmania and Victoria. It is a tree of the moist forest belt, native to south-eastern Australia.
Harvest season
The sources relied on here give no collection calendar for Turkey; the plant does not grow there naturally.
Local names
It has no established Turkish local name.

Mix-up risk

It is in the same genus as eucalyptus (*Eucalyptus globulus*, *E. camaldulensis*) but a separate species; one species’ monograph and restrictions do not belong to its relative. It should not be confused with lemon-scented gum either: that species is placed today in the genus *Corymbia* and the scent of its oil comes from citronellal.

Preparation

No dose is accepted in an official monograph. The sources relied on here give no measure, dilution or duration for use in humans. The essential oil is not taken internally.

What science says

In the sources available to us there is no study in humans at all for this species. Its literature is thin: some of the citations returned under the species name are botanical papers describing fungi that live on the tree. Both measurements we have are laboratory measurements, and they do not carry the same weight: in the in vitro susceptibility test (Veterinary Sciences, 2023) this species’ oil inhibited *Escherichia coli* strains better than the antiseptics did, while against *Staphylococcus* strains it trailed *E. globulus* oil; in the cell culture study (Plants, 2020) the species is among those tested but no effect is reported for it. The aromatherapy trade says the oil carries 1,8-cineole much as eucalyptus oil does; the sources relied on here contain no study measuring the composition of this species’ oil, and this record does not confirm that claim.
