Star anise

Illicium verum
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

Production of the flu drug oseltamivir relied for years on shikimic acid from star anise; during the 2005 bird flu scare demand rose so much that the spice’s price climbed sharply.

Other names
Chinese star anise, badyan, ba jiao
Part used
Fruit
Region
Asia and Siberia
Use
Food, Healing, Poisonous
In short

What is Star anise?

Star anise (Illicium verum) is a plant of the Schisandraceae family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia. Part used: fruit.

What is Star anise used for?

Traditional use: The base of Chinese five-spice powder and the scent of Vietnamese pho. Scientific evidence: traditional. Star anise is an important source of shikimic acid, the starting material for the flu drug oseltamivir; the spice itself has not been shown to affect flu.

Is Star anise safe?

Safety label: caution. Chinese star anise is safe in food. But poisonous Japanese star anise (Illicium anisatum) looks almost identical and has been mixed into the trade; poisonings with seizures and vomiting have been reported in babies given star anise tea for colic. Do not give star anise tea to babies or young children.

Traditional use

China and Vietnam

The base of Chinese five-spice powder and the scent of Vietnamese pho. Chinese medicine uses it for colds and colic.

Ottoman and modern Türkiye

Known in Ottoman times as badyan and added to sherbets and electuaries; today it goes into winter teas.

In nature

Where it grows
An evergreen tree of the humid mountain forests of southern China and northern Vietnam.
Harvest season
Fruits are picked green, before they ripen, and sun-dried.
Local names
yıldız anason, badyan

Mix-up risk

Japanese star anise (I. anisatum) has smaller, more irregular stars and smells more like incense or camphor than anise; telling them apart is a job for experts.

Preparation

One or two stars are added to tea or food and cooked with it.

What science says

Star anise is an important source of shikimic acid, the starting material for the flu drug oseltamivir; the spice itself has not been shown to affect flu. Its scent comes from the same molecule as anise, anethole. Antimicrobial effects have been shown in the lab.

Safety

Caution

Chinese star anise is safe in food. But poisonous Japanese star anise (Illicium anisatum) looks almost identical and has been mixed into the trade; poisonings with seizures and vomiting have been reported in babies given star anise tea for colic. Do not give star anise tea to babies or young children.
