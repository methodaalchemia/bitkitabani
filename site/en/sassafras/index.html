In short

What is Sassafras?

Sassafras (Sassafras albidum) is a plant of the Lauraceae family. Traditions that use it: North America. Part used: root bark, leaf.

What is Sassafras used for?

Traditional use: Indigenous peoples drank root-bark tea for fever and rheumatism and put the leaves on wounds. Scientific evidence: traditional. Most of the root-bark oil is safrole.

Is Sassafras safe?

Safety label: high risk. Do not drink root-bark tea or oil: safrole damages the liver and is carcinogenic in animals. Even small amounts of the oil can cause serious poisoning. Filé powder contains very little safrole and is used in small amounts in food.

Traditional use

North America

Indigenous peoples drank root-bark tea for fever and rheumatism and put the leaves on wounds. In the 17th century it was sold to Europe as a cure-all and became one of the first exports of the Virginia colony.

American cooking

The root once flavoured root beer. In Cajun and Creole cooking in Louisiana, powdered dried leaves (filé) thicken gumbo.

In nature

Where it grows
Woodland edges, old fields and thickets of eastern North America.
Harvest season
Leaves are picked in summer; root bark was dug in spring and autumn.
Local names
sassafras

Mix-up risk

“Sassafras oil” sold in Brazil comes from a different tree (Ocotea) and is also rich in safrole.

Preparation

Internal use of the root bark is not recommended today. Filé powder is made from the leaves, which contain very little safrole.

What science says

Most of the root-bark oil is safrole. Because safrole causes liver cancer in animals, the US FDA banned it from food in 1960; root beer is now made with artificial flavouring or safrole-free extract. Safrole can also be used to make illegal drugs, so it is controlled in many countries.

Safety

High risk

Do not drink root-bark tea or oil: safrole damages the liver and is carcinogenic in animals. Even small amounts of the oil can cause serious poisoning. Filé powder contains very little safrole and is used in small amounts in food.
