Comfrey

Symphytum officinale L.
Evidence: LimitedHigh risk

The Turkish folk name "bone herb" comes from a belief that the plant knits fractures, and the Latin name says the same thing: Symphytum, from the Greek symphyo, "to grow together". The same claim in two languages. The indication the monograph actually approves is far narrower: minor sprains and bruises, on the skin, for ten days.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the Turkish names "kafesotu, karakafesotu". It is also called kemik otu, "bone herb", in Turkish folk usage — the name itself carries the claim.

In short

What is Comfrey?

Comfrey (Symphytum officinale L.) is a plant of the Boraginaceae (borage family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the root.

What is Comfrey used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph gives a single indication under "traditional use": symptomatic relief of minor sprains and bruises. Scientific evidence: limited. The efficacy side is thin.

Is Comfrey safe?

Safety label: high risk. The root is not taken by mouth. The Turkish monograph’s reasoning is explicit: pyrrolizidine alkaloids are hepatotoxic, and repeated low doses induce tumours. The alkaloid content of a product must be stated and daily exposure must stay below 1 microgram. This is not an abstract warning: a case report in the Journal of Gastroenterology and Hepatology in 1990 describes a 23-year-old man who had been taking comfrey leaves, presented with hepatic veno-occlusive disease and severe portal hypertension, and died of liver failure; occlusion of the small hepatic veins and widespread haemorrhagic necrosis were found. The authors note two other documented cases linked to comfrey. Even topical use has limits: not on broken or irritated skin (damaged skin increases absorption), away from eyes and mucous membranes, no longer than 10 days, not under 18. Studies with isolated pyrrolizidine alkaloids in animals have shown reproductive toxicity, so it is not used in pregnancy or lactation. The Turkish monograph requires supervision by a doctor or pharmacist trained in herbal treatment.

Traditional use

Europe — topical only

The EMA monograph gives a single indication under "traditional use": symptomatic relief of minor sprains and bruises. The form matters: the monograph covers semi-solid preparations only, that is, products applied to the skin. Oral use is not in the monograph — and as the safety section explains, that is a deliberate limit rather than an omission.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the roots" and sets two numerical limits: the semi-solid form is not to be used for more than 10 days, and the pyrrolizidine alkaloid content of the product must be stated, with daily exposure kept below 1 microgram.

Anatolia — folk use and the name

In Anatolia a poultice is made for fractures, dislocations and sprains; the name "kemik otu" comes from that. This traditional use is topical and runs in the same direction as the monograph indication. Using it as a tea, by contrast, is dangerous for the reason set out below.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to Europe and western Asia; grows in damp meadows, along streams and in ditches. Found in northern and western Turkey.
Harvest season
Purple-pink flowers from April to July; the root is lifted in autumn or early spring.
Local names
Karakafesotu, kafesotu, kemik otu, uyuzotu.

Mix-up risk

It can be confused with other members of the Boraginaceae, which matters because many of them also carry pyrrolizidine alkaloids. The riskier confusion is assuming the young leaves are edible: the leaf contains the alkaloids too, and leaves were what the patient in the fatal case above had been taking.

Preparation

For adults and the elderly the monograph describes a thin layer of a semi-solid form containing 10% liquid extract, applied twice daily. Not to be used for more than 10 days. Not recommended under 18. Not to be applied to broken or irritated skin, and contact with the eyes and mucous membranes must be avoided. There is no form to be taken by mouth.

What science says

The efficacy side is thin. The 2014 Cochrane review of low back pain examines a comfrey root extract ointment in a single trial (120 participants) and finds it better than placebo ointment for short-term pain on a visual analogue scale, rating the evidence "low quality". The EMA likewise rests the indication on long use rather than clinical evidence. The real subject of this record is not efficacy: the safety section explains why the plant is confined to topical use, and that is where the important information is.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No drug interactions reported in either monographBoth the EMA and the Turkish monograph leave the interaction section empty. That is expected: the approved use is topical and short, so there is little scope for systemic interaction. The real risk is not interaction but oral use.
Other substances that burden the liverNo interaction study exists, but because pyrrolizidine alkaloids target the liver, taking this plant by mouth while using liver-affecting medicines or alcohol adds to that burden. It should not be taken by mouth in any case.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Comfrey.
