In short

What is Ash?

Ash (Fraxinus excelsior) is a plant of the Oleaceae (zeytingiller) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: leaf.

What is Ash used for?

Traditional use: In the Völuspá of the Poetic Edda, the world tree Yggdrasil is an ash: it stands ever green by Urd’s well, and the dew that falls in the dales comes from it. Scientific evidence: traditional. According to the EMA assessment report, all sources agree that efficacy for the claimed uses has not been documented; use rests on long tradition alone.

Is Ash safe?

Safety label: generally safe. Not for conditions where fluid intake must be restricted, such as severe heart or kidney disease. Safety under 18 has not been established. See a doctor if a urinary complaint comes with fever, blood in the urine or cramps, or does not go away.

Traditional use

Norse mythology

In the Völuspá of the Poetic Edda, the world tree Yggdrasil is an ash: it stands ever green by Urd’s well, and the dew that falls in the dales comes from it. A hart bites its top, its trunk rots and the dragon Nidhogg gnaws its roots. Odin hangs nine nights on “the windy tree” to win the runes; that tree is Yggdrasil, whose name means “Odin’s horse”.

England

Gilbert White wrote in 1789 that in Selborne children with hernias were passed naked through the split trunk of a young ash, in the belief that as the tree healed so would the child. Twigs of an ash in which a live shrew had been plugged were believed to cure lame cattle.

Rome

Pliny writes that ash-leaf juice cures snakebite and that no snake will even lie in an ash’s shadow.

European folk medicine

The leaf and bark have been known as a diuretic and rheumatism remedy since Hippocrates. The EMA recognises the leaf as a traditional remedy for minor joint pain and for increasing urine in minor urinary complaints.

In nature

Where it grows
Three ashes are native to Turkey: narrow-leaved ash (F. angustifolia), common ash (F. excelsior) and manna ash (F. ornus). Common ash grows in Thrace, Kocaeli and the Black Sea region, in damp valleys and along streams in beech–hornbeam and fir–spruce forests (Atay 1984).
Harvest season
Leaves are gathered in early summer; flowers open in spring, before the leaves.
Local names
Adi dişbudak

Mix-up risk

The EMA monograph covers leaves of common and narrow-leaved ash; rowan leaves also look like ash leaves.

Preparation

According to the EMA monograph, the dried leaf is made into a tea; for urinary complaints it is taken with plenty of fluid.

What science says

According to the EMA assessment report, all sources agree that efficacy for the claimed uses has not been documented; use rests on long tradition alone.

Safety

Generally safe

Not for conditions where fluid intake must be restricted, such as severe heart or kidney disease. Safety under 18 has not been established. See a doctor if a urinary complaint comes with fever, blood in the urine or cramps, or does not go away.
