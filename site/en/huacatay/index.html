Huacatay

Tagetes minuta L.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

Huacatay is a herb of Andean cooking and also an alien plant of Türkiye: it appears in the Turkish alien flora checklist as "neophyte, naturalised, herb, of American origin", and Kew likewise marks Türkiye as "introduced" for this species. The plant that goes into soup in the Andes stands by the roadside here as a weed.

Other names
It has no established Turkish name; huacatay, chinchilla and suico in the Andean countries, southern marigold in English. Neither the European Medicines Agency nor Türkiye’s TİTCK has a monograph for this species — but that does not mean it appears in no institutional document: the species is regulated under EU cosmetics law and restricted on grounds of phototoxicity. In Türkiye it is recorded as a naturalised alien plant.

In short

What is Huacatay?

Huacatay (Tagetes minuta L.) is a plant of the Asteraceae (daisy family) family. Traditions that use it: Andes. Part used: leafy shoots.

What is Huacatay used for?

Traditional use: A 2021 study in the Journal of Ethnopharmacology introduces the species this way: "*Tagetes minuta* ‘Huacatay’ is an endemic species of South America that has been used in traditional medicine since ancient times as a remedy for stomach and intestinal discomfort". Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no study at all showing efficacy in humans.

Is Huacatay safe?

Safety label: caution. The real risk is phototoxicity: applying it to the skin and going out in the sun. The 2015 opinion of the EU Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety (SCCS) states: "*Tagetes* species belong to the family of Compositae. Several members of this plant family are known to be phototoxic. The component of *Tagetes* for which phototoxicity in humans has clearly been demonstrated is alpha-terthienyl, also called terthiophene." The same opinion records two important limits: "the available literature does not provide a dose-response relationship for the phototoxicity of TTP", and "therefore, no firm conclusion regarding a safe level of *Tagetes* can be drawn from the human studies". Species-specific human data exists but it is thin and not clean. In an older study, an occlusive patch trial in 25 volunteers produced no reactions at 0.01% or 0.05%, while 2 of 25 reacted at 0.25% and the authors judged the material phototoxic at that concentration; SCCS notes that it could not obtain the original report and that the terthiophene content is unknown. In a more recent trial completed by 27 people, SCCS’s own recount finds reactions at the regulated concentration too (2 of 27 at 0.01%; 4 at 0.05%). SCCS still treats 0.01% as safe, but frames its reasoning as "inconclusive, albeit mild, reactions in humans". The fragrance industry standard gives a no-effect level in humans of 0.05% and reports phototoxicity in 8 of 10 guinea pigs at 0.01%; SCCS says that guinea pig study cannot be relied upon, because many animals also reacted to the non-irradiated material, and recounts the figure as 6/10 to 7/10. EU cosmetics law restricts this species: at most 0.01% in leave-on products and 0.1% in rinse-off products, with alpha-terthienyl not exceeding 0.35%, and it cannot be used at all in sunscreen products or products marketed for UV exposure. Its sister species *Tagetes erecta* went further: its flower extract and flower oil were placed on the list of substances prohibited in cosmetics by the 2018 regulation — prohibited, not merely restricted. No assessment for pregnancy, breastfeeding or children could be found. Adding the fresh leaf to food in the kitchen does not fall under these warnings, which are for extract and essential oil concentrations. Safety level "moderate".

Traditional use

Andean cooking — huacatay

A 2021 study in the Journal of Ethnopharmacology introduces the species this way: "*Tagetes minuta* ‘Huacatay’ is an endemic species of South America that has been used in traditional medicine since ancient times as a remedy for stomach and intestinal discomfort". In Andean cooking the fresh leaves go into sauces and soups.

South America — an infusion for digestion

A 2018 *Tagetes* review in Molecules records that in Argentina, Bolivia, Brazil, Paraguay and Peru infusions and decoctions of *T. minuta* are considered digestives, appetizers, cholagogues, carminatives, gastric sedatives, antidiarrheics and vermifuges.

In nature

Where it grows
Kew records a range of 110 regions, with a native range given as "Brazil to S. South America". It occurs in Türkiye, though not natively: the Turkish alien flora checklist records it as a naturalised neophyte and Kew marks Türkiye as "introduced". It is a widespread weed worldwide.
Harvest season
Leafy and flowering shoots are gathered in summer; where naturalised in Türkiye it is found in late summer.
Local names
No established name in Turkish. Local names: huacatay, chinchilla, suico.

Mix-up risk

It should not be confused with other species of the same genus: *Tagetes erecta* (African marigold) and *Tagetes patula* are separate species species. *T. patula* is subject to the same limits as *T. minuta* under cosmetics law, and when the two are used together the combined *Tagetes* content may not exceed that same ceiling. The one that differs is *T. erecta*: its flower extract and flower oil were added to the list of substances prohibited in cosmetics by the 2018 regulation — prohibited, not restricted. *T. erecta* is also naturalised in Türkiye, so the two may be found together.

Preparation

No official medicinal monograph sets an accepted dose. In the kitchen the fresh leaves are used. The essential oil is not applied to the skin undiluted, and not at all if there will be sun exposure — the reason is the phototoxicity section below. The limits set by EU cosmetics law are for cosmetic products and are not a recommendation for use: at most 0.01% in leave-on products, at most 0.1% in rinse-off products, and only provided the alpha-terthienyl content of the extract does not exceed 0.35%. The same law prohibits its use in sunscreen products and in products marketed for exposure to UV light.

What science says

There is no study at all showing efficacy in humans. A 2017 systematic review examined the species’ antimicrobial activity and wrote that the findings "should be repeatedly proven in future animal and clinical studies". In a 2021 study the anti-inflammatory effect was shown in vitro (in cell lines, IC50 59.72–66.42 µg/mL); it has not been shown in humans. Findings against insects and ticks sit at the animal and veterinary field level. The essential oil composition is extremely variable, and that is the central obstacle to any study of this species: at least two major chemotypes have been described, one rich in terpenes such as (E)-β-ocimene and limonene, and one rich in tagetone derivatives (the sum of dihydrotagetone and (E)- and (Z)-tagetones). The species also carries thiophenes; a new thiophene compound was characterised in 2014 from material growing in Saudi Arabia, and in 2022 alpha-terthienylmethanol isolated from its leaves was reported to inhibit angiogenesis in vitro. Thiophenes are at the centre of this record’s safety side. Evidence level "traditional".
