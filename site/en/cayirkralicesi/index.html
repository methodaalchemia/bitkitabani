Meadowsweet

Filipendula ulmaria (L.) Maxim.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

The plant’s old genus name is Spiraea, and the Turkish monograph gives "Spirea ulmaria L." as a synonym. According to the Science History Institute, the name "aspirin" comes — as an unsettled account — from the combination of *acetyl*, the Latin *Spiraea*, the genus meadowsweet belongs to, and the "-in" ending used in drug names. So one of the most used painkillers in the world carries the discarded genus name of this meadow plant. That the monograph’s warnings resemble aspirin’s so closely is no coincidence either: both come from salicylate chemistry.

Other names
The Turkish monograph gives the names "çayır kraliçesi, ergeç sakalı, keçi sakalı, teke sakalı"; the synonyms are Spiraea ulmaria L. and Filipendula denudata. The old genus name Spiraea explains the plant’s place in the history of medicine.

In short

What is Meadowsweet?

Meadowsweet (Filipendula ulmaria (L.) Maxim.) is a plant of the Rosaceae (gülgiller) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the flower and aerial parts.

What is Meadowsweet used for?

Traditional use: The EMA has two separate monographs (flower and aerial parts) and both give the same two indications under "traditional use": (1) supportive treatment of the common cold; (2) relief of minor articular pain. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no randomised clinical trial of meadowsweet in humans.

Is Meadowsweet safe?

Safety label: caution. Not for anyone sensitive to salicylate compounds — this contraindication appears in both the EU and the Turkish monograph and follows from the salicylate content; someone allergic to aspirin should not use this plant either. People with asthma should not use it: the Turkish monograph states it has been shown to cause breathing difficulty — salicylate sensitivity often goes with asthma. People with a bleeding problem should not use it. The Turkish monograph also notes that long-term use can bring gastrointestinal irritation, constipation, iron-deficiency anaemia and nutritional disturbance. The EU monograph states that concomitant use with salicylates and other NSAIDs is not recommended without medical advice. If fever exceeds 39°C, persists, or comes with severe headache, see a doctor. For the joint-pain indication, it is not for use in acute arthritis. Not recommended under 18, nor in pregnancy or lactation.

Traditional use

Europe — two indications

The EMA has two separate monographs (flower and aerial parts) and both give the same two indications under "traditional use": (1) supportive treatment of the common cold; (2) relief of minor articular pain. For the second the monograph sets a limit: not for use in acute arthritis, as that condition requires medical advice.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "herba, the flowers" and states plainly the warnings that follow from the salicylate content: not for anyone sensitive to salicylate compounds; long-term use can bring gastrointestinal irritation, constipation, iron-deficiency anaemia and nutritional disturbance, so people with a bleeding problem should not use it either; and people with asthma should not use it, because it has been shown to cause breathing difficulty.

Anatolia

Meadowsweet grows in damp meadows, along streams and at water margins in northern Turkey, recognisable in summer by its cream-coloured, honey-scented flower clusters.

In nature

Where it grows
Damp meadows, streamsides and water margins of Europe and western Asia. Found in the northern regions of Turkey.
Harvest season
Cream-coloured, strongly honey-scented flower clusters from June to August; the flower and aerial parts are gathered in flower.
Local names
Çayır kraliçesi, ergeç sakalı, keçi sakalı, teke sakalı — all four appear in the Turkish monograph.

Mix-up risk

Dropwort (Filipendula vulgaris) of the same genus is commoner in Turkey and looks similar; the EU monographs cover F. ulmaria only. The distinguishing feature is the leaf: F. ulmaria has large leaflets, few in number, while F. vulgaris has many fine ones. The Turkish name "keçi sakalı" (goat’s beard) is also used for other plants.

Preparation

The monographs describe oral preparations of the flower and aerial parts. The duration differs by indication: consult a doctor if symptoms have not resolved after a week for a cold or four weeks for joint pain. If fever exceeds 39°C, persists, or comes with severe headache, see a doctor. Extracts containing ethanol must carry the appropriate labelling. Not recommended under 18.

What science says

There is no randomised clinical trial of meadowsweet in humans. All the recent literature is at laboratory level: chemical profiling, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory measurements, gastroprotective effects in animal models, antimicrobial peptide work. These show what is in the plant; they do not show whether it relieves a cold or joint pain in a patient. The EU monographs rest both indications on long use. The plant’s chemistry, on the other hand, is well known and gives the logic of the use: it carries salicylate derivatives, and salicylic acid is the basis of aspirin’s active substance. That explains both where the indication (pain, fever) comes from and why the warnings resemble aspirin’s: salicylate sensitivity, bleeding risk, breathing difficulty in asthma, and the caution about using it with NSAIDs. But one thing must not be confused: a plant carrying the building block of a drug does not mean it is as effective as that drug — the amount, the form and the absorption all differ. The evidence level is Traditional.

Safety

Caution

Not for anyone sensitive to salicylate compounds — this contraindication appears in both the EU and the Turkish monograph and follows from the salicylate content; someone allergic to aspirin should not use this plant either. People with asthma should not use it: the Turkish monograph states it has been shown to cause breathing difficulty — salicylate sensitivity often goes with asthma. People with a bleeding problem should not use it. The Turkish monograph also notes that long-term use can bring gastrointestinal irritation, constipation, iron-deficiency anaemia and nutritional disturbance. Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Salicylates and other painkillers (NSAIDs)The Turkish monograph states the plant should not be used together with salicylates and their derivatives or with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs; the EU monograph puts the same thing as "not recommended without medical advice". The reason is plain: the plant already carries salicylate, and adding a drug that works the same way stacks both the effect and the risk.
Blood-thinning medicinesThe Turkish monograph states it should not be used together with anticoagulants. Salicylates can raise bleeding risk, and this group already works within a narrow balance.
No interactions reported in the EMA monographThe European monograph lists nothing in its interaction section, but in its warnings it ties concomitant use with salicylates and NSAIDs to medical advice.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Meadowsweet.
