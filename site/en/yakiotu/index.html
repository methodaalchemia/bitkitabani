Fireweed

Epilobium angustifolium L.
Evidence: TraditionalCaution

The plant’s name looks at the same event in two languages: fireweed in English, "yakıotu" (burn-herb) in Turkish. Both come from its being among the first species to colonise burnt or cleared ground — in the first summer after a forest fire it can cover the gap with its pink-purple spikes. A plant named for its ecological behaviour rather than its use is rare; here the name tells you where to find it.

Other names
The Turkish monograph covers two species at once: Epilobium angustifolium L. and E. parviflorum Schreb., under the Turkish names "yakıotu, ırazyakıotu, küçük çiçekli yakıotu". In English: willow herb, fireweed — named for being among the first plants to colonise burnt ground.

In short

What is Fireweed?

Fireweed (Epilobium angustifolium L.) is a plant of the Onagraceae (çuhaçiçeğigiller) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the aerial parts.

What is Fireweed used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": relief of lower urinary tract symptoms related to benign prostatic hyperplasia — after serious conditions have been excluded by a medical doctor. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no randomised clinical trial of fireweed tested on its own; human studies test multi-ingredient mixtures containing it, and the two need separating.

Is Fireweed safe?

Safety label: caution. Not for anyone sensitive to the active substance or to the Onagraceae family. Use under 18 is contraindicated in the Turkish monograph — an unusually firm wording in this catalog, where most monographs say "not recommended". If complaints worsen, or if fever, spasms, blood in the urine, painful urination or urinary retention occur, see a doctor. Not recommended in pregnancy or lactation for lack of data; the EU monograph notes that pregnancy and lactation are not relevant given the indication. No undesirable effects are reported in the monographs, but the wording is "none known". The most important warning is not about toxicity: because lower urinary tract symptoms can belong to other and serious conditions, the diagnosis has to come before the herbal product.

Traditional use

Europe — prostate symptoms

The EMA monograph gives one indication, under "traditional use": relief of lower urinary tract symptoms related to benign prostatic hyperplasia — after serious conditions have been excluded by a medical doctor. That condition matters for the same reason it always does with this indication: the same symptoms can belong to prostate cancer.

Turkey — official list

The Turkish monograph gives the part used as "the aerial parts" and states the age limit firmly: use under 18 is contraindicated — not "not recommended" but contraindicated. If complaints worsen, or if fever, spasms, blood in the urine, painful urination or urinary retention occur, see a doctor.

Anatolia

Fireweed grows in forest clearings and high meadows of northern Anatolia, recognisable by its tall pink-purple flower spikes. It spreads quickly in clearings after fire, which is where the English name comes from; the Turkish "yakıotu" (burn-herb) looks at the same thing.

In nature

Where it grows
Temperate and cold regions of the northern hemisphere; forest clearings, post-fire ground and high meadows. Found in northern Anatolia in Turkey.
Harvest season
Tall pink-purple flower spikes in July and August; the aerial parts are gathered in flower.
Local names
Yakıotu, ırazyakıotu, küçük çiçekli yakıotu — all three appear in the Turkish monograph; the last two are used for E. parviflorum.

Mix-up risk

Many Epilobium species grow in Turkey and all are called yakıotu. The Turkish monograph covers two of them (E. angustifolium and E. parviflorum), while the EU monograph under the name Epilobii herba takes the small-flowered species as its basis — so the species name on the label matters. Fireweed is also usually sold in mixtures rather than on its own.

Preparation

The monograph describes preparations of the aerial parts: herbal tea, infusion, decoction or macerate. Use is long-term by nature. If complaints worsen, or the symptoms listed above occur, see a doctor. Use under 18 is contraindicated in the Turkish monograph.

What science says

There is no randomised clinical trial of fireweed tested on its own; human studies test multi-ingredient mixtures containing it, and the two need separating. A randomised controlled trial published in 2026 in Urologia is a good example: in 60 patients it compared tamsulosin with a mixture of five plants (Solidago virgaurea, Phyllanthus niruri, Epilobium angustifolium, Peumus boldus and Ononis spinosa) for symptoms related to a ureteral stent. Both treatments improved every measure from baseline, but the only significant difference between the groups was in the urinary symptoms domain, in favour of tamsulosin (at days 7 and 21); there was no significant difference in pain, general health, quality of work, sexual score or additional problems. The authors conclude from this that the mixture could be as effective as tamsulosin; that reading should be taken with care, since the significant difference on the main domain favoured tamsulosin and the trial was open-label, with 60 patients, in a different indication. In any case the trial measures not fireweed but a five-plant mixture: how much any one plant in it contributed cannot be known. One curious detail is that four of those five plants are in this same batch of the catalog — fireweed, boldo, restharrow and goldenrod. The EU monograph rests its indication on long use. The evidence level is Traditional.

Safety

Caution

Not for anyone sensitive to the active substance or to the Onagraceae family. Use under 18 is contraindicated in the Turkish monograph — an unusually firm wording in this catalog, where most monographs say "not recommended". If complaints worsen, or if fever, spasms, blood in the urine, painful urination or urinary retention occur, see a doctor. Not recommended in pregnancy or lactation for lack of data; the EU monograph notes that pregnancy and lactation are not relevant given the indication. No undesirable effects are reported in the monographs, but the wording is "none known". The most important warning is not about toxicity: because lower urinary tract symptoms can belong to other and serious conditions, the diagnosis has to come before the herbal product.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No drug interactions reported in either monographBoth the EU and the Turkish monograph leave the interaction section empty. "None reported" is not evidence of no interaction; it means not studied.
Prostate medicinesThere is no interaction study; the issue here is not interaction but substitution. In the trial above, the mixture containing fireweed was compared with tamsulosin, and the significant difference in urinary symptoms favoured tamsulosin. Replacing a prescribed prostate medicine with a herbal product is a decision for a doctor.
Multi-ingredient productsNot an interaction warning, but important in practice: fireweed is usually sold together with other plants rather than alone. Read the whole ingredient list on the label, because the warnings and interactions apply separately to every plant on it.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Fireweed.
