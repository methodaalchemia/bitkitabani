In short

What is Black locust?

Black locust (Robinia pseudoacacia L.) is a plant of the Fabaceae (legume family) family. Traditions that use it: North America, Europe. Part used: honey made from the flower nectar; the bark, seeds and leaves are poisonous and are not used.

What is Black locust used for?

Traditional use: A review published in Forest Ecology and Management in 2017 writes that the tree is valued in beekeeping for its high nectar yield. Scientific evidence: traditional. The medical literature on this tree concerns poisoning, not treatment.

Is Black locust safe?

Safety label: high risk. ⚠️ The bark, seeds and leaves are poisonous. In case reports, children who sucked or chewed the bark had abdominal pain, thirst, nausea, vomiting, a dry throat, muscle weakness, dilated pupils, headache, dizziness and diarrhoea; coma was seen in one case series. Children are the group most at risk; the bark of trees in school yards and parks, and the seeds inside the pods, should not be put in the mouth. It can be fatal to horses. Its splinters are not harmless either: severe tissue necrosis has been reported after a puncture. There is no safe use for pregnant or breastfeeding women or people on medication. If poisoning is suspected, call 114 (the National Poison Information Centre in Türkiye) or 112. These warnings concern the tree itself, not the honey.

Traditional use

Central Europe — a honey tree

A review published in Forest Ecology and Management in 2017 writes that the tree is valued in beekeeping for its high nectar yield. According to the review, honey yield changes with the age of the tree and reaches 418 kg per hectare a year in 15-year-old stands in Hungary. In Central Europe the largest producer of Robinia honey is Hungary, with about 25,000 tonnes a year. The honey sold on the market as "acacia honey" is the honey of this tree.

Hungary — afforestation and a "national tree"

According to the same review, the first large afforestation was carried out in Hungary in 1750, on 290 hectares, to protect a fortification. Today about 24% of Hungary’s forests consist of this tree, and it is regarded as the country’s unofficial national tree. Its fast growth, its water- and rot-resistant wood, firewood, leaf fodder and erosion control are the main reasons for its use.

Europe — a beloved and unwanted invader

A 2024 paper in Veterinární medicína reports that black locust was the first North American tree species brought to Europe. The 2017 review describes the conflict between the tree’s economic benefit and the harm it does to the environment: this nitrogen-fixing pioneer species has damaged 33,000 hectares of open sandy grassland in Hungary and has colonised 40% of the dry and semi-dry grassland in Austria.

The flowers — the only part not considered poisonous

According to the 2024 veterinary case report, all parts of the plant except the inflorescences are toxic, the bark and seeds especially. The polyphenol content of the flower extract has been the subject of laboratory studies in recent years. The sources relied on here contain no study establishing a safe amount of the flowers for human consumption.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew POWO its native range is the east-central and eastern USA; it is a shrub or tree and grows mainly in the temperate zone. In Europe it has been planted in gardens, along roads and in afforestation areas, and has spread from these on its own. It tolerates dry and poor soils; it does not tolerate a high water table or shade.
Harvest season
According to the review, the main factor limiting the tree in Central Europe is late spring frost. The sources relied on here give no flowering calendar for Türkiye.
Local names
In Turkish it is known as yalancı akasya ("false acacia"); the sources relied on here give no other local name.

Mix-up risk

The "acacia" in its name is misleading: the true acacias (Acacia) are a different genus. The species name pseudoacacia also means "false acacia". "Acacia honey", in turn, comes not from true acacia but from the nectar of this tree.

Preparation

Preparing and using it is not recommended; this page deliberately gives no recipe. The reason is that the bark, seeds and leaves cause poisoning in humans and animals and that no part other than the flowers is considered safe. There is no accepted dose; no EMA, WHO or Turkish (TİTCK) monograph could be found. The product consumed as food is the honey that bees make from the flower nectar.

What science says

The medical literature on this tree concerns poisoning, not treatment. In a 1989 study from Spain, six girls (mean age 6.5) who sucked or chewed the bark in a school yard had abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, muscle weakness, dilated pupils, dizziness and diarrhoea; 31.6% of those who tasted the bark fell ill, and all recovered with treatment. In another case series from 1990, three of seven boys developed neurological symptoms including coma, and all recovered. In a 2024 veterinary case report, one of two mares that ate the bark died and the other developed severe encephalopathy. A 2020 case report states that the toxalbumin in the tree, called robin, causes cell death by inhibiting protein synthesis, and that a patient developed tissue necrosis after a splinter injury. The studies with flower extracts are at the laboratory level; no therapeutic effect has been shown in humans.
