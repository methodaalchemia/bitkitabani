Gum tragacanth milkvetch

Astragalus gummifer Labill.; A. longifolius Lam.; A. muschianus Kotschy & Boiss. ex Boiss.
Evidence: TraditionalGenerally safe

Turkey is the world centre of *Astragalus* diversity — over four hundred species, many of them found nowhere else. The gum of this thorny cushion of a plant has been industry’s quiet material for a century, from printing ink to pill binder, from confectioner’s pastillage to salad dressing. In Bingöl the same plant is the signature of a registered honey.

Other names
Turkish *geven*, *keven*; its gum is kitre (English tragacanth, Persian katira). ⚠️ The species listed as "geven" on Turkey’s official medicinal plant list is not this one — that monograph is for the Chinese *Astragalus membranaceus*; Anatolia’s milkvetches are not on the list. This page is for the Anatolian species. The part used is the gum that exudes from the stem; the plant itself is pasture and a honey plant. No EU or WHO monograph.

In short

What is Gum tragacanth milkvetch?

Gum tragacanth milkvetch (Astragalus gummifer Labill.; A. longifolius Lam.; A. muschianus Kotschy & Boiss. ex Boiss.) is a plant of the Fabaceae (legume family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Asia and Siberia. Part used: the gum exuding from the stem (tragacanth); the flowering branches as pasture and a honey plant.

What is Gum tragacanth milkvetch used for?

Traditional use: Milkvetch is the most familiar plant of the central and eastern Anatolian steppe: ground-hugging, thorny, growing in cushions. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no clinical evidence for a medicinal use of the Anatolian milkvetches, and this page makes no such claim.

Is Gum tragacanth milkvetch safe?

Safety label: generally safe. Tragacanth is a widely used food gum whose safety has been assessed. It should not be swallowed dry — because it swells, it can obstruct the throat or oesophagus; take it with plenty of liquid. Anyone with difficulty swallowing or a narrowed bowel should ask a doctor. Hypersensitivity has been reported with occupational exposure (pharmaceutical and confectionery work). The plant itself is thorny and can injure the hands. ⚠️ The autoimmune-disease and immunosuppressant warnings that apply to Chinese milkvetch do not apply to these species; those belong to its own page.

Traditional use

Anatolia — the thorny cushion of the steppe

Milkvetch is the most familiar plant of the central and eastern Anatolian steppe: ground-hugging, thorny, growing in cushions. Turkey is one of the world centres of diversity for *Astragalus*; more than four hundred species grow here and many of them nowhere else. In the villages the roots and branches are fuel, the plant is grazing, and the gum that oozes from the stem is something to sell.

Tragacanth — the gum of the pharmacy and the confectioner

Cut in summer, the milkvetch stem weeps a liquid that dries into translucent ribbons: gum tragacanth. It swells in water into a thick gel, and so, before the modern drug industry, it bound pills, held syrups in suspension and thickened ointments. It is still a pharmaceutical excipient and a food additive, E 413; in confectionery it binds pastillage. Its safety has been assessed separately against food-additive criteria (*Food Additives and Contaminants*, 1989). ⚠️ Tragacanth is not a medicine but an excipient — its job is to give body to whatever carries it.

Bingöl — an indicator plant of the honey

The denomination-of-origin registration for Bingöl honey counts milkvetch among the five taxa that distinguish it. The document records *A. muschianus* and *A. gummifer* as abundant on the steppe; in the pollen table of the honey *A. longifolius* is dominant, *A. gummifer* secondary to minor and *A. caspicus* a trace. Because milkvetch flowers through the dry steppe summer it is a true nectar source for bees — not one of the wind-pollinated species whose pollen simply falls into the honey from the air.

⚠️ Chinese milkvetch is not the same plant

The supplement sold as "astragalus" is the *Astragalus membranaceus* of Chinese medicine — a dried root sold on immune claims, and listed by the Turkish authority under the same Turkish name, "geven". That is not the milkvetch of Bingöl. Its evidence, warnings and drug interactions do not transfer to the Anatolian species on this page; they are in their own record.

In nature

Where it grows
The steppes of central, eastern and south-eastern Anatolia, generally between 1,000 and 2,500 m, on dry stony slopes. ⚠️ Many Anatolian milkvetches are endemic and vulnerable to over-collection; cutting the stem for gum does not kill the plant, pulling it up by the root does.
Harvest season
Flowers from June to August; the gum is also taken in summer — the stem is cut and the liquid that seeps out dries into ribbons within a few days.
Local names
Geven, keven, gevenotu, kitre, katıra; tragacanth (English), katira (Persian).

Mix-up risk

⚠️ Chinese milkvetch (*Astragalus membranaceus*) is a different plant — and because the Turkish authority lists it under the same Turkish name, the confusion has reached official documents. ⚠️ Tragacanth is not gum arabic; gum arabic comes from *Acacia*, another family. ⚠️ Not every thorny cushion plant of the steppe is a milkvetch; *Acantholimon* and some *Onobrychis* species look similar from a distance.

Preparation

Tragacanth swells slowly in water; it is not swallowed dry but taken with plenty of liquid. In pharmacy and food it is used as a ready powder or in ribbons, as a thickener and suspending agent. The plant itself is not human food — it is pasture and a honey plant, and its thorns keep it from being gathered by hand.

What science says

There is no clinical evidence for a medicinal use of the Anatolian milkvetches, and this page makes no such claim. What is established belongs to pharmaceutical and food technology: the thickening, suspending and binding function of tragacanth gum — a property of the material, not a drug effect. The gum’s safety as a food additive has been assessed against modern criteria (*Food Addit Contam*, 1989). ⚠️ The immune and fatigue studies on Chinese milkvetch (*A. membranaceus*) do not transfer: a different species, a different part (the root), a different use. Evidence rating "traditional".

Safety

Generally safe

Tragacanth is a widely used food gum whose safety has been assessed. It should not be swallowed dry — because it swells, it can obstruct the throat or oesophagus; take it with plenty of liquid. Anyone with difficulty swallowing or a narrowed bowel should ask a doctor. Hypersensitivity has been reported with occupational exposure (pharmaceutical and confectionery work). The plant itself is thorny and can injure the hands. ⚠️ The autoimmune-disease and immunosuppressant warnings that apply to Chinese milkvetch do not apply to these species; those belong to its own page.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Oral medicinesGel-forming gums can slow a drug’s absorption; it is sensible not to take tragacanth at the same time as medication but a few hours apart. There is no clinical interaction study specific to tragacanth; the principle comes from what gums have in common.
Diabetes medicines (theoretical)Gel-forming fibres can slow the absorption of sugar; no such effect has been shown for tragacanth. Blood sugar should be monitored with regular use.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Gum tragacanth milkvetch.
