Black benniseed

Sesamum radiatum Thonn. ex Hornem.
Evidence: TraditionalGenerally safe

Most of its names in African languages describe neither its taste nor its look but the slippery texture of the leaf in the mouth — which is exactly what the kitchen wants from it.

Other names
English black benniseed, black sesame; it has many local names in Benin and Nigeria. It has no established Turkish name; "kara susam" (black sesame) is a descriptive name used here to distinguish it from sesame (*Sesamum indicum*). Neither the European Medicines Agency nor the Turkish agency has a monograph for this species.

In short

What is Black benniseed?

Black benniseed (Sesamum radiatum Thonn. ex Hornem.) is a plant of the Pedaliaceae (sesame family) family. Traditions that use it: Africa. Part used: leaf (as a vegetable); seed.

What is Black benniseed used for?

Traditional use: A review published in Economic Botany in 2018 writes that in Africa the leaves of wild and cultivated Pedaliaceae are gathered as vegetables, and that these leaves, with their protein, lipid, calcium and iron content, are eaten alongside dry cereal staples. Scientific evidence: traditional. The published literature on this species is thin, and in the sources relied on here there is no study in humans at all.

Is Black benniseed safe?

Safety label: generally safe. The leaf is a vegetable and is eaten regularly in West Africa; the sources relied on here report no harm from it. Because there is no monograph there is no official list of contraindications. People with a sesame allergy should be careful: this species is the same genus as sesame and the possibility of cross-reaction has not been measured. There are no data for pregnancy, lactation, children or drug interactions, so concentrated products made from the oil or the seed are not recommended.

Traditional use

West and Central Africa — a mucilaginous leaf vegetable

A review published in Economic Botany in 2018 writes that in Africa the leaves of wild and cultivated Pedaliaceae are gathered as vegetables, and that these leaves, with their protein, lipid, calcium and iron content, are eaten alongside dry cereal staples. The same review describes the water-soluble mucilage of the leaves as a source of fibre and records that many African vernacular names refer directly to the slimy texture of the leaf. It credits the discovery of these leaves to women gathering greens to eat with cereal dishes.

Benin — a vegetable in the course of domestication

The references of the same review show that the species is cultivated as a leafy vegetable in Benin and that work has been done on domesticating it. This is a plant on the way from wild to cultivated.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew’s records its range covers 28 regions: most of West and Central Africa, along with Cape Verde and Borneo. It is a herbaceous plant of hot, humid country, growing at field edges and near villages.
Harvest season
The sources relied on here give no collection calendar for Turkey; the plant does not grow there.
Local names
It has no established Turkish local name.

Mix-up risk

It is the same genus as sesame (*Sesamum indicum*) but a different species: in sesame what is eaten is the seed, in this species mainly the leaf. The use and the restrictions of one species do not belong to its relative.

Preparation

No official monograph sets an accepted dose; the leaf is a vegetable and is eaten cooked. The sources relied on here give no medicinal preparation or measure.

What science says

The published literature on this species is thin, and in the sources relied on here there is no study in humans at all. A study published in Polymers in 2021 put the plant’s oil into a polymer film with gold nanoparticles and reported that the film reduced inflammatory markers in cell culture and inhibited the growth of Leishmania and Trypanosoma cultures. That is a materials-science study: what was examined is a composite film made in a laboratory, not the plant or any traditional preparation of it. Its results cannot be applied to eating the leaf or the seed.

Safety

Generally safe

The leaf is a vegetable and is eaten regularly in West Africa; the sources relied on here report no harm from it. Because there is no monograph there is no official list of contraindications. People with a sesame allergy should be careful: this species is the same genus as sesame and the possibility of cross-reaction has not been measured. There are no data for pregnancy, lactation, children or drug interactions, so concentrated products made from the oil or the seed are not recommended.
