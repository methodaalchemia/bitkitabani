Blessed thistle

Cnicus benedictus L. (syn. Centaurea benedicta (L.) L.)
Evidence: TraditionalHigh risk

Its name carries the claim of an era: "blessed thistle", the monastery plant held a cure-all against plague in the Middle Ages. Centuries later the European regulator wrote a monograph for the same plant in 2021, and only two measured sentences remained: loss of appetite and mild indigestion, on long use alone. How a plant’s reputation shrinks over time can be read from its name and its monograph together.

Other names
Turkish kutsal devedikeni, mübarek dikeni — both renderings of the Latin and English names (benedictus, blessed thistle); this catalog has no sourced established Turkish name and there is no Turkish monograph. In English: blessed thistle, holy thistle. The record rests on the EU monograph (2021) and POWO.

In short

What is Blessed thistle?

Blessed thistle (Cnicus benedictus L. (syn. Centaurea benedicta (L.) L.)) is a plant of the Asteraceae (daisy family) family. Traditions that use it: Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Europe. Part used: the flowering aerial parts.

What is Blessed thistle used for?

Traditional use: The EMA monograph (2021) places both under "traditional use": (1) temporary loss of appetite — before meals; (2) symptomatic relief of dyspepsia and mild spasmodic disorders of the gastrointestinal tract. Scientific evidence: traditional. There is no clinical trial of blessed thistle in humans; recent PubMed publications on the plant concern composition (the bitter sesquiterpene lactone cnicin) and laboratory work.

Is Blessed thistle safe?

Safety label: high risk. Not for anyone hypersensitive to the daisy family (sunflower, chrysanthemum, mugwort, milk thistle). Doses above 5 g per cup may cause gastric irritation and vomiting — the monograph’s overdose note; the tea should not be made stronger on the idea that "more bitter is better". Not recommended under 18, in pregnancy or lactation. Tinctures and liquid extracts may contain ethanol. No other adverse effects known; no interactions reported. Bitter drugs are generally unsuitable in active gastric ulcer — not in the monograph, but the class’s common caution.

Traditional use

Europe — two indications

The EMA monograph (2021) places both under "traditional use": (1) temporary loss of appetite — before meals; (2) symptomatic relief of dyspepsia and mild spasmodic disorders of the gastrointestinal tract. Classic bitter-drug logic: the bitter taste stimulates digestive secretion. More than 5 g per cup of tea may cause gastric irritation and vomiting.

Turkey

There is no monograph for this plant on the Turkish list. An annual thistle of Mediterranean origin, it grows in western and southern Turkey. This catalog has no sourced Anatolian folk use.

Europe — a monastery plant

The "benedictus" (blessed) in its name comes from medieval monastery gardens; it was held a cure-all in plague epidemics and became an ingredient of bitter herbal liqueurs such as Benedictine. The two indications the EU monograph approves are the modest surviving part of that far more ambitious tradition: appetite and digestion.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to the Mediterranean basin; dry stony ground and field margins. Grows in western and southern Turkey.
Harvest season
Annual; yellow flower heads with spiny bracts in summer. The flowering aerial parts are gathered in summer.
Local names
Kutsal devedikeni, mübarek dikeni — translated names; no unsourced vernacular name given.

Mix-up risk

⚠️ Its name gets confused with devedikeni (milk thistle, Silybum marianum, the liver plant); the two are separate genera, with separate monographs and separate indications. Many species of spiny daisy-family plants look alike; a pharmacy product should be preferred. ⚠️ Because the plant’s synonym is Centaurea benedicta, it is also confused with the cornflower (Centaurea) species; those are separate plants and are not within the scope of this monograph.

Preparation

Adults: 1–2 g of the drug three times daily; or as tea, 1.5–3 g of the comminuted drug as an infusion or decoction in 150 ml of water, three times daily; liquid extract 1.5–2 ml three times daily; tincture 3–6 ml three times daily. Before meals for loss of appetite. See a doctor if symptoms last longer than two weeks. Not recommended under 18.

What science says

There is no clinical trial of blessed thistle in humans; recent PubMed publications on the plant concern composition (the bitter sesquiterpene lactone cnicin) and laboratory work. Even with a date as recent as 2021 the EU monograph rests the indication on long use alone — the regulator searched in recent years and found no clinical evidence. The shared mechanism of bitter drugs (stimulation of digestive secretion through taste) is the same as in this catalog’s gentian and bogbean records. The evidence level is Traditional.

Safety

High risk

Not for anyone hypersensitive to the daisy family (sunflower, chrysanthemum, mugwort, milk thistle). Doses above 5 g per cup may cause gastric irritation and vomiting — the monograph’s overdose note; the tea should not be made stronger on the idea that "more bitter is better". Not recommended under 18, in pregnancy or lactation. Tinctures and liquid extracts may contain ethanol. No other adverse effects known; no interactions reported. Bitter drugs are generally unsuitable in active gastric ulcer — not in the monograph, but the class’s common caution.
