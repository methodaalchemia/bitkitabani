Black horehound

Ballota nigra L.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

Among the plant’s most studied compounds are two substances named after the genus: the phenylpropanoid glycoside ballotetroside and the diterpene ballonigrin. Both were isolated and identified from Ballota species.

Other names
English black horehound; the pharmaceutical drug name is Ballotae nigrae herba (the dried flowering tops). Its synonym is Ballota foetida Lam. According to Kew’s plant list its native range is Europe, Türkiye, the Caucasus, Iran, the eastern Mediterranean and North Africa. Subspecies such as Ballota nigra subsp. anatolica grow in Türkiye. There is no EMA monograph; there is an ESCOP monograph (2015).

In short

What is Black horehound?

Black horehound (Ballota nigra L.) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: Europe, Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Africa. Part used: dried flowering tops.

What is Black horehound used for?

Traditional use: A 2000 study (Daels-Rakotoarison et al.) describes black horehound as “a European plant known for its neurosedative properties”. Scientific evidence: limited. The evidence is predominantly at the laboratory and animal level.

Is Black horehound safe?

Safety label: caution. Its safety in humans has not been studied systematically; the ESCOP monograph addresses contraindications, interactions and adverse effects, but the details are not in its open-access summary. Because of its sedative effect it may cause drowsiness; care is needed when driving or operating machinery. It has been shown to lower blood glucose and raise insulin in animals; people with diabetes should be careful. There are no safety data on use in pregnancy, breastfeeding or children; it should not be used in these periods. In a 2014 study examining samples gathered in Pakistan, chromium was above the permissible value (1.5 mg/kg) in all parts of the plant; plants gathered from polluted sites may carry heavy metals. For persistent anxiety or insomnia a doctor should be consulted.

Traditional use

Europe — a calming herb

A 2000 study (Daels-Rakotoarison et al.) describes black horehound as “a European plant known for its neurosedative properties”. The 2015 monograph of the European Scientific Cooperative on Phytotherapy (ESCOP) gives the drug’s field of use as tenseness, restlessness and irritability with difficulty in falling asleep. This is an indication based on traditional use and a limited number of studies.

The Mediterranean and Middle East — the genus in folk medicine

A 2019 review (Morteza-Semnani and Ghanbarimasir) reports that Ballota species are used in folk medicine as antiemetic, antispasmodic, sedative, vermifuge and antihaemorrhoid agents and for cough. This information is for the whole genus of 33–35 species; not every use belongs to black horehound.

Türkiye — studies on Ballota species

The 16 Ballota species growing in Türkiye, among them Ballota nigra subsp. anatolica, were examined for their antioxidant properties in a 2004 study (Citoğlu et al.). That study is laboratory data; no record of folk use in Türkiye appears in the sources of this page.

In nature

Where it grows
A perennial herb. Its native range is Europe, Türkiye, the Caucasus, Iran, the eastern Mediterranean and North Africa; it was later carried to Britain and North America.
Harvest season
Flowers in summer; the tops are gathered during flowering.
Local names
No established local name in Turkish.

Mix-up risk

Marrubium vulgare (English white horehound) belongs to a separate genus; the EMA monograph (Marrubii herba) belongs to that plant and its field of use is cough and digestion, not sedation. The products of the two plants should not be used in place of one another.

Preparation

The part used is the tops, gathered during flowering and dried; the traditional forms are an infusion (tea) and a hydroalcoholic extract. There is no EMA, WHO or TİTCK monograph; the ESCOP monograph contains dosage information, but this is not in its open-access summary. For this reason no dose is given on this page; doses are not invented. According to a 2015 study, shoots collected at the flowering period contain more phenolic compounds and flavonoids than immature plants.

What science says

The evidence is predominantly at the laboratory and animal level. In the 2000 study by Daels-Rakotoarison and colleagues, four of the five phenylpropanoids isolated from the plant bound to benzodiazepine, dopamine and opioid receptors in the test tube (half-maximal concentration 0.4–4.7 mg/mL); the authors relate this to the plant’s known sedative effect. The summary of the ESCOP monograph reports sedative, anxiety-reducing and barbiturate-sleep-prolonging effects in rodents. The only human data come from an open (uncontrolled) study in patients with generalised anxiety disorder: after 60 days of use the responder rate was 65%; because there was no placebo group, this result does not prove efficacy. In a 2007 rat study (Nusier et al.) the extract (7 days, 400 mg/kg) lowered blood glucose and total cholesterol and raised the insulin level. In addition, its phenylpropanoids inhibited LDL oxidation in the test tube and showed moderate activity against Staphylococcus aureus.

Safety

Caution

Its safety in humans has not been studied systematically; the ESCOP monograph addresses contraindications, interactions and adverse effects, but the details are not in its open-access summary. Because of its sedative effect it may cause drowsiness; care is needed when driving or operating machinery. It has been shown to lower blood glucose and raise insulin in animals; people with diabetes should be careful. There are no safety data on use in pregnancy, breastfeeding or children; it should not be used in these periods. In a 2014 study examining samples gathered in Pakistan, chromium was above the permissible value (1.5 mg/kg) in all parts of the plant; plants gathered from polluted sites may carry heavy metals. For persistent anxiety or insomnia a doctor should be consulted.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
Sedatives, sleeping medicines, anti-anxiety medicines and alcoholThe plant’s compounds bind to the benzodiazepine receptor in the test tube and prolong barbiturate sleep in rodents; an increase in sedative effect is possible. There is no interaction study in humans.
Diabetes medicines and insulinIn the 2007 rat study the extract lowered blood glucose and increased insulin release; with combined use blood glucose may fall too far. The data rest on an animal study.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Black horehound.
