Quassia

Quassia amara L.
Evidence: LimitedCaution

The only effect of this famously bitter Amazonian tree tested in humans by a controlled study is on the skin of the face: in a randomised double-blind trial its 4% gel ran against two prescription creams in seborrhoeic dermatitis and came out ahead.

Other names
It has no established Turkish name; it appears as kuasya, acı ağaç and "acı kabuk". English bitterwood, amargo, Surinam quassia. The species name "amara" means bitter; quassin in its wood is counted among the most bitter natural substances known. Neither the European Medicines Agency nor the Turkish agency has a monograph for this species.

In short

What is Quassia?

Quassia (Quassia amara L.) is a plant of the Simaroubaceae family. Traditions that use it: Amazon. Part used: wood and bark.

What is Quassia used for?

Traditional use: According to a South American survey in Current Drug Safety, Quassia amara is among the plants most often used in self-medication in Argentina; over one year in 73 pharmacies of Buenos Aires province the survey counted 37,102 self-medicated plants against only 1,532 physician-prescribed ones. Scientific evidence: limited. A randomised, double-blind comparative study in the Journal of Drugs in Dermatology divided 60 patients with facial seborrhoeic dermatitis into three arms and gave 4% Quassia amara gel, 2% ketoconazole gel or 1% ciclopirox olamine gel for four weeks; 54 patients completed the study, all three options were effective, and the quassia extract showed a significant advantage in efficacy.

Is Quassia safe?

Safety label: caution. The only form tested in humans is a topical 4% gel, and safety and tolerability were good in both studies. Oral use is not the same thing, and that is what sets the safety level of this record. It must not be taken orally during pregnancy or breastfeeding. The wood and bark used here carry a compound called quassin. The opinion on quassin adopted by the EU Scientific Committee on Food (SCF) on 2 July 2002 states that two reproductive toxicity studies of quassin and Quassia extract give rise to toxicological concern: effects were noted at the lowest dose tested — a quassin equivalent of 0.1 mg/kg body weight, taken as the LOEL — and no NOEL could be established, so a proper risk assessment could not be performed. The same opinion notes that there are no chronic toxicity, carcinogenicity, genotoxicity or metabolism studies for quassin. The two studies it rests on are these: Njar et al. (1995, Planta Med) report that quassin inhibited both basal and LH-stimulated testosterone secretion by rat Leydig cells; Raji and Bolarinwa (1997, Life Sci) gave stem wood extract in the drinking water and found falls in the weight of testis, epididymis and seminal vesicle, in epididymal sperm count and in serum testosterone, LH and FSH in male rats. It would be wrong to read the literature as "consistent": the SCF expressly notes that in that study the effects were not dose-dependent and appeared alike at all three dose levels, that all the parameters measured returned to normal after an eight-week recovery period, and that the study was weak — only five animals per group, no histopathology performed, and insufficient information on litter sizes. The study in Reproductive Toxicology (Parveen et al., 2003) gave a chloroform extract of the bark by intramuscular injection for 15 days and reports falls in testis and epididymis weight, in sperm count, motility and viability and in epididymal α-glucosidase activity — that study did not measure testosterone; the testosterone finding comes from Life Sci 1997 alone. A 2021 study in Andrologia reports a Dag-like defect of the sperm tail (angulation and coiling of the flagellum) in mice given Quassia amara/quassin. In female rats, oral quassin (60 days, 0.1–2.0 mg/kg) lowered ovary and uterus weight, serum oestrogen and litter number and weight, but those effects too were ameliorated after quassin was withdrawn (Niger J Physiol Sci 2010). These are animal studies; no reproductive effect has been measured in humans — the SCF likewise records that no human data on quassin are available; since there is no human safety data, caution is warranted. Oral use should be avoided in children and by anyone of childbearing age. The South American survey lists the plant among those most often self-medicated, and the review holds that this unsupervised use is the real risk. As there is no monograph there is no official list of contraindications.

Traditional use

Tropical America — bitter tonic and topical use

According to a South American survey in Current Drug Safety, Quassia amara is among the plants most often used in self-medication in Argentina; over one year in 73 pharmacies of Buenos Aires province the survey counted 37,102 self-medicated plants against only 1,532 physician-prescribed ones. The same review holds that self-medication is the main risk and that adverse reactions could be avoided by taking contraindications and interactions into account.

Dermatology — as a 4% gel

The use tested in humans is not oral but extract applied to the skin. Because antiparasitic and anti-inflammatory properties have been reported, a gel containing 4% Quassia amara extract has been tried in rosacea and facial seborrhoeic dermatitis.

In nature

Where it grows
According to Kew’s records its range covers 33 regions: humid forests of the Amazon basin, Central America and the Caribbean. It is a small tree or shrub.
Harvest season
It does not grow in Turkey; the sources relied on here give no collection calendar for Turkey. Wood and bark are gathered year-round.
Local names
No Turkish regional name. Local names: quassia, amargo, hombre grande.

Mix-up risk

Jamaica quassia (Picrasma excelsa) is a separate species and may be sold under the same name. Which species a "bitterwood" product comes from may not be clear from the label.

Preparation

No official monograph sets an accepted dose. What the human studies relied on here used was a ready-made gel containing 4% standardised extract, applied topically; that is not the same thing as a decoction made at home. The sources relied on here give no accepted measure for oral use.

What science says

A randomised, double-blind comparative study in the Journal of Drugs in Dermatology divided 60 patients with facial seborrhoeic dermatitis into three arms and gave 4% Quassia amara gel, 2% ketoconazole gel or 1% ciclopirox olamine gel for four weeks; 54 patients completed the study, all three options were effective, and the quassia extract showed a significant advantage in efficacy. A study in the Journal of Clinical Pharmacology tried the same gel in rosacea, but it was single-centre and open-label (no placebo or comparator arm), with 27 of 30 patients completing. Both studies are small and come from the same circle of investigators. The sources relied on here contain no controlled human study of oral use; reported effects against malaria and microbes come from animal and laboratory work. Moreover the study reporting antimalarial activity in mice (Ajaiyeoba et al., 1999, Journal of Ethnopharmacology) was done with leaf extracts of Q. amara — the highest suppressive activity was shown by the hexane and methanol extracts of the leaf — whereas the part used in this record is wood and bark; because the part differs, that finding does not carry directly to this record.
