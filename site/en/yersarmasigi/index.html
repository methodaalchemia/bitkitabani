Ground ivy

Evidence: LimitedGenerally safe

The study carried out in Latvia showed that this wild herb can be grown in organic farming: the highest dry herb yield was 565.8 kg per hectare, and cultivation did not markedly change the plant’s chemical composition compared with the wild plant.

Other names
English ground ivy. Its synonym is Nepeta glechoma Benth. Despite its name it is not related to true ivy (Hedera); it is a creeping herb of the mint family. According to Kew’s plant list its native range is the whole of Europe, Türkiye, the Caucasus, Central Asia and Siberia; it was later carried to North America. There is no EMA, WHO or TİTCK monograph.

In short

What is Ground ivy?

Ground ivy (Glechoma hederacea L.) is a plant of the Lamiaceae (mint family) family. Traditions that use it: Europe, Anatolia and the Mediterranean, Asia and Siberia. Part used: flowering aerial parts.

What is Ground ivy used for?

Traditional use: A 2022 study carried out in Latvia (Sile et al.) reports that the aerial parts of the plant have been used in folk medicine for centuries for colds, asthma, bronchitis, gastric diseases, diabetes and inflammatory conditions. Scientific evidence: limited. There are no clinical studies of ground ivy in humans; the data are at the cell and animal level.

Is Ground ivy safe?

Safety label: generally safe. Its safety in humans has not been studied. The available data are at the laboratory level: in a 2002 study and in the Latvian study the extracts were not found toxic in the brine shrimp test at the concentrations tested (at most 10 mg/mL); in the samples gathered in Croatia heavy metals were below the permitted levels. These findings do not show that long-term use in humans is safe. There are no data on use in pregnancy, breastfeeding or children; it should not be used in these periods. People with gallstones, jaundice or liver disease should not attempt self-treatment on the basis of animal studies but should see a doctor. Plants gathered from roadsides may carry pollution. Anyone on regular medication should consult their doctor.

Traditional use

Europe — a folk herb for colds and the stomach

A 2022 study carried out in Latvia (Sile et al.) reports that the aerial parts of the plant have been used in folk medicine for centuries for colds, asthma, bronchitis, gastric diseases, diabetes and inflammatory conditions. Studies from Croatia (Šeremet et al.) describe the plant as a “traditional plant” used for generations. These are traditional uses; they have not been tested in humans.

Chinese medicine — gallstones and jaundice

A 2017 study (Wang et al.) notes that in traditional Chinese medicine the plant is frequently prescribed to patients with gallstones, dropsy, abscess, diabetes, inflammation and jaundice. The material used in studies from China is mostly the plant’s close relative in East Asia (it also appears under the name Glechoma hederacea var. longituba); it should not be regarded as identical to the European plant.

Food — nutritional value and a candidate additive

The 2022 study examining samples gathered from the wild in Croatia found the plant rich in insoluble fibre, protein, calcium and potassium, and proposed that it be considered as a functional food ingredient. Sile and colleagues write that its use as a food additive has also been studied.

In nature

Where it grows
Forests, scrub and roadsides. It is a perennial; it spreads by creeping stems, and its flowering shoots grow upright (17.6 cm on average in the Latvian study). Its native range is Europe, Türkiye, the Caucasus, Central Asia and Siberia.
Harvest season
Produces flowering shoots in spring; for phenolic compounds it is gathered during flowering.
Local names
No established local name in Turkish.

Mix-up risk

True ivy (Hedera helix) is a woody climbing plant from a different family; although the name is similar, its use and safety are different. The East Asian plant used in Chinese medicine (Glechoma hederacea var. longituba) and the European plant should not be regarded as the same.

Preparation

The traditional form is an infusion (tea) or decoction of the flowering aerial parts. Because there is no EMA, WHO or TİTCK monograph, there is no accepted dose. According to the Latvian study the most suitable harvesting time for phenolic compounds is the flowering period. The essential oil content is low (0.32–2.98 mL per kilogram of dry weight).

What science says

There are no clinical studies of ground ivy in humans; the data are at the cell and animal level. In the 2017 rat study a hot water extract reduced blood markers, inflammation and the increase in connective tissue in liver injury produced by ligating the bile duct. In a 2021 study (Xiao et al.) a commercial extract, given three times a day to mice fed a gallstone-forming diet, lowered the size and number of gallstone crystals and total cholesterol; it did not, however, change the bile flow rate. In the Latvian study the extracts showed an anti-inflammatory effect in immune cells and did not harm these cells between 50 and 750 μg/mL. A 2023 study (Sławińska et al.) reported that in human blood cells the extracts reduced oxidative DNA damage but lowered cell viability to at most 65% over 24 hours. The main compounds are rosmarinic and chlorogenic acid, and rutin, apigenin and luteolin derivatives.

Safety

Generally safe

Its safety in humans has not been studied. The available data are at the laboratory level: in a 2002 study and in the Latvian study the extracts were not found toxic in the brine shrimp test at the concentrations tested (at most 10 mg/mL); in the samples gathered in Croatia heavy metals were below the permitted levels. These findings do not show that long-term use in humans is safe. There are no data on use in pregnancy, breastfeeding or children; it should not be used in these periods. People with gallstones, jaundice or liver disease should not attempt self-treatment on the basis of animal studies but should see a doctor. Plants gathered from roadsides may carry pollution. Anyone on regular medication should consult their doctor.
