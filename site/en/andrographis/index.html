Andrographis

Andrographis paniculata (Burm.f.) Nees
Evidence: LimitedCaution

The 250th plant in this catalog is a plant outside the official lists — and that is no accident. There is a 7,175-patient meta-analysis for Andrographis; even so the European regulator could not write a monograph, because the rules ask something else: "has it been used in the EU for thirty years?" (no — Scandinavia, the 1990s) and "have recognised efficacy and acceptable safety been shown?" (not with poor-quality trials). There is no straight line between a plant’s number of clinical trials and its regulatory status — the last lesson of this catalog’s 250 records.

In short

What is Andrographis?

Andrographis (Andrographis paniculata (Burm.f.) Nees) is a plant of the Acanthaceae (acanthus family) family. Traditions that use it: Asia and Siberia. Part used: the leaves.

What is Andrographis used for?

Traditional use: The European Medicines Agency concluded on 17 September 2013 that a monograph on Andrographis leaf "cannot be established at present", because neither the well-established-use requirement (recognised efficacy, acceptable safety, and the period having elapsed) nor the traditional-use requirement (sufficient period of use in the EU) is met. Scientific evidence: limited. Andrographis is a plant in this catalog to which the regulator said "no" while the literature is anything but empty.

Is Andrographis safe?

Safety label: caution. With no monograph there is no official safety section; in the meta-analysis adverse events were rare and mostly gastrointestinal. The plant is intensely bitter; stomach upset and taste disturbance are reported. Not in pregnancy or lactation — traditional sources consider it unsuitable in pregnancy and there are no data. Anyone with autoimmune disease or on immunosuppressants should not use it without asking a doctor: the plant is credited with immune effects. Allergic reactions have been reported. In non-standardised supplements the content is uncertain.

Traditional use

Europe — no monograph, a public statement

The European Medicines Agency concluded on 17 September 2013 that a monograph on Andrographis leaf "cannot be established at present", because neither the well-established-use requirement (recognised efficacy, acceptable safety, and the period having elapsed) nor the traditional-use requirement (sufficient period of use in the EU) is met. The committee added that it "will welcome the provision of the necessary data" — the door is not closed, it is waiting for data.

Turkey

There is no monograph for Andrographis on the Turkish list. It does not grow in Turkey; it is sold as imported capsules for colds, often in products combined with Siberian ginseng.

India and southern China — king of bitters

Native to India and Sri Lanka; a fever and liver plant in Ayurveda, a "heat-clearing" bitter herb in Chinese medicine. Sold in Scandinavia as a cold remedy since the 1990s — and that recent date is exactly why the 30-year EU tradition requirement could not be met.

In nature

Where it grows
Native to India and Sri Lanka; cultivated in South and Southeast Asia. It does not grow in Turkey.
Harvest season
Annual; small white-purple flowers in late summer. The leaves are gathered at the start of flowering.
Local names
No Turkish name; Hindi kalmegh, Chinese chuān xīn lián.

Mix-up risk

The andrographolide content of products sold as Andrographis varies from product to product; most trials in the meta-analysis gave no manufacturing details. Products combined with Siberian ginseng do not count as single-ingredient.

Preparation

This record gives no dose: there is neither an EU nor a Turkish monograph. The products used in clinical trials are mostly commercial extracts standardised for andrographolide; the plant itself is intensely bitter and is not commonly drunk as a tea.

What science says

Andrographis is a plant in this catalog to which the regulator said "no" while the literature is anything but empty. A systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2017 in PLoS One pooled 33 randomised trials (7,175 patients) in acute respiratory tract infections: against placebo Andrographis improved cough (SMD −0.39; 95% CI −0.67 to −0.10) and sore throat (SMD −1.13; −1.37 to −0.89), shortened time to symptom resolution, and adverse events were rare and mild. But the authors stress three things: the methodological quality of the trials was overall poor, heterogeneity high, and most products were commercial mixtures giving no manufacturing or quality-control details (some combined with Siberian ginseng). The European Medicines Agency’s "we await data" and the meta-analysis’s "promising but poor quality" are two phrasings of the same thing. Evidence level Limited.

Drug interactions

Medicine or substanceWhy it matters
No official interaction listWith no EU or Turkish monograph there are no reported interactions. Theoretical notes: immunosuppressants (the plant is credited with immune effects), anticoagulants and antidiabetics (laboratory findings). None shown in a clinical study; telling the doctor is enough for anyone on regular medication.
Products combined with Siberian ginsengA large share of the European clinical trials used an Andrographis + Eleutherococcus mixture; those trials measure the mixture. See the Siberian ginseng record in this catalog.

This list is not complete. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist or doctor before trying Andrographis.
